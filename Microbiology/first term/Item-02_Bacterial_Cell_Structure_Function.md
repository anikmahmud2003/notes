Peptidoglycan:
e Synonyms: murein or mucopeptide.
e Peptidoglycan: peptides (protein) + glycan (sugar or Carbohydrate)-
a. Backbone: glycan/ sugar. Composed of alternating two sugars: N-acetylmuramic acid
(NAM or NAMA) and N-acetylglucosamine molecules (NAG).
b. Side chain: Tetrapeptide side chain, attached to NAM.
c. Cross-bridge or inter-bridge: pentaglycine cross bridge for gram-positive bacteria
and direct cross bridge for gram-negative bacteria.

The backbone is the same in all bacterial species; the tetrapeptide side chains and the peptide
cross-bridges vary from species to species.

Backbone: Neacetylmirainie acid (NAM or NAMA) and N-acetylglucosamine molecules
(NAG) linked together by of 1, 4-B linkage or glycosyl bond.
Side chain: Tetrapeptide side chain, attached to NAM.
Tetrapeptide side chain:

1. L-alanine

2: ‘D-glutamine

3. L-lysine in case of gram-positive bacteria, Diaminopimelic acid in case of gram-
negative bacteria.

4. D-alanine
Cross-bridge or inter-bridge:
Transpeptidase enzymes make the cross-links between adjacent tetrapeptides.
In case of gram-positive bacteria: L-lysine of one tetrapeptide chain is linked by the covalent
linkage to the terminal D-alanine of another chain by pentaglycine cross bridge.
In case of gram-negative bacteria: Diaminopimelic acid of one tetrapeptide chain is linked by
the covalent linkage to the terminal D-alanine of another chain by direct linkage (no
pentaglycine).

Flagella and Pili/ Fimbriae:
Flagella: Flagella are protein (flagellin :protein), which are whip or thread like structures
responsible for motility of bacteria».
Only certain bacteria have flagella. Many rods are flagellated, but cocci are non-flagellated.
Capsulated bacteria are usually non-flagellated.

Parts of flagella:

a. Filament: formed by flagellin protein

b. Hook: short.curved structure

c. Basal body: consists of a set of rings
One pair,of rings in Gram positive bacteria
Two pair of rings in gram negative bacteria.

Bacterial classification according to flagellar arrangement:
There are four types of flagellar arrangement.
1. Monotrichous: Single polar flagellum e.g. Vibrio cholerae and Campylobacter jejuni.
2. Amphitrichous: Single flagellum at both ends e.g. Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
3. Lophotrichous: Tuft of flagella at one or both ends e.g. Helicobacter pylori.
4.

Peritrichous: Flagella surrounding the bacterial cell. Some enterobacteriaceae, e.g., Salmonella
typhi, Escherichia coli, Proteus.

Plasmid & Transposon:

Plasmid: Another name: episome.
Plasmids are extrachromosomal, double-stranded, circular DNA molecules that are capable of
replicating independently of bacterial chromosome.
Although plasmids are usually extrachromosomal, they can be integrated into the bacterial
chromosome.
Types of plasmids:
a. Transmissible: large (MW 40-100 million), few copies (1-3copies) per cell and contain
the transfer gene (which synthesize sex pili.)
b. Non-transmissible: small (MW 3-20 million), many copies (10-60 copies) per cells and
they do not contain the transfer gene.
Functions of plasmids:
Plasmids carry the genes, which encode-
a. Enzymes such as B lactamase, which is responsible for drug resistance
b. Reductase enzyme, which is responsible for resistance to heavy metals, such as
mercury (component of antisepsis), silver etc.
DNA repair enzymes, which is responsible for resistance to UV light.

c.

d. Exotoxins

e. Sex pili

f. Bacteriocins (colicins of E. coli and pyocins of P. aeruginosa), they are toxin products
of certain bacteria and used to infections caused by antibiotic-resistant bacteria.

g. Several antibiotics produced by Streptomyces.

h. Degradative enzymes of Pseudomonas, which are capable of cleaning up

environmental hazards. :

Transposons: Another name: jumping genes.
Transposons are short segments of DNA that move readily from one site to another either within

or between the DNAs of bacteria, plasmids and bacteriophages.
They cannot capable of replicating independently, they replicate as part of the DNA in which they

are integrated.
Because of their unusual,ability to move, they are called ‘jumping genes’.

Types:

™ Replicative transposition: they are move by replicating their DNA and inserting the new
copy:into another site. (Copy and paste )

b. Direct transposition: they are excised from the site without replicating and then inserted
into the new site. (cut and paste)

Functions of transposons:
1. They carry genes that can code enzymes, which are responsible for drug resistance.

2. They carry genes that can code toxins and variety of metabolic enzymes.
3. Can cause mutations in the gene.

Spore:

Two types.

1. Exospore:
a. Formed outside the parent vegetative cell.
b. It is for reproduction.
c. Example: fungal spore (and sometimes Coxiella burnetti)

2. Endospore:
a. Formed inside the parent vegetative cell.
b. It is for survival, not for reproduction. _
c. Example: bacterial spore (and sometimes Coccidioides immitis & rhinosporidium

seeberi).

Bacterial Spore/ Endospore:

Definition: Spores or Endospores or Bacterial spores are highly resistant, dormant structures
formed in response to adverse (unfavorable) environmental conditions.

Structures of spore:

From inside to outside-

Spore core: rich in calcium dipicolinate (dipicolinic acid). Contain chromosome.

Spore wall (germ cell wall): contains normal peptidoglycan.

Spore cortex: contains abnormal peptidoglycan.

Spore coat: toughest or thickest layer. It contains keratin like protein.
