Virology: Introduction

Virus is a smallest particle composed of an internal core containing either DNA or RNA (but
not both) covered by a protective coat called Capsid. Some viruses have an outer lipoprotein
membrane, called an envelope, external to the coat.

Virion:

Virion is the complete virus particle that is structurally intact and infectious.

Properties of virus:

].

Cellularity:
They are acellular. They are obligate intracellular parasites, because they can reproduce

only within cells.

NAs: They possess either DNA or RNA but never both.

Energy generation: They cannot generate energy (mitochondria absent).

(Depend upon synthetic machinery of host cells.)

Protein synthesis: They cannot synthesize proteins (ribosome absent).

(Lack of enzymes necessary for protein and nucleic acid synthesis and so depend upon

synthetic machinery of host cells.)
Microscopy: They are smaller and cannot visualize by light microscope. They can be seen

by electron microscope.
Culture: They cannot be grown on routine culture media (However, they can grow in cell
containing experimental animals, embryonated eggs, cells or tissue culture media).
Methods of multiplication: They multiply by replication, but not by binary fission or
mitosis.

(One virus: by replication, can produce hundreds of progeny virus.

One bacteria: by binary fission, can produce two daughter cells).
Drug susceptibility: They are susceptible to antiviral drugs such as interferon but not to
antibiotics.

Th-17 cells and HIV:
HIV infection also targets a subset of CD4-positive cells called Th17 cells. Th17 cells give

mucosal immunity, especially in the gastrointestinal tract. Th17 cells produce interleukin-
17 (IL-17), which attracts neutrophils to the site of bacterial infection. The loss of Th17
cells predisposes HIV-infected individuals to bloodstream infections by colonic normal flora.

Elite controllers:
‘Elite controllers’ are a rare group (<!%) of HIV-infected people who have no detectable HIV

in their blood. Their CD4 counts are normal without using antiretroviral drugs. Although
the mechanism is unclear, there is evidence that certain HLA alleles are protective and that an
inhibitor of the cyclin-dependent kinase known as p21 plays an important role.

Mutations in the nef gene

There is a group of HIV-infected individuals who have lived for many years without
opportunistic infections and without a reduction in the number of their helper T (CD4)
cells. The strain of HIV isolated from these individuals has mutations in the nef gene.

Long-term “nonprogressors”
Long-term “nonprogressors” produce large amounts of a-defensins. o-defensins are a family

of positively charged peptides with antibacterial activity that also have antiviral activity.
They interfere with HIV binding to the CXCR¢4 receptor and block entry of the virus into the

cell.

‘Escape mutants’
In ‘escape mutants’ individual, mutations in any of the genes encoding class I MHC proteins

result in a more rapid progression to clinical AIDS. The mutant class I MHC proteins cannot
present HIV epitopes, which results in cytotoxic T cells being incapable of recognizing and
destroying HIV infected cells.
