## Section 1 — Non-Communicable Disease (NCD) — General

<details>
<summary><b>Q1. Define NCD. Cardinal features.</b></summary>

### Non-Communicable Disease (NCD)
> 🔴 **NCDs** are a group of **chronic diseases** where the causative agents are not clearly identified, but a set of **risk factors** have been identified to be associated. They are **non-infectious** and **non-transmissible** from person to person.

### Cardinal Characteristics (Commission on Chronic Illness, USA)
1. They are **permanent**.
2. They leave **residual disability**.
3. They are caused by **non-reversible pathological alteration**.
4. They require **special training of the patient for rehabilitation**.
5. They may be expected to require a **long period of supervision, observation or care**.

### Key Epidemiological Features of Chronic Diseases
1. **Absence of a known agent** — for many NCDs the causative agent is unknown.
2. **Multifactorial causation** — cumulative effects of multiple risk factors.
3. **Long latent period** — long gap between first exposure and disease manifestation.
4. **Indefinite onset** — slow onset; distinction between diseased and non-diseased is difficult.

> 🎯 NCD = chronic, non-infectious, irreversible, disabling, requires long-term care.

> 📖 Ch-06, Endeavour PDF pp. 251–253

<details>
<summary><b>Q2. Difference between NCD and CD.</b></summary>

### NCD vs CD — Comparison

| Feature | NCD (Non-Communicable) | CD (Communicable) |
|---------|------------------------|-------------------|
| **Cause** | Multifactorial; no single agent | Specific infectious agent |
| **Transmission** | Not transmitted person-to-person | Transmitted via air, water, food, vectors |
| **Onset** | Slow, insidious | Usually acute |
| **Duration** | Chronic, long-lasting | Often short, self-limiting |
| **Course** | Irreversible pathology | Often curable/recoverable |
| **Disability** | Residual disability common | Disability less common |
| **Examples** | CVD, DM, Cancer, HTN | TB, Cholera, Measles, Malaria |
| **Prevention** | Lifestyle modification, screening | Vaccination, sanitation, vector control |
| **Treatment** | Long-term management | Curative (antibiotics, antivirals) |

> 🎯 NCD = no agent, not transmissible, chronic, irreversible. CD = specific agent, transmissible, often acute, often curable.

> 📖 Ch-06, Endeavour PDF pp. 251–253

<summary><b>Q3. Common NCD in BD. Justify "NCD is increasing in BD and worldwide".</b></summary>

### Common NCDs in Bangladesh
1. **Cardiovascular diseases** — CHD/IHD, Hypertension, RHD
2. **Cerebrovascular disease** — TIA and Stroke
3. **Diabetes Mellitus**
4. **Cancer**
5. **Mental illness**, **Blindness**, **Accidents**

### Why NCDs Are Increasing
Countries are passing through **demographic and epidemiological transition** — improvement of health status, economic development, better health coverage, and education have caused a demographic shift with an **increasing proportion of adults and elderly persons**.

**Main Factors:**
1. **Rapid change of food habit, lifestyle, and behavioural pattern** — more fat, sugar, salt; less physical activity; smoking; alcohol.
2. **Increasing proportion of adults and elderly persons** — NCDs are diseases of aging.
3. **Urbanization and industrialization** — sedentary occupations, stress, pollution.
4. **Failure to obtain preventive services**.

> 🎯 NCD increasing = demographic & epidemiological transition + lifestyle change + aging population + urbanization.

<details>
<summary><b>Q4. What do you mean by epidemiological transition?</b></summary>

### Epidemiological Transition
> 🔴 **Epidemiological transition** = the shift in disease patterns from predominantly **communicable/infectious diseases** to predominantly **non-communicable/degenerative diseases**. It accompanies demographic transition — as death rates fall and life expectancy rises, chronic diseases replace infections as the leading causes of morbidity and mortality.

| Stage | Leading Causes of Death |
|-------|------------------------|
| **1. Age of Pestilence and Famine** | Infectious diseases, malnutrition |
| **2. Age of Receding Pandemics** | Infectious diseases declining; CVD emerging |
| **3. Age of Degenerative and Man-made Diseases** | CVD, cancer, DM dominant |
| **4. Age of Delayed Degenerative Diseases** | Degenerative diseases but mortality delayed |

> 🎯 Epidemiological transition = shift from infectious → chronic disease pattern as a country develops.

> 📖 Ch-06; Park's PSM (Omran, 1971)

### Preventive Measures of NCD

| Level | Definition | Examples for NCD |
|-------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Premordial** | Prevention of **emergence of risk factors** themselves | Preventing smoking, unhealthy diet, sedentary lifestyle in children |
| **Primary** | Prevention of **disease onset** in those with risk factors | HTN control, smoking cessation, weight reduction, exercise |
| **Secondary** | **Early detection and treatment** before disease advances | Screening (BP, blood sugar, Pap smear, mammography) |
| **Tertiary** | **Rehabilitation and disability limitation** | Diabetic retinopathy management, post-stroke rehab, cancer treatment |

### Detailed NCD Prevention (WHO)
1. Screening and health examination techniques
2. Improved methods of diagnosis
3. Treatment and rehabilitation
4. Control of food, water, and air pollution
5. Reducing accidents
6. Influencing human behaviour and lifestyle
7. Upgrading institutional care
8. Comprehensive medical care including PHC
9. Political approaches (smoking control, legislation)
10. Integrated programme for prevention and control of NCDs

> 🎯 Premordial = prevent risk factor emergence · Primary = prevent disease · Secondary = early detection · Tertiary = rehabilitation.

## Section 2 — Cardiovascular Disease (CVD)

<details>
<summary><b>Q6. Risk factors and preventive measures of CVD. Coronary factors/diseases.</b></summary>

### Principal Cardiovascular Diseases
1. CHD / Ischaemic heart disease (IHD)
2. Hypertension
3. Rheumatic heart disease (RHD)
4. Congenital heart diseases

### Risk Factors of CVD

**A) Non-modifiable:** Age (↑ risk with age), Sex (males > females), Family history, Genetic factors

**B) Modifiable:** Smoking, Hypertension, Hyperlipidaemia (↑ LDL), Obesity, Sedentary habits, Stress, DM, Alcohol

### CHD — Definition
> 🔴 **CHD** = "Impairment of heart function due to **inadequate blood flow to the heart** compared to its needs, caused by obstructive changes in the coronary circulation." Commonest cause of cardiovascular disability & death.

### Pathophysiology
Abnormal lipid metabolism → excessive cholesterol/fat → atherosclerosis (fatty streaks → subendothelial lipid → smooth muscle migration → thrombus → partial/complete occlusion)

### Clinical Manifestations
1. Angina pectoris · 2. Myocardial infarction (MI) · 3. Arrhythmias · 4. Cardiac failure · 5. Sudden death

### Prevention of CVD

**Primary — Population Strategy:**
- Dietary changes: reduce fat, saturated fat, cholesterol (<100 mg/1000 kcal/day), increase complex carbs, avoid alcohol
- Prevention of smoking: health education, fiscal measures, legislation
- BP maintenance within normal values
- Regular physical activity, weight control

**Primary — High-Risk Strategy:** Identify risk by serum cholesterol, treat HTN, avoid smoking

**Primordial Prevention:** Prevent emergence of risk factors in children

**Secondary:** Smoking cessation, HTN/DM control, healthy nutrition, exercise, drug therapy (beta-blockers, anticoagulants, lipid-lowering agents, aspirin)

> 🎯 CVD risk = modifiable (smoking, HTN, cholesterol, obesity, DM) + non-modifiable (age, sex, genetics). Prevention = lifestyle + screening + drugs.

> 📖 Ch-06, Endeavour PDF pp. 257–262

## Section 3 — Hypertension (HTN)

<details>
<summary><b>Q7. Define HTN. Risk factors — explained.</b></summary>

### Definition of Hypertension
> 🔴 **Hypertension** = persistently raised systolic BP of **≥140 mmHg** AND diastolic BP of **≥90 mmHg** in subjects not taking antihypertensive medication.

### Classification
- **Primary / Essential HTN** (>90%): No specific cause known
- **Secondary HTN** (<10%): Renal diseases, endocrine (phaeochromocytoma, Cushing's, Conn's), coarctation of aorta, pregnancy, drugs (OCP, steroids)

### Stages
| Stage | Systolic (mmHg) | Diastolic (mmHg) |
|-------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Normal | <130 | <85 |
| High normal | 130–139 | 85–89 |
| Stage 1 (Mild) | 140–159 | — |
| Stage 2 (Moderate) | 160–179 | — |
| Stage 3 (Severe) | ≥180 | — |

### Risk Factors of HTN

**Non-modifiable:**
| Factor | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| Age | BP rises with age |
| Genetics | Monozygotic twins > dizygotic |
| Family history | 2 hypertensive parents → **45% risk**; normotensive parents → 3% |

**Modifiable:**
| Factor | Detail |
|--------|--------|
| Obesity | Directly proportional to BP |
| Salt intake | High salt → water retention → ↑BP |
| Saturated fat | Promotes atherosclerosis |
| Alcohol | Heavy intake raises BP |
| Physical inactivity | Sedentary lifestyle |
| Stress | Environmental stress |
| Others | OCP, noise, vibration, temperature |

> 🎯 HTN = SBP ≥140 and/or DBP ≥90. 90% essential, 10% secondary. Risk = age + genetics + obesity + salt + sedentary + stress.

> 📖 Ch-06, Endeavour PDF pp. 262–265

<details>
<summary><b>Q9. Rule of half regarding HTN. Ice-berg phenomenon.</b></summary>

### Rule of Halves (HTN)
> 🔴 HTN is an **"iceberg disease"** — only a fraction of cases are detected and treated.

| Step | Statement |
|------|-----------|
| 1 | Only about **half** of hypertensive population are **aware** |
| 2 | Only about **half** of those aware are being **treated** |
| 3 | Only about **half** of those treated are **adequately treated** |

→ 100% hypertensive → 50% aware → 25% treated → **12.5% adequately treated**

### Iceberg Phenomenon
Like an iceberg, the **visible portion** (diagnosed and treated) is small compared to the **hidden mass** (undiagnosed and untreated). The iceberg figure shows: whole community → normotensive → hypertensive → undiagnosed → diagnosed but untreated → diagnosed and treated → inadequately treated → adequately treated.

> 🎯 Rule of halves: half aware, half treated, half adequately treated → only 12.5% well controlled.

> 📖 Ch-06, Endeavour PDF pp. 265–266

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q10. Complications of HTN. Preventive measures.</b></summary>

### Complications of Untreated HTN
| System | Complication |
|--------|-------------|
| **Cardiovascular** | LVH, CHD, CCF, arrhythmias, myocardial ischaemia, **sudden death** |
| **Cerebrovascular** | Intracranial hemorrhage, cerebral infarction (stroke), encephalopathy |
| **Renal** | Renal failure, nephrosclerosis |
| **Peripheral vascular** | PVD, atherosclerotic complications |
| **Aortic** | Aortic dissection |
| **Emergencies** | Encephalopathy, nephropathy, retinopathy, MI, heart failure, pulmonary edema, eclampsia |
| **Eyes** | Hypertensive retinopathy → blindness |

Called the **"Silent Killer"** — often asymptomatic until complications develop. Damages: **Heart, Brain, Kidneys, Eyes, Blood vessels**.

### Prevention of HTN

**Primary — Population Strategy:**
- Salt reduction (<5 gm/day), moderate fat, avoid alcohol, restrict energy
- Weight reduction, exercise (30 min/day), behavioral changes, health education

**Primary — High-Risk Strategy:** Screening of high-risk groups, individual counseling

**Secondary:** Early case detection, treatment (lifestyle + drugs), patient compliance

> 🎯 HTN complications = heart + brain + kidneys + eyes + vessels. Prevention = salt <5g/day + exercise + weight control + screening + compliance.

> 📖 Ch-06, Endeavour PDF pp. 266–268

## Section 4 — Rheumatic Fever / RHD

<details>
<summary><b>Q11. Define RF. Epidemiological features. Consequences of RHD.</b></summary>

### Rheumatic Fever (RF)
> 🔴 **RF** = a **febrile disease affecting connective tissues**, particularly heart and joints, initiated by infection of the throat by **Group A beta-haemolytic streptococci (GABHS)**.

### Epidemiological Features
| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Agent | Group A β-haemolytic Streptococcus |
| Age | **5–15 years** (childhood and adolescence) |
| Sex | Both equally |
| SES | **Social disease** — poverty, overcrowding, poor housing |
| High-risk | School-age children, slum dwellers, closed communities |

### Consequences of RHD
1. Continuing damage to the heart
2. Increasing disabilities
3. Repeated hospitalization
4. Premature death (usually by age 35 or earlier)

### Prevention
**Primary:** Prophylactic antibiotic in sore throat — Phenoxymethyl penicillin 250 mg 6-hourly for 10 days

**Secondary (prevent recurrence):** Benzathine penicillin 1.2 million units IM monthly
- Duration: ≥5 years after last attack or up to age 18 (whichever longer)
- If carditis: ≥10 years or up to age 25
- Severe valvular disease: **lifelong**

> 🎯 RF = strep throat → autoimmune heart/joint damage. 5–15 years, poverty. RHD → heart damage + disability + premature death. Prevention = penicillin prophylaxis.

> 📖 Ch-06, Endeavour PDF pp. 268–271

## Section 6 — Diabetes Mellitus (DM)

<details>
<summary><b>Q17. What is DM? Difference between type-1 and type-2.</b></summary>

### Diabetes Mellitus
> 🔴 **DM** = a condition of **absolute or relative deficiency of insulin**, resulting in hyperglycaemia.

### Type 1 vs Type 2 DM
| Feature | Type 1 (IDDM) | Type 2 (NIDDM) |
|---------|---------------|-----------------|
| Onset | Abrupt | Gradual (insidious) |
| Age | Usually <30 yrs | Usually >40 yrs |
| Body | Lean | Obese |
| Insulin | Very low/absent | Normal or high (resistant) |
| Ketoacidosis | Common | Rare |
| % of DM | 5–10% | 90–95% |
| Treatment | Insulin essential | Diet + oral agents ± insulin |
| Autoantibodies | Present | Absent |

### Classical Symptoms
**Polyuria** + **Polydipsia** + **Polyphagia** + weight loss + weakness

> 🎯 Type 1 = young, lean, abrupt, autoimmune, insulin-dependent. Type 2 = old, obese, insidious, resistant, 90%.

> 📖 Ch-06, Endeavour PDF pp. 278–282

</details>

<details>
<summary><b>Q18. Complications of DM. GDM — definition and importance.</b></summary>

### Complications of DM

**Acute:** Diabetic ketoacidosis, hypoglycaemic coma, non-ketotic hyperosmolar coma, lactic acidosis

**Chronic — Microvascular:**
| Complication | Consequence |
|-------------|-------------|
| Retinopathy, cataract | Blindness |
| Nephropathy | Renal failure |
| Peripheral neuropathy | Sensory/motor loss |
| Autonomic neuropathy | Postural hypotension, gastroparesis |
| Foot disease | Ulceration, amputation |

**Chronic — Macrovascular:** Coronary artery disease (MI), cerebrovascular disease (stroke), PVD (gangrene)

### GDM (Gestational Diabetes Mellitus)
> 🔴 **GDM** = glucose intolerance first recognized **during pregnancy** (usually 2nd/3rd trimester). Usually resolves after delivery but ↑risk of Type 2 DM later.

**Why important:**
- **To fetus:** Macrosomia, birth trauma, neonatal hypoglycaemia, respiratory distress, stillbirth
- **To mother:** Pre-eclampsia, caesarean delivery, ↑risk of future Type 2 DM

> 🎯 Acute = ketoacidosis + hypo coma. Micro = eyes + kidneys + nerves + feet. Macro = heart + brain + vessels. GDM = diabetes in pregnancy → macrosomia + neonatal hypoglycaemia.

> 📖 Ch-06, Endeavour PDF pp. 283–285

<details>
<summary><b>Q19. Control of DM. "DM cannot be fully cured, but controlled" — Explain.</b></summary>

### Control of DM
1. **Diet:** Carb 45–60%, fat <35%, protein 10–15%, fruit/veg 5 portions/day, salt <6g
2. **Exercise:** 30 min/day (walking, swimming, cycling)
3. **Monitoring:** Home blood glucose, HbA1c (3-monthly), BP, weight, visual acuity
4. **Drugs:** Type 1 → insulin (lifelong); Type 2 → diet + oral agents ± insulin
5. **Education:** Self-care, foot care, hypoglycaemia recognition

### "Cannot Be Fully Cured, But Controlled"
> 🔴 DM is a **chronic metabolic disorder** with **no cure**. However, it can be effectively controlled:
> - **Type 1:** Autoimmune β-cell destruction is irreversible → lifelong insulin
> - **Type 2:** Insulin resistance is progressive → lifestyle + medications
>
> **Goal:** HbA1c <7%, fasting glucose 80–130, postprandial <180, BP <130/80, LDL <100
>
> Good control → prevents/delays complications → near-normal life expectancy

> 🎯 DM = no cure, but diet + exercise + drugs + monitoring → good control → prevents complications.

> 📖 Ch-06, Endeavour PDF pp. 285–287

<summary><b>Q21. Define demography. Demographic processes. Vital events of life.</b></summary>

### Demography
> 🔴 **Demography** = the scientific study of human population — **size, composition, and distribution**.

### Five Demographic Processes
| # | Process | Effect |
|---|---------|--------|
| 1 | **Fertility** | ↑ fertility → ↑ population |
| 2 | **Mortality** | ↑ mortality → ↓ population |
| 3 | **Marriage** | Early → ↑ population; Late → ↓ |
| 4 | **Migration** | Immigration → ↑; Emigration → ↓ |
| 5 | **Social Mobility** | Changes in social status |

📌 **NOT** demographic processes: Morbidity, Fecundity, Census

### Vital Events of Life
> 🔴 Events concerning the **beginning and end of life**: **Fertility, Mortality, Morbidity, Marriage, Divorce**

> 🎯 Demography = study of population. 5 processes = fertility + mortality + marriage + migration + social mobility. Vital events = birth · death · marriage · divorce · sickness.

> 📖 Ch-09, Endeavour PDF pp. 317–318

<details>
<summary><b>Q24. Population growth rate, doubling time. Census — define and classify.</b></summary>

### Population Growth Rate
```
Growth Rate (%) = (CBR − CDR) / 10
```
Example: BD 2001: (18.9 − 4.8) / 10 = **1.41%**

### Doubling Time
```
Doubling Time = 70 / Growth Rate (%)
```
| Growth Rate | Doubling Time |
|-------------|---------------|
| 2.0% | 35 years |
| 1.5% | 47 years |
| 1.41% (BD) | ~50 years |

### Census
> 🔴 **Census** (UN) = "collecting, compiling and publishing demographic, economic and social data pertaining to **all persons** in a country at a specified time."

**By counting:** De-facto (where you are) vs De-jure (where you live)
**By method:** Enumeration (enumerator visits) vs Questionnaire (head of household fills) vs Combination

**BD Census:** 10-year intervals: 1974, 1981, 1991, 2001, 2011. First subcontinent census: 1881. First BD census: 1974.

> 🎯 Growth rate = (CBR−CDR)/10. Doubling = 70/growth rate. Census = all-person data collection. De-facto = where you are; De-jure = where you live.

<details>
<summary><b>Q25. Fertility, NRR, GFR, TFR — Definition.</b></summary>

### Fertility
> 🔴 **Fertility** = child-bearing performance of individuals, couples, or populations.

### General Fertility Rate (GFR)
> 🔴 Live births per **1,000 women in reproductive age** (15–44/49) per year.
```
GFR = (Live births / Mid-year female pop. 15–44/49) × 1,000
```

### Total Fertility Rate (TFR)
> 🔴 Average number of children a woman would have if she bore children at current age-specific rates through her reproductive years. = **Completed family size**. BD TFR = 2.45.

### Net Reproduction Rate (NRR)
> 🔴 Number of **daughters** a newborn girl will bear in her lifetime at current fertility and mortality rates.
- **NRR = 1** = replacement level = **2-child norm**
- NRR < 1 = below replacement
- NRR = 1 achievable if **CPR ≥60%**

> 🎯 GFR = births/1000 women. TFR = completed family size. NRR = daughters per girl → NRR 1 = 2-child norm = CPR 60%.

### Post-Natal Complications
Puerperal sepsis (infection within 3 weeks; ↑temp, foul lochia), thrombophlebitis, secondary haemorrhage, UTI, mastitis

## Section 15 — Environment, Water, Air, Noise, Radiation, Global Warming, Ventilation

<details>
<summary><b>Q47. Define and classify environment. Safe water criteria. Water-borne and water-related diseases.</b></summary>

### Environment
> 🔴 All external factors — living and non-living, material and non-material — which surround man.

**Classification:** Internal (tissues, organs) vs External/Macro (air, water, food, housing)

**Components:** Physical (water, air, soil) + Biological (plants, animals, microorganisms) + Social (customs, culture, income, occupation)

### Safe and Wholesome Water
> 🔴 Water that is: 1. Free from pathogenic agents 2. Free from harmful chemicals 3. Pleasant to taste (colourless, odourless) 4. Usable for domestic purposes

**Water requirement:** Basic: ~2 L/head/day. Adequate: 150–200 L/head/day

### Water-Borne Diseases
**Viral:** Hepatitis A & E, polio, rotavirus
**Bacterial:** Typhoid, dysentery, cholera, E. coli diarrhoea
**Protozoal:** Amoebiasis, giardiasis
**Helminthic:** Ascariasis, thread worm

### Water-Related Diseases
Schistosomiasis (snail), Guinea worm (Cyclops), Weil's disease (leptospira)

> 🎯 Safe water = pathogen-free + no harmful chemicals + pleasant + usable. Water-borne = cholera + typhoid + hepatitis A. Water-related = schistosomiasis + Guinea worm.

<summary><b>Q48. Water purification — large scale and small scale. Chlorination — S/N.</b></summary>

### Large Scale Purification
1. **Storage:** 90% impurities settle in 24 hrs; bacteria drop tremendously
2. **Filtration:**
   - **Slow sand filter:** Sand bed 60cm, infiltration rate 0.1–0.4 m³/hr/m². **Schmutzdecke** (biological layer) = "heart of slow sand filter." Removes organic matter, bacteria.
   - **Rapid sand filter:** Coagulation → rapid mixing → flocculation → sedimentation → filtration. Removes 99.9–99.99% bacteria.
3. **Disinfection:** Chlorination, ozonation, UV irradiation

### Small Scale Purification
- **Boiling:** Rolling boil 5–10 min — kills all bacteria, spores, cysts
- **Chemical:** Bleaching powder (30g/100 gal), KMnO₄ (0.5 ppm), alum (2–5g/gal), chlorine tablet (0.5g/20L), iodine (2 ppm)
- **Filtration:** Pasteur/Chamberland/Berkefeld filters

### Chlorination
> 🔴 Cl₂ + H₂O → HCl + **HOCl** (hypochlorous acid — main disinfectant)

**Principles:**
1. Water must be clear (free from turbidity)
2. Chlorine demand must be estimated
3. Free residual chlorine for ≥1 hour contact
4. Minimum 0.5 mg/L for 1 hour
5. Dose = chlorine demand + 0.5 mg/L

**Types:** Super chlorination (large dose → dechlorination for heavily polluted water) + Break point chlorination (point where residual chlorine appears after all combined chlorines destroyed)

> 🎯 Large scale = storage + slow/rapid sand filtration + disinfection. Small scale = boiling + chemicals + filters. Chlorination = HOCl is active agent. Dose = demand + 0.5 mg/L.

> 📖 Ch-15, Endeavour PDF pp. 493–498

<summary><b>Q49. WHO criteria of drinking water. Health effects of air pollution. Prevention.</b></summary>

### WHO Drinking Water Standards

**Physical:** Colourless, odourless, no turbidity, pleasant taste

**Chemical:** Chloride <200 mg/L, pH 5–9, Iron <0.1 mg/L, Hardness 100–200 mg/L, Sulphate <270 mg/L, Zinc <4 mg/L

**Bacteriological:** **E. coli** not detectable in any 100 mL sample; no sample >3 coliforms/100mL; ≤5% samples coliform-positive/year

**Why coliforms as indicators:** Abundant in intestine, easily detected (1 bacterium/100mL), survive longer than pathogens, greater resistance to purification

### Health Effects of Air Pollution
**Immediate:** Acute bronchitis, respiratory symptoms, immediate death by suffocation
**Delayed:** Chronic bronchitis, primary lung cancer, bronchial asthma, emphysema, respiratory allergies
**Other:** Lead → children's intelligence impairment; plant/animal damage; economic loss

### Prevention of Air Pollution (WHO)
1. **Containment** — enclosure, ventilation, air cleaning
2. **Replacement** — non-polluting processes
3. **Dilution** — within self-cleaning capacity; vegetation helps
4. **Legislation** — air pollution control through law
5. **International action** — WHO monitoring networks

> 🎯 WHO water = E. coli absent in 100mL + chemical standards. Air pollution effects = respiratory + cancer + asthma. Prevention = containment + replacement + dilution + legislation.

> 📖 Ch-15, Endeavour PDF pp. 498–504

### Noise Level Chart
| Source | Decibels (dB) |
|--------|---------------|
| Whispering | 20–30 |
| Quiet library | 40 |
| Normal conversation | 60–65 |
| Heavy street traffic | 60–80 |
| Printing press | 80 |
| **Recommended maximum** | **85** |
| Train passing | 100–110 |
| Motor car horn | 120 |
| **Threshold of pain** | **130–140** |
| Mechanical damage | 150–160 |

### Health Effects of Noise

**Auditory:** Auditory fatigue (at 90 dB, greatest at 4000–6000 Hz); Deafness (85 dB daily = tolerance limit; >160 dB single exposure → tympanic membrane rupture)

**Non-auditory:** Interference with speech, annoyance, reduced efficiency, ↑BP, ↑heart rate, ↑breathing, ↑intracranial pressure, sweating, giddiness, nausea, fatigue

### Prevention of Noise Pollution
1. City planning — zone separation, wide green belts
2. Vehicle control — no heavy vehicles in narrow streets
3. Acoustic insulation of buildings
4. Industries/railways outside residential zones
5. Hearing protection for exposed workers
6. Legislation
7. Education through media

> 🎯 Recommended max = 85 dB. Effects = auditory (fatigue + deafness) + non-auditory (↑BP + ↑heart rate + annoyance). Prevention = city planning + green belts + insulation + legislation.

<summary><b>Q51. Ionizing radiation — sources, health effects. Acute radiation syndrome.</b></summary>

### Ionizing Radiation — Sources
**Natural:** Cosmic rays, terrestrial/environmental, internal (K-40, C-14)
**Man-made:** Medical/dental (X-rays), occupational, nuclear (fallout), TV, radioactive watches

### Health Effects
**Somatic — Immediate:** Radiation sickness, acute radiation syndrome
**Somatic — Delayed:** Leukaemia, carcinogenesis, fetal abnormalities, shortening of life
**Genetic:** Chromosome mutations, point mutations

### Acute Radiation Syndrome
> 🔴 Whole body exposure to **>100 rems** penetrating radiation in single exposure within 1–2 days.

**Phases:** Nausea + vomiting + diarrhoea + headache + erythema → hair loss + desquamation → **bone marrow aplasia** (pancytopenia, ↓Hb, ↓WBC, thrombocytopenia)

**Syndromes by dose:**
| Syndrome | Dose |
|----------|------|
| CNS syndrome | >10,000 rads |
| GI syndrome | (intermediate) |
| Bone marrow syndrome | ≥50 rads |

### Long-term Effects
Aplastic anaemia, chronic radiodermatitis, cataract, sterility, congenital malformation, carcinoma (skin, bone, leukaemia, thyroid, lung), genetic defects

### Prevention
Internal: Notification + dose limits + classification + monitoring + medical surveillance
External: Avoid unnecessary X-rays + shielding/distance/time + lead apron + film badge + periodic exams

> 🎯 Ionizing = penetrating tissue energy. Sources = cosmic + medical + nuclear. Acute syndrome = >100 rems → nausea → bone marrow failure. Prevention = shield + distance + time + monitoring.

> 📖 Ch-15, Endeavour PDF pp. 514–518

<summary><b>Q52. Greenhouse effect and greenhouse gases. Global warming — mechanism, health effects.</b></summary>

### Greenhouse Effect
> 🔴 Atmosphere acts like greenhouse glass — solar short-wave radiation absorbed by earth → re-radiated as long-wave infrared → atmospheric gases trap this → warming earth progressively.

### Greenhouse Gases
| Gas | Contribution |
|-----|-------------|
| **CO₂** | **49%** (principal) |
| **CH₄ (Methane)** | **18%** |
| **CFC** | **14%** |
| **N₂O** | **6%** |
| Others | 13% |

**Sources:** CO₂ from motor vehicles/industry/fossil fuels; CH₄ from paddy waste/marshland; CFC from aerosols/refrigerants; deforestation reduces CO₂ absorption

### Global Warming
> 🔴 Projected ~3°C increase in average global temperature by 2030; sea level rise 0.1–0.3m by 2050.

### Health Effects on BD
1. Sea level rise 3 feet → Barisal & Khulna may become islands
2. Salinity of major rivers
3. More violent/frequent floods, cyclones
4. Disappearance of Sundarbans, coastal greenery
5. Crops submerged, water contamination
6. Wildlife reduction, habitat loss

### Prevention
Reduce CO₂ + more trees + solar/hydraulic/atomic energy + fuel-efficient vehicles + conserve forests + organic agriculture + stop CFC use + coastal dams/walls

> 🎯 Greenhouse = atmosphere traps heat. CO₂ = 49% (main). Global warming → BD: sea rise + salinity + floods + Sundarbans loss. Prevention = reduce CO₂ + afforestation + alternative energy.

> 📖 Ch-15, Endeavour PDF pp. 518–521

<summary><b>Q53. Ventilation — definition, types. Effects of ill-ventilation.</b></summary>

### Ventilation
> 🔴 **Ventilation** = science of maintaining comfortable and healthful atmospheric conditions by replacing vitiated air with fresh outdoor air, controlling temperature, humidity, and purity.

**Standards:** Cubic space 1000–1200 cft/person; Air change 2–3/hr (home), 4–6/hr (assemblies)

### Types of Ventilation

**Natural:**
- **Wind:** Perflation (air blows through), aspiration (suction), cross-ventilation
- **Diffusion:** Through smallest openings (slow)
- **Temperature differences:** Air flows from high to low density

**Mechanical:**
- **Exhaust:** Fans extract air → vacuum → fresh air entry
- **Plenum:** Fresh air blown in → displaces vitiated air
- **Balanced:** Combination of exhaust + plenum
- **Air conditioning:** Simultaneous control of temperature, humidity, air movement, dust, bacteria, odours

### Effects of Ill-Ventilation

**Changes in room:** ↑CO₂, ↓O₂, ↑temperature, ↑humidity, ↓air movement, body odours, bacterial pollution

**Clinical effects:** Headache, drowsiness, lassitude, inability to concentrate, depression, fatigue, ↑risk of communicable diseases

> 🎯 Ventilation = replace vitiated with fresh air. Natural (wind + diffusion + temp) vs Mechanical (exhaust + plenum + AC). Ill-ventilation = headache + fatigue + drowsiness + ↑infection risk.

> 📖 Ch-15, Endeavour PDF pp. 521–524

## Section 16 — Communicable Disease

<details>
<summary><b>Q54. Incubation period. TB — DOTS. Prevention of communicable diseases.</b></summary>

### Incubation Period
> 🔴 **Incubation period** = time interval between **entry of pathogen** into the body and **appearance of first symptoms**. It varies by disease (e.g., influenza 1–3 days, rabies weeks to months, TB weeks to years).

### TB — DOTS (Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course)
> 🔴 **DOTS** = WHO strategy for TB control with 5 elements:
> 1. Government commitment
> 2. Good microscopy services
> 3. Regular supply of drugs
> 4. Direct observation of treatment
> 5. Recording system

**Phase 1 (Intensive):** 2 months — Isoniazid (H) + Rifampicin (R) + Pyrazinamide (Z) + Ethambutol (E) = **HRZE**
**Phase 2 (Continuation):** 4 months — Isoniazid + Rifampicin = **HR**
**Total duration:** 6 months (8 months for retreatment)

### Prevention of Communicable Diseases (General Principles)
1. **Source control** — isolation, treatment, carrier detection
2. **Mode of transmission control** — sanitation, hygiene, vector control, safe water
3. **Host protection** — vaccination, nutrition, immunity, health education

> 🎯 Incubation = pathogen entry → first symptoms. DOTS = 5 elements + 2HRZE/4HR (6 months). Prevention = source control + transmission control + host protection.

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 115–120; Park's PSM

<summary><b>Q55. Complications — Measles, Mumps, Enteric fever, Rubella.</b></summary>

### Complications of Measles
1. **Otitis media** (commonest complication)
2. **Pneumonia** (commonest cause of death)
3. **Encephalitis** (rare but serious)
4. **Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE)** — fatal, years later
5. **Koplik spots** (pathognomonic sign, before rash)

### Complications of Mumps
1. **Orchitis** (post-pubertal males — may cause sterility)
2. **Oophoritis** (females)
3. **Meningoencephalitis**
4. **Pancreatitis**
5. **Deafness** (unilateral, usually permanent)

### Complications of Enteric (Typhoid) Fever
1. **Intestinal perforation** (most dangerous — usually ileum)
2. **Intestinal haemorrhage**
3. **Myocarditis**
4. **Meningitis**
5. **Hepatosplenomegaly**
6. **Relapse** (if treatment incomplete)

### Complications of Rubella
1. **Congenital Rubella Syndrome (CRS)** — if infected in 1st trimester:
   - **C**ataract, **C**ardiac defects (PDA, pulmonary stenosis)
   - **D**eafness (sensorineural)
   - **M**ental retardation
2. Arthritis (adults, especially women)
3. Encephalitis (rare)
4. Thrombocytopenic purpura

> 🎯 Measles = otitis media + pneumonia + encephalitis + SSPE. Mumps = orchitis + meningitis + pancreatitis + deafness. Typhoid = perforation + haemorrhage. Rubella = CRS (cataract + cardiac + deafness) if 1st trimester.

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 115–250

<summary><b>Q56. Transmission of Hepatitis. Rabies — vaccination, C/F.</b></summary>

### Transmission of Hepatitis

| Type | Transmission |
|------|-------------|
| **Hepatitis A (HAV)** | **Fecal-oral** — contaminated water/food, poor sanitation |
| **Hepatitis B (HBV)** | **Parenteral** — blood, sexual contact, vertical (mother→child), needle-stick, contaminated instruments |
| **Hepatitis C (HCV)** | **Parenteral** — blood transfusion, IV drug use, needle-stick, rarely sexual |
| **Hepatitis D (HDV)** | Only with HBV — same routes as HBV |
| **Hepatitis E (HEV)** | **Fecal-oral** — contaminated water (similar to HAV); dangerous in pregnancy |

### Rabies — Clinical Features
> 🔴 **Hydrophobia** (fear of water) is pathognomonic.

**Stages:**
1. **Prodromal:** Fever, headache, malaise, tingling at bite site
2. **Excitative (Furious):** Hyperactivity, agitation, hydrophobia, aerophobia, hypersalivation, seizures
3. **Paralytic (Dumb):** Descending paralysis, coma, death
4. **Death:** Usually within **4–7 days** of symptom onset (nearly 100% fatal)

### Rabies — Post-Exposure Vaccination (WHO)
| Wound Type | Treatment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Category I** (touching/feeding) | Wash wound; no vaccine needed |
| **Category II** (minor scratches) | Wash + **Rabies vaccine** (days 0, 3, 7, 14, 28) |
| **Category III** (deep bites, mucous membrane) | Wash + **Rabies vaccine + Rabies immunoglobulin (RIG)** |

**ARV (Anti-Rabies Vaccine):** Human diploid cell vaccine (HDCV) — 5 doses on days 0, 3, 7, 14, 28 (IM, deltoid)

> 🎯 Hep A/E = fecal-oral. Hep B/C/D = parenteral. Rabies = hydrophobia + agitation + paralysis → 100% fatal. Post-exposure: wash + vaccine (±RIG for Category III).

<summary><b>Q57. Types of rash in different fevers. Dengue, Chikungunya — prevention.</b></summary>

### Types of Rash in Different Fevers

| Disease | Rash Type | Distribution | Other Features |
|---------|-----------|-------------|----------------|
| **Measles** | Maculopapular, erythematous | Face → trunk → extremities (cephalocaudal); **Koplik spots** on buccal mucosa | High fever, coryza, cough |
| **Rubella** | Maculopapular, pink, finer than measles | Face → trunk (spreads faster); **post-auricular/post-occipital lymphadenopathy** | Mild fever, joint pain |
| **Scarlet fever** | Diffuse erythema with **sandpaper texture** | Face → trunk; **Pastia's lines** in folds; **strawberry tongue** | Sore throat, fever |
| **Typhoid** | **Rose spots** — faint, macular, blanching | Abdomen and chest | Stepladder fever, relative bradycardia |
| **Chickenpox** | **Vesicular** (papule → vesicle → crust) | **Crops** in different stages; centripetal (trunk > face) | Itching, fever |
| **Meningococcal** | **Petechial/purpuric** | Trunk, limbs | Neck stiffness, fever, altered consciousness |
| **Dengue** | Maculopapular (2–5 days after fever) | Trunk, limbs | **Biphasic (saddle-back) fever**, hemorrhagic manifestations |
| **Drug rash** | Variable | Generalized | Temporal relationship with drug |

### Prevention of Dengue and Chikungunya

**Vector Control (primary):**
1. **Source reduction** — eliminate Aedes breeding sites (standing water in flower pots, tyres, containers, gutters)
2. **Larviciding** — temephos (abate) in water containers
3. **Adulticiding** — space spraying/insecticide fogging during outbreaks

**Personal Protection:**
4. Mosquito repellents (DEET, picaridin)
5. Long-sleeved clothing
6. Window/door screens
7. Sleep under mosquito nets (especially daytime — Aedes bites during day)

**Community/Institutional:**
8. Community awareness and education
9. Proper waste disposal (remove potential breeding sites)
10. Surveillance and early case detection
11. Hospital preparedness for dengue haemorrhagic fever/dengue shock syndrome

> 🎯 Rash types: Measles = maculopapular (cephalocaudal) + Koplik. Rubella = finer + lymphadenopathy. Scarlet = sandpaper + strawberry tongue. Typhoid = rose spots. Chickenpox = vesicular (crops). Dengue/maculopapular + biphasic fever. Prevention = source reduction + repellent + community awareness.

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 115–250; Park's PSM

<summary><b>Q58. Planning cycle. Define planning and management.</b></summary>

### Planning
> 🔴 **Planning** = the process of deciding how the future should be better than the present, what changes are necessary, and how the changes should be implemented.

### Planning Cycle (3 Steps)
1. **Plan formulation** — identifying objectives, assessing needs, setting priorities
2. **Execution** — implementation of the plan with available resources
3. **Evaluation** — assessing outcomes, monitoring progress, making adjustments

### Types of Planning
| Type | Also Called | Duration |
|------|-----------|----------|
| **Long-term** | Perspective planning | 15–20 years |
| **Medium-term** | Strategic planning | 4–6 years |
| **Short-term** | Tactical planning | 1–2 years (coincides with budget) |

### Purposes of Planning
1. Match limited resources with many problems
2. Eliminate wasteful or duplicate expenditure
3. Develop best course of action for defined objectives

### Management
> 🔴 **Management** = the process of **planning, organizing, staffing, directing, coordinating, reporting, and budgeting** (POSDCORB) to achieve organizational goals efficiently and effectively.

**Functions of management:**
1. **Planning** — setting objectives and strategies
2. **Organizing** — arranging resources and tasks
3. **Staffing** — recruiting and developing personnel
4. **Directing** — leading and motivating
5. **Coordinating** — synchronizing activities
6. **Controlling** — monitoring performance
7. **Budgeting** — financial planning and control

**DG Health functions:** Planning → Implementation → Monitoring → Review

> 🎯 Planning = decide how future > present. Cycle = formulation → execution → evaluation. Types: long (15–20 yr) + medium (4–6 yr) + short (1–2 yr). Management = POSDCORB.
