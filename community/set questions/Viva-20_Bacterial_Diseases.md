## Q1. What is the significance of open case of TB. Mention the DOTS regimen. (S6,Q2)

### Significance of an open case of TB
> 🔴 **Open case of TB (smear-positive TB)** = patient with **at least one initial sputum smear positive for AFB**.
- **Epidemiological significance:** such a patient is **infectious to others** — he is a **source of infection** in the community (spreads TB bacilli by droplets). Open cases are **dangerous for the community**; a closed (smear-negative) case is not a significant source.

### DOTS regimen (standard WHO category-I)
- **DOTS** = **Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course** (introduced in **Bangladesh, 1993**).
- **Category I (new smear-positive):** initial phase **2 months HRZE** (isoniazid + rifampicin + pyrazinamide + ethambutol) **daily**, followed by continuation phase **4 months HR** (thrice weekly), all under direct observation by a DOT agent.
  - H = Isoniazid, R = Rifampicin, E = Ethambutol, Z = Pyrazinamide.
- **Category II: initial phase 3 (2+1) months, continuation 5 months.**

> 🎯 Open case = **sputum AFB-positive → infectious source.** DOTS = **2 HRZE / 4 HR, every dose watched.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 145–146, 149–150

## Q3. What are the significant of 'open case' of TB? Imterpret the "montoux" test. (S14,Q2)

### Significance of an open case of TB
> 🔴 Open (smear-positive) case = sputum AFB positive — **infectious to the community**, the principal reservoir/spreader of infection → **early detection, isolation-mindedness & treatment of open cases is the key to TB control.**

### Mantoux test (Tuberculin test) & its interpretation
- **What it is:** A **delayed hypersensitivity (type IV) test** measuring reaction to intradermal tuberculin — **PPD (2 TU PPD-RT-23 + Tween 80, WHO recommended)**.
- **Technique:** 0.1 ml injected **intradermally** on the dorsal aspect of the forearm.
- **Reading:** examined after **72 hours (3 days)**; induration measured across the transverse diameter.
- **Interpretation:**
  - **≥ 10 mm induration → Mantoux test positive** (indicates tuberculin infection/exposure).
  - < 5–10 mm → negative (no significant sensitivity).

**Positive occurs in:** active TB, past TB, tuberculous infection, after BCG vaccination.
**Negative may occur despite TB in:** kwashiorkor/malnutrition, measles & some vaccinations, **overwhelming disease (miliary TB, TBM)**, immunosuppressive therapy, **AIDS**, and during the **incubation period (4–6 weeks)** of infection.

> 🎯 Mantoux = **0.1 ml PPD ID forearm · read 72 hrs · ≥ 10 mm = positive** (but false-negative in miliary TB, AIDS, kwashiorkor).

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 143, 145

## Q4. What do you mean by DOTS? Describe the DOTS regimen. (S15,Q2)

### DOTS — definition
> 🔴 **DOTS** = **Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course** — a strategy in which **someone (usually a health worker — "DOT agent") takes responsibility for observing the patient take each dose** of the anti-TB drugs, ensuring full & regular treatment.

**Aim:** to ensure regular, complete anti-tubercular treatment (that the patient takes full treatment for TB).

**5 (6) elements of DOTS:**
1. **Political commitment** to the programme.
2. **Reliable microscopic diagnosis** (sputum smear).
3. **Standardized, multi-drug, directly observed treatment.**
4. **Adequate supply of drugs.**
5. **Proper recording & reporting** (cohort analysis of treatment outcome).

### DOTS regimen (WHO/NTP, Bangladesh)
| Category | Type of patient | Initial phase | Continuation phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | New smear-positive (no past / < 1 month anti-TB drug history) | **2 months HRZE** daily | **4 months HR** (3 times weekly) |
| **II** | Relapse / treatment-failure / defaulters (past > 1 month treatment) | **3 months (2+1) HRZE** | **5 months HRE** |

- H = Isoniazid; R = Rifampicin; E = Ethambutol; Z = Pyrazinamide; FDC = fixed-dose combination.
- BCG vaccination of newborns is part of EPI.

> 🎯 DOTS = **watch every dose** · 5 elements = **political commitment + microscopy + standardized Rx + drug supply + record/report.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 139–140, 145, 149–150

## Q5. What are the causitive agents and mode of transmission of enteric fever. (S7,Q2)

### Causative agents of enteric fever
> 🔴 Enteric fever includes both **typhoid and paratyphoid fevers**:
- **Typhoid fever — *Salmonella typhi*** (major cause).
- **Paratyphoid fever — *S. paratyphi* A and *S. paratyphi* B.**

### Mode of transmission
- **Faecal-oral route** — contamination of food & water by **facces and urine** from cases or carriers.
- **Fingers, flies, fomites, food & fluid (the "5 F's")** — direct or indirect contamination.
- Chief vehicle: **contaminated water & food**; overcrowding & poor sanitation favour spread.

> 🎯 Enteric fever = **S. typhi (+ S. paratyphi A & B)** · Transmission = **faeco-oral (5 F's — contaminated water/food).**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 126–128

## Q7. What are the types of diptheria. Mention the complications of diptheria. (S9,Q2)

### Types of diphtheria
Three major clinical types:
1. **Anterior nasal** (mild, often chronic).
2. **Tonsillar (faucal).**
3. **Pharyngeal.**

Other types: **Laryngeal** (serious in infants & young children) and **cutaneous**; occasionally eyes (conjunctiva), nose or vagina.

### Complications of diphtheria (time-wise)
| Time | Complication |
|---|---|
| **1st week** | **Laryngeal obstruction / paralysis** |
| **2nd week** | **Myocarditis**, acute circulatory failure (around 10th day) |
| **3rd week** | **Peripheral neuropathy**, palatal paralysis |
| **4th week** | Paralysis of muscles of accommodation |
| **5th week** | Generalized polyneuritis, paraesthesia, weakness |
| **6th week** | **Pharyngeal & diaphragmatic paralysis** |

> 🎯 Diphtheria types = **Nasal · Tonsillar · Pharyngeal (+ laryngeal, cutaneous)** · Complications = **obstruction → myocarditis → neuropathy.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 123, 126

## Q8. Tell the treatment of diptheria. What are the contents of diptheria patch? (S10,Q2)

### Treatment of diphtheria
1. **Notification** of the case to public health authorities.
2. **Admission/urgent referral** to a specialist infectious diseases unit; **rest & isolation**.
3. **Antitoxin (ADS)** — the definitive treatment — given **IM** in adequate dose:
   - Mild cases: 4,000–8,000 units; moderate severity: **16,000–40,000 units**.
   - A test dose (history & sensitivity test for horse serum) precedes the full dose.
4. **Antibiotics:** Penicillin (or amoxicillin) or **erythromycin** (if penicillin-allergic) for **2 weeks** to eliminate *C. diphtheriae*.
5. **Immunize with diphtheria toxoid** after recovery (natural infection gives poor immunity).
6. **Strict isolation** until 3 throat swabs (24 hrs apart) are culture-negative.
7. **Treatment of complications** (e.g. tracheostomy for laryngeal obstruction).

### Contents of "diphtheria patch" (diphtheria vaccine/toxoid component)
- **Diphtheria toxoid** = **formaldehyde-inactivated diphtheria toxin** (from *C. diphtheriae* culture).
- **Purified, concentrated, adsorbed on an adjuvant** — **aluminium phosphate/aluminium hydroxide** (to enhance immunogenicity).
- Formulated as **DPT (with pertussis + tetanus toxoids)** or **DT (paediatric) / Td (adult)**, 0.5 ml IM/SC.

> 🎯 Treatment = **antitoxin (ADS) + penicillin/erythromycin + toxoid immunization + isolation** · Patch/vaccine = **formalinized diphtheria toxoid adsorbed on aluminium adjuvant (DPT/DT).**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 126–127

## Q9. Mention the common complications of wooping cough? (S11,Q2)

### Complications of whooping cough (pertussis)
**1. Respiratory system:**
- **Bronchitis, bronchopneumonia, bronchiectasis.**
- **Haemoptysis, atelectasis.**

**2. Eye:** sub-conjunctival haemorrhage.
**3. Nose:** epistaxis.
**4. Brain:** punctate cerebral haemorrhage.
**5. Rectum:** **rectal prolapse.**
**6. Tongue:** **ulceration of the frenulum** (in children).
**7. Umbilicus:** **umbilical hernia** (from paroxysmal coughing).

> 🎯 Pertussis complications = **bronchopneumonia · subconjunctival haemorrhage · rectal prolapse · frenular ulcer · umbilical hernia.**

## Q10. Enumerate the characteristics features of Clostridium tetani and mention the clinical features of tetanus. (S18,Q2)

### Characteristics of *Clostridium tetani*
1. **Gram-positive** bacillus.
2. **Anaerobic** (obligate) organism.
3. **Spore-forming** — spores are highly resistant & found in **soil, dust, earth, animal & human excreta**.
4. Reservoir: intestine of infected animals (cattle, goats, sheep).
5. Produces the **exotoxin tetanospasmin** (released after death of bacilli) — acts by blocking release of inhibitory neurotransmitters (glycine & GABA), causing unopposed motor-neuron discharge.
6. Enters through wounds — **puncture wounds, pinpricks, abrasions, burns, unsterile surgery, bites**; spores germinate in necrotic, poorly oxygenated tissue.

### Clinical features of tetanus
1. **Restlessness, irritability, pain, rigidity & twitching** at onset; then muscle **spasm**.
2. **Trismus (lockjaw)** — spasm of the masseter muscle (earliest sign).
3. **Risus sardonicus** (sardonic smile) — spasm of facial muscles.
4. **Opisthotonos** — spasm of back muscles (arched back).
5. **Generalized tonic spasms** — hyper-reflexia & **spastic paralysis**; preponderence of powerful muscles.
6. Localized/caudal rigidity; spasms may involve pharyngeal & laryngeal muscles → difficulty swallowing/breathing.
7. **Death usually results from respiratory muscle paralysis.**

> 🎯 *Cl. tetani* = **Gram +ve · anaerobic · spore-forming · tetanospasmin** · Features = **trismus → risus sardonicus → opisthotonos → generalized spasm.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 132–133

## Q11. What do you mean by neonatal tetanus? Mentin the preventive measures of tetanus neonatorum. (S19,Q2)

### Neonatal tetanus (tetanus neonatorum)
> 🔴 **Neonatal tetanus** = tetanus occurring in the newborn (usually 3–28 days of life), typically due to **septic cutting of the umbilical cord by unsterilized instruments** in unhygienic delivery. Features: **inability to suck** → trismus → generalized spasms (opisthotonos) → high mortality.

### Prevention & control of tetanus neonatorum
**1. Immunization of mother (tetanus toxoid):**
- TT 0.5 ml IM, 5 doses from **15 years** (TT₁ first exposure ≥ 15 yrs; TT₂ +1 month; TT₃ +6 months; TT₄ +1 year; TT₅ +1 year; min gap between doses but no max gap).
- **Pregnant woman:** if unimmunized — **TT₁ at 16–20 weeks, TT₂ ≥ 4 weeks later & ≥ 4 weeks before delivery**; if previously immunized — **single booster**.

**2. Proper antenatal care** & health education of the mother.

**3. Intra-natal care — clean delivery practices** (can reduce ~90% of neonatal tetanus deaths):
- **Clean hands** of birth attendants.
- **Clean delivery surface.**
- **Clean cord care** — clean blade & clean tie for cutting/tying the cord.

**4. Postnatal measures:** infants of mothers without 2 doses of TT → **anti-tetanus toxin (antitoxin) within 6 hours of birth**.

**5. Other measures:** health-centre/hospital deliveries; training & equipping village midwives (TBA) & MCH workers; health education.

> 🎯 Prevention = **TT immunization of mother · clean delivery & cord care · antitoxin at birth if unimmunized mother.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 132–135
