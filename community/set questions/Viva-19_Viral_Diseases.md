## Q1. Why and how small pox was possible to be eradicated? (S1,Q2)

### Why smallpox eradication was possible (epidemiological basis)
1. **No known animal reservoir** (man is the only host).
2. **No long-term carrier** of the virus.
3. **Life-long immunity** after recovery from the disease.
4. **Easy recognition** of cases even by non-medical persons.
5. **Sub-clinical infections did not transmit** the disease.
6. Availability of a **stable, potent vaccine.**
7. **International co-operation.**

### How it was done
- **Edward Jenner** introduced smallpox vaccination in **1796**. Global eradication programme initiated by **WHO in 1958**, intensified since **1967** (surveillance & containment).
- **Last natural case:** Somalia, **26 October 1977**.
- **8 May 1980**, the World Health Assembly declared global eradication.
- In Bangladesh: last case (variola major) — **Rahima Banu, Bhola, 16 Oct 1975**; certified smallpox-free since **December 1977**.

> 🎯 Eradication possible = **human-only host · no carrier · lifelong immunity · easy recognition · potent vaccine + global co-operation.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 182–183

## Q2. Enumerate the characteristics of chicken pox rash? (S1,Q2)

### Characteristics of chicken pox rash
1. **Distribution:** symmetrical; rash is **centripetal** — first & most abundant on the **trunk**, then face, arms; **palms & soles usually not affected**; mucosal surfaces (mouth, conjunctiva) & axilla may be affected.
2. **Rapid evolution:** **Macule → Papule → Vesicle → Scab** in 2–4 days (usually within a day or two the macule becomes a vesicle).
3. **"Dew-drop on rose petal" appearance** — superficial, unilocular, fluid-filled vesicles with an area of **inflammation around** them.
4. **Pleomorphism:** all stages (papule, vesicle, crust) seen **simultaneously in the same area** — a hallmark of chickenpox.
5. **Successive crops** of rash, more on covered surfaces, scalp & mucous membranes.
6. **Fever may show exacerbations** with each fresh crop of eruption (temperature rises with each new crop).

> 🎯 Chickenpox rash = **centripetal · pleomorphic · dew-drop vesicles · macule→papule→vesicle→scab · successive crops.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 175, 180–181

## Q3. Mention the complications of chicken pox. (S3,Q2)

### Complications of chicken pox
1. **Haemorrhage (varicella haemorrhagica).**
2. **Pneumonia** (varicella pneumonia).
3. **Encephalitis.**
4. **Acute cerebral ataxia.**
5. **Reye's syndrome** (acute encephalopathy with fatty degeneration of viscera, especially liver).
6. **Congenital defects & malformations** (if in pregnancy) — foetal wastage, cutaneous scars, atrophied limbs, microcephaly, low birth weight, cataract, microphthalmia, chorioretinitis, deafness, cerebro-cortical atrophy.
7. **Oncogenicity** (VZV).
8. **Skin infection, septicaemia, septic arthritis** (secondary bacterial).

> 🎯 Chickenpox complications = **haemorrhage · pneumonia · encephalitis · Reye's syndrome · congenital defects.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 180–181

## Q4. What are the characteristics of dengue rash? (S2,Q2)

### Characteristics of dengue rash
1. **Maculopapular rash** — typically over the **dorsum of limbs and trunk**.
2. Usually appears about the **2nd–5th day of fever** (with defervescence/defervescing phase).
3. May be accompanied by **flushing** (erythematous flush over face, neck and chest).
4. Histopathologically due to **extravasation** — perivascular infiltration with petechial haemorrhagic component in DHF.
5. Associated with other symptoms — **retro-orbital pain, muscle/joint pain, headache, nausea, vomiting**.

> 🎯 Dengue rash = **maculopapular, dorsum of limbs & trunk, with flushing; petechial in DHF.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 175, 191–192 (dorsum of limbs/trunk; maculopapular); clinical features p. 191

## Q5. What do you mean by Dengue Shock syndrome. (S8,Q2)

### Dengue Shock Syndrome (DSS)
> 🔴 **DSS** = the most severe manifestation of dengue viral infection — **classical dengue/haemorrhagic dengue with features of circulatory failure (shock)**.

**Features:**
- Rapid, weak (thready) pulse with **narrowing of pulse pressure (< 20 mm Hg)** or **hypotension** with **cold, clammy skin** and **restlessness**.
- Haemorrrhagic manifestations: petechiae, purpura, ecchymosis, epistaxis, gum bleeding, haematemesis/melaena.
- **Thrombocytopenia (< 100,000/mm³)** and **haematocrit rise > 20%** (haemoconcentration).

**Pathogenesis (WHO):** previous infection → heterologous strain infection → large amounts of cross-reacting antibody → **antigen-antibody complex** → complement activation → **increased vascular permeability** → plasma extravasation → **shock**.

> 🎯 DSS = **DF/DHF + circulatory failure (narrow pulse pressure, hypotension, cold clammy skin, restlessness).**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 192–193

## Q6. Explain dengue shock syndrome. (S22,Q2)

*Repeat of Q5* — DSS = classical dengue + **features of circulatory failure**:
- Rapid, weak pulse, narrowing of pulse pressure (< 20 mm Hg) or **hypotension with cold clammy skin & restlessness**.
- Thrombocytopenia (< 100,000/mm³); haematocrit ↑ > 20%.
- Pathogenesis: cross-reacting antibodies → antigen-antibody complex → complement activation → ↑ vascular permeability → plasma extravasation → shock.

## Q7. Classify dengue. What do you mean by dengue shock syndrome? (S28,Q2)

### Classification of dengue (dengue syndrome)
```text
Dengue virus infection
├── Asymptomatic
└── Symptomatic
    ├── Undifferentiated (viral) fever
    └── Dengue fever (classic)
        └── Dengue haemorrhagic fever (DHF)
            ├── DHF without shock
            └── Dengue shock syndrome (DSS)
```

### Severity grading (WHO, 5 grades)
| Grade | Features |
|---|---|
| I | DF features (or history) + **leucopenia + thrombocytopenia**; no change in haematocrit |
| II | DF features + **bleeding** (positive tourniquet test / spontaneous bleeding); thrombocytopenia < 100,000/mm³, HCT ↑ > 20% |
| III | Features/history of DF + **circulatory failure (DSS-III)** |
| IV | Features/history of DF + **profound shock (DSS-IV)** |

### Dengue shock syndrome
As in Q5/Q6 — DF/DHF with circulatory failure: rapid weak pulse, pulse pressure < 20 mm Hg, hypotension, cold clammy skin, restlessness, thrombocytopenia < 100,000/mm³ & haemoconcentration.

> 🎯 Dengue = **Asymptomatic → DF → DHF → DSS** — DSS = shock grade III–IV.

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 190–192

## Q8. List the complications of measles. (S2,Q2)

### Complications of measles
**1. Effects of measles virus itself:**
- Stomatitis, enteritis, **pneumonia**, keratitis, **measles-associated diarrhoea**.

**2. Secondary bacterial infection:**
- **Otitis media, bronchopneumonia, conjunctivitis.**

**3. Neurological complications:**
- **Post-viral encephalitis.**
- **Sub-acute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE)** — late, rare complication.

**4. Nutritional:**
- Severe weight loss, **kwashiorkor**, corneal ulceration (**→ keratomalacia, blindness — treated with vitamin A**).

> 🎯 Measles complications = **pneumonia · otitis media · encephalitis/SSPE · diarrhoea & malnutrition (vit-A deficiency blindness).**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF p. 174

## Q9. What is pathognomic sign of measles? (S3,Q2)

### Koplik's spot — the pathognomonic sign of measles
> 🔴 **Koplik's spots** are the **pathognomonic sign of measles**, characterized by:
1. **Small, bluish-white spots on a red base.**
2. Smaller than the **head of a pin**.
3. Found on the **buccal mucosa opposite the 1st & 2nd upper molar teeth**.
4. Appear **1–2 days before the rash** (prodromal/catarrhal stage, day 2+).

**Importance:** Diagnosis of measles is based on the typical rash and Koplik's spots.

> 🎯 Koplik's spots = **blue-white pin-head spots on red base, buccal mucosa near upper molars, 1–2 days before rash.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF p. 173

## Q10. Enumerate the complications of German measles. (S7,Q2)

### Complications of Rubella (German measles)
**1. Congenital rubella syndrome (CRS)** — the most important (if infection in 1st trimester):
- Classical triad: **Patent ductus arteriosus, cataract & deafness.**
- Risk of congenital anomaly: **↑ 80%** in 1st 12 weeks of pregnancy; < 5% after 16 weeks.
- Other congenital defects: septal defects, **mental retardation**, microcephaly, low birth weight, microscopically chorioretinitis.

**2. Rare complications in the adult/child:**
- **Thrombocytopenic purpura.**
- **Encephalitis** (rare).
- **Arthralgia/arthritis** (more in adult females).
- Orchitis (rare).

> 🎯 German measles risk = **CRS — PDA + cataract + deafness** (worst in 1st trimester).

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 176–178

## Q11. Name the complications of mumps. (S8,Q2)

### Complications of mumps
**Frequent complications:**
1. **Orchitis** (most common in adult males).
2. **Ovaritis.**
3. **Pancreatitis.**
4. **Meningo-encephalitis** (most common in childhood).
5. Thyroiditis · Neuritis · Hepatitis · Myocarditis.

**Rare complications:** Bilateral orchitis → **sterility**; **diabetes.**

**Rarer complications:** Nerve (sensorineural) deafness, polyarthritis, hydrocephalus, encephalitis, cerebellar ataxia, facial palsy, transverse myelitis.

> 🎯 Mumps complications = **orchitis · ovaritis · pancreatitis · meningo-encephalitis (frequent).**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF p. 160

## Q12. How can you control Hepatitis B infection in a community? (S10,Q2)

### Control of Hepatitis B in a community
**1. Immunization:**
- **Hepatitis B vaccine** — 0, 1, 6 months (IM 1 ml; children < 10 yrs: 0.5 ml); booster every 3–5 years.
- **Passive-active immunization** — HBIG + vaccine for accidental exposure (HBIG within 6 hrs, preferably not later than 48 hrs; test HBsAg first; if negative start a full course of vaccine).

**2. Screening & safe practices:**
- **Screening of all blood donors** for HBsAg — reject HBsAg-positive blood.
- **Sterilization of surgical instruments** & use of disposable needles/syringes.
- Carriers should **not share razors & tooth brushes.**
- **Barrier contraception** (condom, diaphragm).
- **Health education** of the community about transmission & prevention.

**3. Protection of high-risk groups:**
- Pre-exposure vaccination in high-prevalence countries (like Bangladesh).
- Vaccination of **newborns of HBsAg-positive mothers** (passive-active → prevents carrier state).
- Vaccine for health-care workers, sex workers & those with multiple sex partners.

> 🎯 Hep-B control = **vaccine (0,1,6) + HBIG for exposure + screen blood donors + sterilize instruments + barrier method + health education.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 165–168

## Q13. Mention the types and severity of Hepatitis with their route of transmission. (S20,Q2)

### Types of viral hepatitis with route of transmission
| Type | Route of transmission | Severity / course |
|---|---|---|
| **Hepatitis A** (infectious hepatitis) | **Faeco-oral** (contaminated food/water); also parenteral & sexual | Acute, self-limiting; **no chronic carrier**, no chronicity; life-long immunity; most recover in 2–4 weeks |
| **Hepatitis B** (serum hepatitis) | **Parenteral** (blood, contaminated needles/syringes), **perinatal/vertical** (mother-to-child), **sexual** | Can persist → **chronic active hepatitis, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma** |
| **Hepatitis C** | **Blood**, sexual contact, **mother-to-child** (during birth) | **Most dangerous** — high chronicity → **cirrhosis & HCC** |
| **Hepatitis D** (delta) | Parenteral, sexual (superinfection with B) | Severe — increases severity of Hep B |
| **Hepatitis E** (epidemic) | **Faeco-oral** (water/food borne) | Acute, self-limiting; **high mortality in pregnancy**; no chronicity |

**Classification by route (easiest):**
- **Faeco-oral:** HAV, HEV.
- **Parenteral/sexual/perinatal (blood-borne):** HBV, HCV, HDV, HGV.

> 🎯 **A & E = faeco-oral (acute) · B, C, D = blood/sex/perinatal (chronic → cirrhosis/HCC).**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 148, 162

## Q14. Classify infective hepatitis. How can you prevent hepatitis A infection in a community. (S21,Q2)

### Classification of infective (viral) hepatitis
- **According to duration:** Acute & chronic hepatitis.
- **According to aetiology:**
  - **Classical hepatitis viruses:** HAV, HBV, HCV, HDV, HEV, HGV.
  - **Other viruses:** CMV, EBV, yellow fever virus, rubella, herpes simplex, varicella, adenovirus (esp. in immunocompromised).
  - Non-viral causes: autoimmune, toxins, alcohol, drugs (e.g. anti-TB drugs); miscellaneous (Wilson's disease).

### Prevention of Hepatitis A in a community
1. **Control of reservoir (difficult):**
   - Notification, **isolation & complete bed rest** of patients, **disinfection of faeces & fomites**.
2. **Control of transmission:**
   - Personal & community hygiene — **hand washing after toilet & before eating**, **sanitary disposal of excreta**, **purification of community water supplies**.
3. **Control of susceptible population:**
   - **Human immunoglobulin** (passive immunity) early in the incubation period — prevents or mitigates the disease.
   - **Vaccines** for active immunization are being developed (cost is high).

> 🎯 HAV prevention = **hand washing · safe water & food · sanitary excreta disposal · immunoglobulin in contacts.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 149, 162–164

## Q15. What do you mean by epidemic jaundice? Mention its occurrence. (S22,Q2)

### Epidemic jaundice
> 🔴 **Epidemic jaundice** = jaundice occurring in epidemic form due to **Hepatitis A or Hepatitis E** viral infection, transmitted through **contaminated food or water** (faeco-oral route). Outbreaks are common where sanitation & water supplies are poor (e.g. hostel outbreaks, flood-affected areas, slum communities).

### Occurrence
- **Hepatitis A / infectious hepatitis:** endemic in most **developing countries**; outbreaks occur after **flood, heavy rainfall** — poor sanitation & overcrowding favour spread (water-borne/food-borne epidemics).
- **Hepatitis E:** causes **large water-borne epidemics (epidemic non-A non-B hepatitis)** in developing countries; **high case-fatality in pregnant women** (up to 20%).
- Slum people & overcrowded institutions (hostels, schools) are particularly affected.

> 🎯 Epidemic jaundice = **HAV/HEV via contaminated water/food** — outbreaks in poor sanitation, floods, slums.

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 150, 163, 169

## Q16. Define infective jaundice. What are the after effects/consequences of hepatitis? How can you prevent infective jaundice in a community? (S23,Q2)

### Infective jaundice
> 🔴 **Infective (viral) hepatitis** = inflammation of the liver caused by hepatitis viruses (HAV–HEV), presenting with **jaundice** (dark urine, yellowish sclera/skin) plus prodromal fever, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, fatigue & enlarged liver.

### After-effects / consequences of hepatitis
1. **Fulminant hepatic failure.**
2. **Chronic hepatitis.**
3. **Cirrhosis of liver.**
4. **Hepatocellular carcinoma** (HBV/HCV).
5. Relapsing hepatic failure.
6. Aplastic anaemia.
7. Post-hepatitis syndrome.
8. Cholestatic hepatitis.
9. Renal failure (hepato-renal syndrome).
10. Hyperbilirubinaemia & its complications.
11. Connective tissue disease, papular acrodermatitis.

### Prevention of infective jaundice in a community
1. **Safe water supply & sanitation** — purification of community water, sanitary disposal of excreta.
2. **Personal hygiene** — hand washing before eating & after toilet.
3. **Food hygiene** — avoid contaminated/unboiled food & water.
4. **Isolation of cases** & disinfection of faeces/fomites.
5. **Immunoglobulin** for exposed contacts (HAV).
6. **Vaccination** (HAV & HBV vaccine) & screening of blood donors (for HBV/HCV).
7. **Health education.**

> 🎯 Consequences = **fulminant failure · chronic hepatitis · cirrhosis · HCC** · Prevention = **water, sanitation, hygiene, isolation, vaccine.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 149, 163–164, 168

## Q17. Classify rabies animal. Differentiate fixed and street virus. (S4,Q2)

### Classification of rabid animals
**A. By type (domestic vs wild):**
- **Domestic animals:** dog (99% of cases in Bangladesh), cat, cattle, horse, pig.
- **Wild animals:** fox, jackal, wolf, mongoose, hyena, deer, monkey, **bat**.

**B. By risk category (classical classification):**
- **Type A — rabid & carnivorous animals (highest risk):** dog, cat, wolf, jackal, fox, mongoose.
- **Type B — domestic/farm animals (lower risk):** cattle, buffalo, horse, sheep, goat.
- **Type C — low-risk:** rabbit, rats, mice, squirrels (bites usually do not carry risk of rabies).

### Street virus vs Fixed virus
| Feature | **Street virus** | **Fixed virus** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Virus recovered from **naturally occurring cases** of rabies | Virus produced by **serial brain-to-brain passage** of street virus in rabbits (incubation fixed at 4–6 days) |
| Incubation period | **Long & variable** (20–60 days) | **Short & fixed** |
| Negri bodies | **Formed** | **Not formed** |
| Multiplication | Neuronal **+ extra-neuronal** (muscle) tissue | Neuronal tissue only (no muscle) |
| Pathogenicity | Pathogenic to all mammals | Non-pathogenic (used to prepare vaccine) |
| Vaccine preparation | Not used | **Used to prepare anti-rabies vaccine** |

> 🎯 Street virus = **natural, variable, forms Negri bodies** · Fixed virus = **rabbit-passaged, fixed 4–6 days, vaccine strain.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 183–185

## Q18. What are the indications of anti-rabies treatment (post exposure immunization) in relation to animal bite? (S30,Q2)

### Indications of anti-rabies treatment (post-exposure)
1. The biting animal **shows signs of rabies or dies within 10 days** of the bite.
2. The biting animal **cannot be traced or identified**.
3. **Unprovoked bites.**
4. **Laboratory tests positive** for rabies (fluorescent rabies antibody test on brain / Negri bodies) in the bitten animal.
5. **All bites by wild animals.**

**Grade of exposure requiring treatment:**
- Category II: minor scratches/abrasions without bleeding; licks on broken skin.
- Category III: **single or multiple transdermal bites/scratches**, contamination of mucous membrane with saliva, exposure to bats → **vaccine + RIG (HRIG) definitely indicated.**

> 🎯 Treat when = **rabid/dying animal · untraceable animal · unprovoked bite · lab-positive · wild animal bite.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 187–188

## Q19. What do you mean by eradication of disease? Mention the factors to be considered in eradicating of Poliomyelitis. (S16,Q2)

### Eradication of disease
> 🔴 Eradication = termination of **all transmission of infection by extermination of the infectious agent** through surveillance and containment (e.g. smallpox — the only disease eradicated).

### Factors favouring eradication of poliomyelitis
1. **Man is the only host** (no animal reservoir).
2. **No long-term carrier state.**
3. **OPV is easy to administer** (oral, no trained personnel needed).
4. **OPV is cheap.**
5. **Half-life of excreted virus is 48 hours** — spread can only occur in this short period.
6. Live attenuated OPV viruses multiply in the intestine & **interrupt transmission of wild viruses (herd immunity)**.
7. **OPV induces both humoral and intestinal immunity.**
8. Effective surveillance (AFP surveillance) & national immunization days.

> 🎯 Polio eradicable = **human-only host · no carrier · cheap easy OPV · short virus half-life · herd immunity via OPV.**

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 156, 159–160

## Q20. Difference between Salk and Sabin polio vaccine. (S17,Q2)

### IPV (Salk) vs OPV (Sabin)
| Feature | **IPV (Salk)** | **OPV (Sabin)** |
|---|---|---|
| Type | **Killed (formalin-inactivated)** virus | **Live attenuated** virus |
| Route | Subcutaneous or **IM** | **Oral** |
| Immunity | Humoral (circulating antibody) only — **no intestinal/local immunity** | **Both humoral & intestinal immunity**; antibody quickly produced |
| Effect on transmission | Prevents paralysis but **does not prevent intestinal reinfection** by wild virus | Prevents paralysis **and** intestinal reinfection (interrupts transmission) |
| Epidemic control | Not useful in controlling epidemics | **Very useful in controlling epidemics** |
| Dose response | Requires multiple doses | Even a single dose gives substantial immunity |
| Production/Cost | More difficult to manufacture → **costly** | Easy to manufacture → **cheap** |
| Storage | Needs strict cold chain (labile) | More heat stable; longer shelf-life |
| Use | **Developed countries** (avoid vaccine-associated polio) | **Developing countries / mass campaigns (EPI)** |

> 🎯 **Salk = killed/IM/humoral-only · Sabin = live/oral/humoral+intestinal.** OPV best for mass campaigns.

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 160–161

## Q21. What are the diseases subject to international regulation? Tell the mode of transmission and vector of yellow fever. (S30,Q6)

### Diseases subject to international regulation (quarantinable / IHR-notifiable)
1. **Plague** (bubonic 2–7 days, septicaemic 2–7 days, pneumonic 1–3 days — incubation).
2. **Cholera** (incubation ~3–6 days, 6 days under IHR isolation).
3. **Yellow fever** (incubation 3–6 days).
4. **Smallpox** (formerly quarantinable — now eradicated; deleted from the list).
> Under current IHR (2005): disease events of international concern include plague, cholera, yellow fever, polio, SARS, and human influenza (new subtype).

### Yellow fever — mode of transmission & vector
- **Mode of transmission: vector-borne** — transmitted by the **bite of an infected female *Aedes* mosquito**; man is the reservoir (urban cycle: man → *Aedes aegypti* → man); no person-to-person spread.
- **Vector: *Aedes aegypti* mosquito** (also vector of dengue fever & chikungunya).

> 🎯 International regulation = **Plague · Cholera · Yellow fever** · YF vector = **Aedes aegypti** (mosquito bite).

> 📖 Ch-05, Endeavour PDF pp. 121–122; Ch-07, Endeavour PDF p. 281 (Aedes → yellow fever, dengue, chikungunya)
