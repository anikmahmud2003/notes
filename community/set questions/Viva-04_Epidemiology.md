<summary><b>Q1. Define Epidemiology &amp; Epidemic. What are the types of Epidemics? (S1,Q2)</b></summary>

### Epidemiology
> 🔴 Epidemiology = **"The study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to the control of health problems."** — John M. Last (1988)

- Word origin (3 Greek roots): **Epi** (among) + **Demos** (people) + **Logos** (study).
- Health-related states & events include: nutrition, smoking, life-style, water supply, birth, death, marriage, migration.
- Basic components (3): **Disease frequency · Distribution of disease (time, place, person) · Determinants of disease.**

### Epidemic
> 🔴 **Epidemic (Epi = upon, demos = people)** = the *unusual occurrence* in a community or region of disease, specific health-related behaviour (e.g., smoking) or health-related events (e.g., traffic accidents) *clearly in excess of the expected occurrence*.

### Types of epidemics (3 major types)
1. **Common-source epidemics**
   - Single exposure or **point source** epidemics (e.g., food poisoning, Bhopal gas tragedy).
   - **Continuous or multiple exposure** epidemics (e.g., Legionnaire's disease 1976 Philadelphia, gonorrhoea).
2. **Propagated epidemics** — person-to-person (hepatitis A, polio), arthropod vector, animal reservoir.
3. **Slow (modern) epidemics** — e.g., cancer.

> 🎯 Definition mnemonics: **Epi (among) + Demos (people) + Logos (study)** → distribution + determinants + application to control.

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 71–72 (Epidemiology), pp. 94–97 (Epidemic & types)

<summary><b>Q2. Aims of Epidemiology. Mention the major types of epidemics (S2,Q2)</b></summary>

### Aims & objectives of epidemiology (International Epidemiological Association)
1. To **describe the distribution and magnitude** of health and disease problems in human populations.
2. To **identify aetiological (risk) factors** in the pathogenesis of disease.
3. To **provide data essential to the planning, implementation and evaluation** of services for the prevention, control and treatment of disease and to setting up priorities among those services.

**Ultimate aim of epidemiology:** To **lead effective action** — i.e., to eliminate or reduce the health problem or its consequences, and to promote the health and well-being of society as a whole.

### Uses / scopes of modern epidemiology
1. To study historically the **rise and fall** of disease in the population.
2. **Community diagnosis**.
3. **Planning and evaluation**.
4. Evaluation of **individual's risks and chances**.
5. **Syndrome identification**.
6. **Completing the natural history** of disease.
7. Searching for **causes and risk factors**.

### Major types of epidemics
1. **Common-source epidemics:** point source (single exposure) — food poisoning, Bhopal gas tragedy; continuous/multiple exposure — Legionnaire's disease, gonorrhoea.
2. **Propagated epidemics:** person-to-person (hepatitis A, polio), arthropod vector, animal reservoir.
3. **Slow (modern) epidemics:** e.g., cancer.

<summary><b>Q3. Define Epidemiology. Illustrate Epidemiological approach. (S3,Q2)</b></summary>

### Definition
*(Epidemiology = study of distribution & determinants of health-related states/events in specified populations + application to control — John M. Last, 1988.)*

### Epidemiological approach (2 steps)
The epidemiologist studies disease occurrence by **asking questions** and **making comparisons**.

**Step 1 — Asking questions:**
| Question | Focus |
|---|---|
| **What** is the disease/health event? | Nature of the problem |
| **Who** is affected? | Person (age, sex, etc.) |
| **Where** did it occur? | Place / geographical distribution |
| **When** did it occur? | Time distribution |
| How much? (rates, ratios) | Disease frequency |

**Step 2 — Making comparisons:**
- Disease frequency is compared **between different groups/populations** (e.g., smokers vs non-smokers, different ages, different places, different times).
- These comparisons provide clues to **aetiology (causation)** and help formulate a **hypothesis**.
- For example, comparing **lung cancer frequency in smokers vs non-smokers** pointed to smoking as a cause.

> 🎯 **4 W-question = What · Who · Where · When** → then **COMPARE** to draw conclusions.

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 71–74

<summary><b>Q4. What are epidemiological measurements? Define incidence &amp; prevalence. (S4,Q2)</b></summary>

### Epidemiological measurements
Measurement of **mortality** (death) and **morbidity** (illness/disability) is the basic quantitative tool of epidemiology. They are calculated as **rates, ratios and proportions**; the important indices are —
- **Mortality measures:** Crude death rate, specific death rates, case fatality rate, proportional mortality rate, survival rate.
- **Morbidity measures:** **Incidence rate & Prevalence rate**, plus attack rate, secondary attack rate.

### Incidence
> 🔴 **Incidence / incidence rate** = **number of NEW cases** of a disease occurring in a **defined population during a specified period of time** (usually 1 year).

```
Incidence rate = No. of NEW cases of the disease during a given period   × 1,000
                  Population at risk during that period
```

### Prevalence
> 🔴 **Prevalence / prevalence rate** = **number of ALL current cases (old + new)** existing at a **given point in time or over a period of time** in a given population.

```
Prevalence rate = No. of current cases (old + new) in a given time/period   × 100
                  Estimated mid-interval population at risk
```

> 🎯 **Incidence = NEW cases only · Prevalence = ALL (old + new) existing cases.**

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 75–80

<summary><b>Q5. Tools used in epidemiological measurements with their definition. (S5,Q2)</b></summary>

### Basic tools: Ratio, Proportion, Rate
| Tool | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ratio** | Expression of the relationship between a **numerator and a denominator (larger or smaller)**; the numerator is **not necessarily a part of the denominator**. | Sex ratio (M:F), maternal mortality ratio, stillbirth ratio |
| **Proportion** | A **ratio in which the numerator is INCLUDED in the denominator**; thus it shows the part of a whole. A proportion can be expressed as a **percentage**. | Proportion of deaths due to TB among all deaths (PMR) |
| **Rate** | A **measure of the frequency of a health event in a defined population in a specified period**; it has 4 elements: **numerator (event), denominator (population at risk), time, multiplier (K)**. | Incidence rate, death rate |

```
Rate =  X (No. of events)   ×  K   (e.g., ×1000)
        Y (population at risk)
```

### Principal disease-frequency indices (used as tools)
**Incidence rate · Prevalence rate · Attack rate · Secondary attack rate · Crude death rate · Specific death rate · Case fatality rate · Proportional mortality rate.**

> 🎯 **Ratio (not part of) vs Proportion (part of) vs Rate (part + time).** Rate must always state **population + time + multiplier (1000/100,000).**

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 75–76

<summary><b>Q6. What are the mortality rate and ratios? (S6,Q2)</b></summary>

### Commonly used mortality rates & ratio measures
1. **Crude death rate (CDR)**
2. **Specific death rates**
3. **Case fatality rate (CFR) — a ratio**
4. **Proportional mortality rate (PMR) — a ratio**
5. **Survival rate**
6. **Adjusted / standardized rates**

### Crude death rate
> Number of deaths (from **all causes**) per 1000 estimated **mid-year population** in one year in a given place.

```
CDR =  No. of deaths during the year   × 1,000
       Mid-year population
```

### Case fatality rate (ratio)
| Numerator | Denominator |
|---|---|
| Total No. of deaths due to a **particular disease** | Total No. of **cases due to the same disease** |

- Represents the **"killing power"** of a disease.
- Classically used in acute infectious diseases (food poisoning, cholera, measles).
- Closely related to **virulence**.

### Proportional mortality rate (ratio)
> **PMR** = proportion of **total deaths** due to a particular cause (or age group).

```
PMR =  Total No. of deaths from the specific disease in a year   × 100 (or 1000)
       Total No. of deaths from all causes in that year
```

**Advantages of PMR:** simplest measure of disease burden in the community; used when population data are not available. **Disadvantage:** limited value.

### Specific death rate
```
Specific death rate =  Total No. of deaths from a specific disease in a year   × 1,000
                       Mid-year population in the same year
```

> 🎯 **CDR (all deaths/1000 mid-yr pop) · CFR (deaths due to disease/cases of disease = killing power of a disease) · PMR (deaths from specific cause/total deaths = proportion).**

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 76–78

<summary><b>Q7. What are the aspects do we consider to measure morbidity? (S7,Q2)</b></summary>

Morbidity (illness) in the community is measured by the following rates:

| Measure | What it answers |
|---|---|
| **1. Incidence rate** | Annual rate of **new cases** (onset) of disease in the population at risk |
| **2. Prevalence rate** | **All current cases** (old + new) at a point or period of time |
| **3. Attack rate (case rate)** | Special incidence rate — proportion of population developing disease **during an outbreak** (epidemic) in a defined interval |
| **4. Secondary attack rate (SAR)** | Spread of infection **within a group/family contacts** (after primary cases) |
| **5. Disease notification / registration** | Incidence data collected through **notification** of notifiable diseases |

Additionally, **disability rates** (temporary/permant disability) measure the consequence of morbidity.

*(Detail: SAR = no. of exposed persons developing the disease within the range of the incubation period ÷ total no. of exposed/susceptible contacts × 100.)*

> 🎯 Morbidity = **Incidence · Prevalence · Attack rate · SAR** — the "how much illness" family.

<summary><b>Q8. What is cohort. List the steps of cohort study. Measures of morbidity. (S8,Q8)</b></summary>

### Cohort (definition)
> 🔴 **Cohort** = a group of people who **share a common characteristic or experience within a defined time period** (e.g., age, occupation, exposure to a drug or vaccine, pregnancy, insured persons).

**Examples:** birth cohort (born same year); marriage cohort; exposure cohort (exposed to a common drug/vaccine/infection).

### Steps / elements of a cohort study (5)
1. **Selection of study subjects** — from general population or special groups (doctors, nurses, students, teachers, lawyers).
2. **Obtaining data on exposure** — personal interview/mailed questionnaire; review of records; medical examination/special tests; environmental surveys.
3. **Selection of comparison groups** — internal comparisons · external comparisons · comparison with general population rates.
4. **Follow-up** (at frequent intervals) — periodic medical examination; review of physician & hospital records; mailed questionnaires; telephone calls; periodic home visits; routine surveillance of death records.
5. **Analysis** — incidence rate of outcome among exposed & non-exposed; estimation of risk (relative risk, attributable risk).

### Measures of morbidity
**Incidence rate · Prevalence rate · Attack rate · Secondary attack rate (SAR) · Disease notification.**
*(Full descriptions in Q7 / Q9.)*

> 🎯 Cohort steps = **Select subjects → Exposure data → Comparison group → Follow-up → Analysis → "SE-C-F-A".**

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 78–80 (morbidity), pp. 87–89 (cohort)

<summary><b>Q9. Define incidence &amp; prevalence rate. What are the special incidence rates? (S9,Q2)</b></summary>

### Definitions
**Incidence rate** = No. of **NEW cases** in a defined population during a specified period (× 1,000).
**Prevalence rate** = No. of **ALL current cases (old + new)** at a point in time / over a period, in a given population (× 100).

- **Point prevalence** = all current cases existing **at one point in time**.
- **Period prevalence** = all current cases existing **during a defined period of time**.

### Types / relationship
- Prevalence depends on **2 factors: incidence + duration of illness**.
- **P = I × D** (Prevalence = Incidence × mean duration) when population is stable.

### Special incidence rates
1. **Attack rate (case rate)** — proportion of persons who develop a disease **among those exposed to risk during an outbreak/epidemic** in a defined period. Widely used in the study of **common-source epidemics** (e.g., food poisoning).
2. **Secondary attack rate (SAR)** — the number of exposed persons developing the disease **within the range of the incubation period after exposure to a primary case**, ÷ the total number of exposed/susceptible contacts. Measures the **spread of infection in a family/closed group** (e.g., measles, chickenpox in households).
3. **Hospital admission rate** — use of hospital admission statistics as an index of disease.

> 🎯 Attack rate = **outbreak index** · SAR = **spread within family/family contacts** — both are special **incidence** rates.

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 79–80

<summary><b>Q10. Importance of incidence and prevalence. (S10,Q2)</b></summary>

### Uses / importance of incidence
1. **To control disease** (identify outbreaks, evaluate control measures).
2. **For research into:**
   - **Etiology & pathogenesis** of disease
   - The **distribution of diseases**
   - The **efficacy of preventive & therapeutic measures**
3. Suitable for **formulation and testing of aetiological hypotheses** (etiological/causative factor identification).

### Uses / importance of prevalence
1. Helps to **estimate the magnitude** of the health/disease problem in the community and **identify potential high-risk populations**.
2. Especially useful for **administrative & planning purposes** — e.g., hospital beds, manpower needs, rehabilitation facilities.
3. (Point prevalence is **not an ideal measure** for studying disease aetiology or causation.)

> 🎯 Incidence = **"NEW risk, cause, control"** · Prevalence = **"magnitude of the problem + planning (beds, manpower)."**

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 79–80

<summary><b>Q11. Classify epidemiological studies. Difference between case control &amp; cohort study. (S11,Q2)</b></summary>

### Classification of epidemiological methods / studies
**A) Observational studies:**
- **Descriptive studies** (population/group as unit)
- **Analytical studies:**
  - Ecological or correlational — **population** as unit
  - **Cross-sectional (prevalence)** — individuals as unit
  - **Case-control (case reference)** — individuals as unit
  - **Cohort (follow-up)** — individuals as unit

**B) Experimental (intervention) studies:**
- **Randomized (clinical) trials** — patients as unit
- **Field trials / community intervention studies** — healthy people as unit
- **Community trials** — communities as unit

### Differences: case-control vs cohort study
| Feature | Case-control study | Cohort study |
|---|---|---|
| **Direction** | **Effect to cause** (retrospective, backwards) | **Cause to effect** (prospective, forward) |
| **Starts with** | Disease | People exposed to risk factors / suspected cause |
| **Hypothesis** | Usually the **first approach** to test a hypothesis | Used for **testing a previously formulated hypothesis** |
| **Number of subjects** | Small | Larger |
| **Time / result** | Quick results (short) | Long duration (delayed results) |
| **Yielding / measures** | Odds ratio | Incidence rates, **relative risk (RR) & attributable risk (AR)** |
| **Study of rare disease** | Suitable | Unsuitable |
| **Information about disease** | Only one outcome | More than one disease outcome |
| **Cost** | Relatively inexpensive | **Expensive** |

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 81 (classification), pp. 90–91 (difference)

<summary><b>Q12. What is descriptive epidemiology? Procedure used in descriptive studies. (S12,Q2)</b></summary>

### Descriptive epidemiology
> 🔴 Descriptive studies are concerned with **observing the distribution of disease or health-related characteristics in human populations** and **identifying the characteristics with which the disease seems to be associated.**

It answers 3 basic questions:
- **When** is the disease occurring? → **Time** distribution
- **Where** is it occurring? → **Place** distribution
- **Who** is getting the disease? → **Person** distribution

**Uses:** magnitude of the problem · clues to aetiology & hypothesis formulation · background data for planning/organizing/evaluating preventive-curative services · describe variation by time, place & person.

### Procedure / steps of a descriptive study
1. **Defining the population to be studied** — number, age, sex, occupation, religion, cultural character, etc.
2. **Defining the disease under study** — operational definition by which the disease can be identified & measured accurately.
3. **Describing the disease by:**
   - **Time:** diarrhoea (rainy season), respiratory infection (winter), measles (winter, early spring), food poisoning (summer), motor car accident (weekend).
   - **Place:** Ca stomach (Japan), heart disease (developed countries), goitre (North Bengal), malaria (hill tracts).
   - **Person — age:** measles (childhood), cancer (middle age), atherosclerosis (old age). **Sex:** lung/prostate Ca (male); breast Ca, gall stones (female). **Occupation:** silicosis (coal miners). **Social:** upper class → CHD, HTN, DM; lower class → diarrhoea, TB, tetanus. **Behaviour:** smoking → CHD, Ca lung.
4. **Measurement of disease** — mortality + morbidity rates; prevalence from cross-sectional, incidence from longitudinal studies.
5. **Comparing with known indices** — comparisons between populations/subgroups give clues to aetiology.
6. **Formulation of a hypothesis.**

> 🎯 Steps = **Population → Disease definition → Describe (T-P-P) → Measure → Compare → Hypothesis.**

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 81–83

### Carrier (definition)
> 🔴 **Carrier** = "an **infected person or animal that harbours a specific infectious agent in the absence of discernible clinical disease** and serves as a **potential source of infection for others**."

- **Classic example:** **"Typhoid Mary"** — a famous typhoid carrier who continued to infect others for years.

### 3 criteria of carrier state
1. Presence in the body of the **disease agent**.
2. **Absence of recognizable symptoms & signs** of the disease.
3. **Shedding of the disease agent** in discharges/excretions (acting as a source of infection for others).

<summary><b>Q14. Categorize place variation in disease occurrences. What are the advantages of case-control study? (S14,Q2)</b></summary>

### Place (geographical) variation in disease occurrence
Different diseases predominate in **different places**:

| Place | Disease |
|---|---|
| **Japan** | Carcinoma stomach |
| **Developed countries** | Heart disease |
| **North Bengal** | Goitre |
| **Hill tracts** | Malaria |

**Geographic distinction:** *International* variation (between countries) vs *Regional/national* variation (within country — urban vs rural, hill tracts vs plains) — used to compare and obtain **clues to disease aetiology**.

### Advantages / merits of case-control study
1. Relatively **easy to carry out**.
2. **Rapid and inexpensive.**
3. Requires **comparatively few subjects**.
4. Particularly suitable to investigate **rare diseases**.
5. **No risk to subjects.**
6. Allows study of **several aetiological factors** (e.g., smoking) simultaneously.
7. **Risk factors can be identified.**
8. **No attrition problems** (no follow-up into the future).
9. **Ethical problems minimal.**

> 🎯 Case-control advantages = **quick, cheap, few subjects, rare diseases, no risk, no attrition — "Q-C-F-R-N-N".**

<summary><b>Q15. Name the international quarantinable disease and notifiable diseases under surveillance? (S14,Q7), (S25,Q7)</b></summary>

### International quarantinable diseases
> Under international health regulations, persons from infected places at ports are subject to quarantine. **International quarantinable diseases (IHR):**
1. **Cholera** — incubation 1–5 days (≈3 days)
2. **Plague** — bubonic 2–7 days; septicaemic 2–7 days; **pneumonic 1–3 days**
3. **Yellow fever** — 3–6 days

The IHR (1969) applied only to these **three** diseases; revised IHR (2005) broadened the scope to any public-health risk (e.g., SARS, ebola, novel influenza).

### Notifiable diseases under surveillance
**Diseases under surveillance by WHO:**
1. **Louse-borne typhus fever**
2. **Relapsing fever**
3. **Paralytic polio**
4. **Malaria**
5. **Viral influenza-A**
6. **SARS**
7. **Smallpox**

> 🎯 International quarantine = **"P-Y-C" (Plague · Yellow fever · Cholera).** International surveillance = **typhus, relapsing fever, polio, malaria, influenza-A, SARS, smallpox.**

> 📖 Ch-05 (Communicable Diseases), Endeavour PDF p. 119–120 (notifiable) · pp. 109 (international quarantine) · Ch-02 p. 62 (diseases under surveillance) · Ch-23 pp. 663–664 (IHR)

<summary><b>Q16. What are steps of conducting case control study (S15,Q2)</b></summary>

### Basic steps of a case-control study (4)
1. **Selection of cases and controls**
   - **Case selection:** define diagnostic criteria + eligibility criteria (only **newly diagnosed** cases); sources — hospitals, general population.
   - **Control selection:** free from the disease under study, similar to cases except for absence of disease; sources — **hospital controls, relatives, neighbourhood controls, general population.**
2. **Matching** — process by which controls are selected to be similar to cases for certain variables (e.g., **age**, sex).
3. **Measurement of exposure** — by interviews, questionnaires, review of past records (hospital records, employment records, etc.).
4. **Analysis & interpretation**
   - Exposure rate among **cases and controls** to the suspected factor.
   - Estimation of **disease risk associated with exposure** (odds ratio).

> 🎯 Case-control steps = **Selection → Matching → Exposure measurement → Analysis — "S-M-E-A".**

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 85–87

<summary><b>Q17. How can you measure disease load in a community. Disadvantages of case control study (S16,Q2)</b></summary>

### Measuring disease load in the community
Disease frequency ("load") is expressed as **rates & ratios**:
- **Morbidity:** incidence rate · prevalence rate · attack rate · secondary attack rate.
- **Mortality:** crude death rate · specific death rates · case fatality rate · proportional mortality rate · survival rate.
- **Rates** express the frequency of a health event in a population at risk over time and allow **comparison between populations**.

### Disadvantages / demerits of case-control study
1. **Problems of bias** (selection, recall/memory bias, interviewer's bias, Berksonian bias, confounding).
2. **Selection of an appropriate control group may be difficult.**
3. **Cannot measure incidence** — can only estimate the relative risk (odds ratio).
4. Does **not distinguish between causes and associated factors**.
5. Selection of comparison groups is a limiting factor.

> 🎯 Disease load = **morbidity rates + mortality rates.** Case-control demerits = **bias, control-group difficulty, no incidence, no causation.**

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 76–80 (measurement), p. 87 (case-control demerits)

<summary><b>Q18. What do you mean by cross sectional and longitudinal studies. list the advantages of cohort study (S17,Q2)</b></summary>

### Cross-sectional study
> Simplest form of observational study — based on a **single examination of a cross-section of the population at one point in time**; results can be projected to the whole population if sampling is correct. Also known as **"prevalence study."**

- **Advantages:** simplest · single observation · less time-consuming · less expensive · useful for chronic diseases · free of selection bias.
- **Disadvantages:** little information about natural history & incidence (rate of new cases).

### Longitudinal study
> **Observations are repeated in the same population over a prolonged period of time** by follow-up examinations.

- **Uses:** natural history of disease · both acute & chronic diseases · identifying risk factors · **finding incidence rate** (new cases).
- **Disadvantages:** difficult to organize · more time-consuming.

### Advantages / merits of cohort study
1. **Incidence can be calculated.**
2. Several possible **outcomes can be studied simultaneously.**
3. Provides a **direct estimate of relative risk**.
4. **Dose-response ratios** can be calculated.
5. **Chance of bias is minimum** (comparison groups formed before disease develops).

> 🎯 Cross-sectional = **one snapshot (prevalence study)** · Longitudinal = **repeated follow-up (incidence) · Cohort = incidence, RR, dose-response, min bias.**

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 83–84 (cross-sectional & longitudinal), p. 89 (cohort merits)

### Experimental studies (enumerate)
1. **Randomized controlled trials (RCT)** — clinical trials, preventive trials, risk-factor trials, cessation experiments, trial of aetiological agents, evaluation of health services.
2. **Non-randomized trials (non-experimental trials)** — uncontrolled trials · natural experiments · before-&-after comparison studies.

*Aims of experimental studies:* scientific proof of etiological factors + measuring effectiveness/efficiency of health services.

### Basic steps of RCT (6)
1. **Drawing up a protocol.**
2. **Selecting reference and experimental population.**
3. **Randomization** (heart of the trial).
4. **Manipulation / intervention.**
5. **Follow-up.**
6. **Assessment of outcome.**

**RCT definition:** clinical trial comparing effects of intervention in an **experimental group** against a **control group** following **random allocation** of participants.

> 🎯 RCT steps = **Protocol → Population → Randomize → Manipulate → Follow-up → Assess — "P-P-R-M-F-A".**

<summary><b>Q20. What are the types of analytical studies. enumerate the difference between cohort and case control study (S20,Q2)</b></summary>

### Types of analytical studies
Analytical studies test hypotheses (individuals are the unit of study). **They comprise 2 distinct types of observational studies:**
1. **Case-control study (retrospective)** — proceeds from **effect to cause**.
2. **Cohort study (prospective)** — proceeds from **cause to effect**.

(Also grouped under analytical: **ecological/correlational, cross-sectional**) — but the two classical analytical designs are case-control & cohort.

### Difference between cohort & case-control study
| Feature | Case-control study | Cohort study |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | **Effect → cause** (retrospective) | **Cause → effect** (prospective) |
| Starts with | Disease (cases with outcome) | People exposed to risk factors |
| Purpose | First approach to test a hypothesis | Tests previously formulated hypothesis |
| Subjects | Small | Larger |
| Result | Quick | Delayed |
| Measures | Odds ratio | Incidence, RR, AR |
| Rare disease | Suitable | Unsuitable |
| Cost | Inexpensive | Expensive |

> 🎯 Analytical = **Case-control (retrospective, effect→cause) + Cohort (prospective, cause→effect).**

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 84–85, 90–91

<summary><b>Q21. Define cohort. what are the steps in cohort studies. list its disadvantages? (S21,Q2)</b></summary>

### Cohort (definition)
> A **cohort** = a group of people who **share a common characteristic or experience within a defined time period** (age, occupation, exposure to a drug/vaccine, pregnancy...). Synonyms of cohort study: **prospective, longitudinal, incidence, forward-looking study.** It proceeds from **cause to effect.**

*(Examples: birth cohort, marriage cohort, exposure cohort.)*

### Steps of cohort study (5)
1. **Selection of study subjects** (general population or special groups).
2. **Obtaining data on exposure** (interview/questionnaire, records, medical exam, environmental surveys).
3. **Selection of comparison groups** (internal, external, general population rates).
4. **Follow-up** (periodic medical exam, records review, questionnaires, phone calls, home visits, death-record surveillance).
5. **Analysis** (incidence rates in exposed & non-exposed; estimation of risk).

### Disadvantages / demerits of cohort study
1. Involves a **large number of people**; unsuitable for **uncommon diseases**.
2. Takes a **long time** to complete.
3. **Administrative problems** — loss of experienced staff, loss of funding, extensive record keeping.
4. **Loss of a substantial proportion of the original cohort** (attrition).
5. Selection of the comparison group is a **limiting factor**.
6. Changes may occur in the **standard diagnostic criteria** of disease over long follow-up.
7. **Expensive.**
8. The study group may **change its life style**.

> 🎯 Cohort demerits = **expensive, slow, big sample, loss to follow-up, unsuitable for rare disease.**

> 📖 Ch-03, Endeavour PDF pp. 87–89

<summary><b>Q23. Define and classify epidemic. what are the steps in investigation an epidemic outbreak? (S23,Q2)</b></summary>

### Epidemic — definition & classification
> Epidemic (Epi = upon, demos = people) = **unusual occurrence** of disease/health-related behaviour/events **in excess of the expected** in a community or region.

**Classification (3 types):**
1. **Common-source epidemics** — single exposure/point source (food poisoning, Bhopal gas tragedy) or continuous/multiple exposure (Legionnaires' disease, gonorrhoea).
2. **Propagated epidemics** — person-to-person (hepatitis A, polio), arthropod vector, animal reservoir.
3. **Slow epidemics** — e.g., cancer.

### Steps of investigation of an epidemic outbreak
1. **Verification of diagnosis** (laboratory tests, clinical criteria, epidemiological diagnosis).
2. **Confirmation of the existence of an epidemic** (check records & seasonal incidence).
3. **Defining the population at risk** (map of the area, count the population).
4. **Rapid search for all cases & their characteristics** (medical survey, epidemiological case sheet, searching for more cases).
5. **Data analysis** by classical epidemiological parameters — **time, place, person.**
6. **Formulation of hypothesis.**
7. **Testing of hypotheses** + evaluation of ecological factors.
8. **Further investigation of the population at risk** (medical examination, screening tests, examination of suspected food/faeces/blood, biochemical studies).
9. **Writing the report** — background, historical data, methodology, analysis of data, control measures.

**Control component (parallel):** isolation & treatment of cases, attack on source of transmission → recommendations to prevent recurrence → continue surveillance.

> 🎯 Investigation = **Diagnosis → Confirm → Population at risk → Find cases → Analyze (T-P-P) → Hypothesis → Test → Report — "D-C-P-F-A-H-T-R".**

<summary><b>Q24. What is carrier? classify the carriers with example. mention the epidemiological importance of carrier. (S24,Q2)</b></summary>

### Carrier (definition)
> An infected person/animal that harbours a specific infectious agent **in the absence of discernible clinical disease** and serves as a potential source of infection for others. **Example:** "Typhoid Mary."

### Classification of carriers
**A) According to type:**
| Type | Time of shedding | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Incubatory carrier** | During the **incubation period** (last few days) | Measles, mumps, polio, pertussis |
| **Convalescent carrier** | During **convalescence** (after clinical recovery) | Typhoid, dysentery, cholera, diphtheria, whooping cough |
| **Healthy carrier** | Sub-clinical infection, never had overt disease | Polio, cholera, salmonellosis, diphtheria |

**B) According to duration:**
1. **Temporary carriers** — shed for short periods (include incubatory, convalescent, healthy).
2. **Chronic carriers** — shed for **indefinite period** (typhoid, hepatitis B, dysentery, malaria).

**C) According to portal of exit:** urinary, intestinal, respiratory, nasal, skin eruptions, open wounds, blood.

### Epidemiological importance of carriers
- Carriers are **less infectious than cases**, but are **more dangerous epidemiologically** because:
  1. They **escape recognition** (no clinical disease).
  2. They **live a normal life among the population**.
  3. They spread the infection **over a wider area and longer period** and readily infect **susceptible individuals under favourable conditions** (e.g., "Typhoid Mary").

> 🎯 **Incubatory (before) · Convalescent (after) · Healthy (never sick) · Chronic (indefinite) — the typhoid-Mary danger!**

<summary><b>Q27. Define isolation and quarantine. (S28,Q2)</b></summary>

### Isolation
> 🔴 **Isolation** = "separation, **for the period of communicability, of infected persons or animals** from others, in such places & under such conditions, as to prevent or limit the direct or indirect transmission of the infectious agent from those infected to those who are susceptible."

- **Oldest communicable disease control measure.**
- **Purpose:** to protect the community by preventing transfer of infection from reservoir to susceptible hosts; distinctive value in **diphtheria, cholera, streptococcal respiratory disease, pneumonic plague.**
- **Types:** standard · strict · protective · high-security isolation.
- **Ring isolation** — infected person surrounded by immunized persons (e.g., smallpox, measles).
- **No value in:** polio, hepatitis A, typhoid, mumps, leprosy, TB, STD.

### Quarantine
> 🔴 **Quarantine** = "the **limitation of freedom of movement of such WELL persons or domestic animals exposed to communicable disease**, for a period of time **not longer than the longest usual incubation period** of the disease, in such a manner as to prevent effective contact with those not so exposed."

**Types:**
1. **Absolute quarantine** — full restriction.
2. **Modified quarantine** — selective partial limitation (e.g., exclusion of children from school).
3. **Segregation** — removal of susceptible children to homes of immune persons.

### Difference: Isolation vs Quarantine
| Feature | Isolation | Quarantine |
|---|---|---|
| **Applied to** | **Infected persons** | **Well persons (healthy contacts)** of infectious disease |
| **Purpose** | Limit spread of disease in the community | Prevent transport of infection across national boundaries |
| **Period** | As long as the disease remains **communicable** | **Longest usual incubation period** of that disease |
| **Method** | Popular | Out-dated |

> 🎯 **Isolation = infected (till no longer communicable) · Quarantine = healthy contacts (till longest incubation period over).**

> 📖 Ch-05 (Communicable Diseases), Endeavour PDF pp. 120–122

<summary><b>Q28. What do you mean by control, elimination and eradication? Give example of each (S29,Q2)</b></summary>

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Disease control** | Reducing the **incidence, duration** (and thus risk of transmission), **effects** of infection, and **financial burden** — the disease process is **kept active but at a low level**; the disease persists in the community | **Malaria control** (WHO); control of TB, diarrhoea, ARI |
| **Disease elimination** | **Interruption of transmission** of the disease from a large geographic region/area (infection persists, but no transmission) — **"biting the chain of transmission"** | **Elimination of measles, polio and diphtheria** from large regions; in BD: **elimination of neonatal tetanus & filaria** |
| **Disease eradication** | **Termination of all transmission of infection by extermination of the infectious agent** (through surveillance & containment) — disease completely wiped out worldwide ("tear out by roots") | **Smallpox** — the only disease eradicated so far (1980); global polio eradication (ongoing) |

> 🎯 **Control (keep it low) → Elimination (no transmission in a region) → Eradication (agent dead worldwide).** 🎯 Smallpox = the **only** eradicated disease!
