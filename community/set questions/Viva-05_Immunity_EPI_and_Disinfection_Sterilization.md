<summary><b>Q1. Define immunity. Classify immunity with example? (S1,Q3)</b></summary>

### Immunity (definition)
> 🔴 **Immunity** = the **ability of the body to recognize, destroy and eliminate antigenic material** (e.g., bacteria, virus) **foreign to its own.**

### Classification of immunity (with examples)
**A) Innate (non-specific) immunity** — present from birth:
| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **1. Genetic / constitutional** | Species, race, individual resistance |
| **2. Mechanical** | Keratin layer of skin · intact mucous membrane · mucociliary movement · reflexes (coughing, sneezing) |
| **3. Humoral** | Normal bacterial flora · acid in gastric juice · complement system · interferons |
| **4. Cellular** | Macrophage · eosinophil · natural killer (NK) cells |

**B) Acquired (specific) immunity:**
| Type | Natural | Artificial |
|---|---|---|
| **Active** (antigen exposed to body → body produces Ab/T-cells) | After clinical & sub-clinical infection (e.g., hepatitis-A virus infection) | Different types of **vaccines** (bacterial, viral, toxoids, live attenuated) |
| **Passive** (antigen NOT exposed; ready-made Ab transferred) | Transfer of maternal antibody to foetus **through placenta**; transfer via **breast milk (IgA)** | **Antisera & antitoxins** — TIG (tetanus Ig), ATS, ADS |

> 🎯 **Innate (born with it) vs Acquired (developed later) — Acquired = Active (make your own Ab) + Passive (receive ready-made Ab).**

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 403–404

<summary><b>Q2. What are the types of non-specific immunity. How active and passive immunity develop in human? (S2,Q3)</b></summary>

### Types of non-specific (innate) immunity
1. **Genetic / constitutional.**
2. **Mechanical:** keratin layer of skin; intact mucous membrane; mucociliary movement; reflexes (coughing, sneezing).
3. **Humoral:** normal bacterial flora; acid in gastric juice; complement system; interferons.
4. **Cellular:** macrophages; eosinophils; natural killer (NK) cells.

### Development of active immunity (host produces antibodies)
Acquired by:
1. Following **clinical infection** — e.g., chickenpox, rubella, measles.
2. Following **sub-clinical / inapparent infection** — e.g., polio, diphtheria.
3. Following **immunization (vaccination)** with an antigen — a **killed vaccine**, a **live attenuated vaccine**, or a **toxoid**.

### Development of passive immunity (ready-made antibodies transferred)
Developed by:
1. Administration of antibody-containing preparations (**immunoglobulin or antiserum**).
2. **Transfer of maternal antibodies across the placenta.**
3. Transfer of maternal antibodies (**IgA**) via **colostrum / breast milk**.
4. Transfer of lymphocytes → passive cellular immunity.

> 🎯 Active = **"infection or vaccination → your own Ab"** · Passive = **"Ab made in another body → given/transferred to you."**

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 404–405

<summary><b>Q3. What is cell mediated and humoral immunity. How active immunity differs from passive immunity? (S3,Q3)</b></summary>

### Cell-mediated immunity (CMI)
> Immunity mediated by **T-lymphocytes** — developed when antigen is processed by macrophages and presented to helper T-cells (→ delayed hypersensitivity). Principal in **intracellular** infections (TB, viral infections).

**Constituents of CMI:**
1. Macrophage
2. Helper T-cell
3. Cytotoxic T-cell
4. Natural killer (NK) cells
5. ADCC (antibody dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity)

### Humoral immunity
> Immunity mediated by **circulating antibodies (immunoglobulins) produced by plasma cells (B-lymphocytes)** — principal against **extracellular / bacterial** infections.

**Mechanism:** antigen → APC (macrophage)→ helper T-cell → B-cell activation → plasma cells → **antibody production**.

### Difference: Active vs Passive immunity
| Trait | Active immunity | Passive immunity |
|---|---|---|
| **Active participation by host** | Yes | No |
| **Onset of action** | After a **considerable latent period** | **Immediate / short** |
| **Duration** | **Long-lasting** | Short-lasting |
| **Immunological memory** | Present | Absent |
| **Secondary immune response** | Occurs | Does not occur |
| **Risk of hypersensitivity (type-I & II)** | No risk | Risk present |
| **Use** | **Prevention** of disease | Prevention **& treatment** of disease |

> 🎯 Active = **slow, long, memory, no risk** · Passive = **quick, short, no memory, hypersensitivity risk (serum sickness!).**

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 404–406

<summary><b>Q4. Define antigen and antibody. What are different type of immunoglobulin. What is toxin and toxoid? (S4,Q3)</b></summary>

### Antigen
> An **antigen** is a substance (protein or polysaccharide in nature) which, when introduced into the body, is **capable of inducing an immune response** leading to formation of **antibodies** with which it reacts **specifically**.

### Antibody
> **Antibodies** are **immunoglobulins that react specifically with the antigens that stimulate their production.** Antibodies are — **IgG, IgM, IgD, IgA, IgE.**

### Types & functions of immunoglobulins
| Ig | Key functions |
|---|---|
| **IgG** | **Crosses the placenta** (natural passive immunity in newborn) · neutralizes bacterial toxin & virus · complement activation · most effective antibody in **secondary response** · very effective complement fixator & agglutinator |
| **IgM** | Produces **early immune response** · effective **first line of defense** · largest molecule (primary response antibody) |
| **IgA** | **Primary defense at mucous membranes** against local infection (secretory IgA of breast milk) |
| **IgD** | Present on B-cell surface (acts as a receptor) · involved in lymphocytic differentiation |
| **IgE** | Mediates **type-I hypersensitivity (anaphylactic)** reaction · defense against **parasitic infections** |

### Toxin
> A **poisonous product formed by microorganisms** (e.g., tetanus exotoxin, diphtheria exotoxin) — has **toxigenicity**; when inactivated loses toxicity but keeps antigenicity.

### Toxoid
> Vaccine prepared from **toxin by inactivation (combining formaldehyde with toxin)** — **has no toxigenicity but retains antigenicity.**
> **Examples:** Tetanus toxoid (TT), Diphtheria toxoid (DT).

> 🎯 **IgG crosses placenta · IgM = first line · IgA = mucosal · IgE = allergy & parasites · IgD = B-cell receptor. Toxoid = toxin made harmless but still antigenic!**

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 405–406, 412

<summary><b>Q5. Differentiate toxoid, toxin and antisera? (S6,Q2)</b></summary>

| Feature | Toxin | Toxoid | Antisera / antitoxin |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | **Poisonous product of microorganism** (e.g., tetanus, diphtheria exotoxin) | **Toxin inactivated with formaldehyde** — no toxigenicity, retains antigenicity | **Antibody-containing material prepared in animals** (e.g., horses) |
| **Nature** | Antigenic + toxic | Antigenic but **non-toxic** | Contains **immunoglobulins (antibodies)** against the toxin |
| **Use** | Not used directly | As a **vaccine (active immunization)** — e.g., TT, DT | **Passive immunization / treatment** (e.g., ATS, ADS, TIG) |
| **Adverse effects** | — | Vaccine reactions | **Serum sickness, anaphylactic reaction** |
| **Contraindication** | — | Some contraindications in pregnancy | **No contraindication in pregnancy** |

> 🎯 **Toxin (toxic) → Toxoid (toxic removed, kept as vaccine) → Antisera (antibody against it, given passively for treatment).**

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 410, 412

<summary><b>Q6. Define herd immunity. How is it developed in human population. State the epidemiological significance of herd immunity. (S7,Q3) · also (S20,Q3) · (S27,Q3)</b></summary>

### Herd immunity (definition)
> 🔴 **Herd immunity** = the **level of resistance of a community (herd) or group of people to a particular disease**; i.e., **group protection beyond that afforded to immunized individuals themselves**, by virtue of the herd structure (reduced transmission among individuals).

### How it is developed / achieved
1. **Immunization** of the herd (on-going immunization programme).
2. **Clinical & sub-clinical infection** in the herd.
3. **Elimination of insect vector.**
4. **Elimination of animal reservoir.**
5. Provision of **safe water supply.**
6. **Sanitary disposal of refuse & sewage.**
7. Provision of **proper nutrition.**

*(Determination of herd immunity — by **serological surveys**.)*

**Elements contributing:** occurrence of clinical/sub-clinical infection; immunization; **herd structure** (host species, alternative animal hosts, insect vectors, environmental/social factors). Influencing factors of herd structure: **new births, deaths, population mobility.**

### Epidemiological significance
1. Provides an **immunological barrier** to spread of disease in the herd.
2. If sufficiently high, an **epidemic is highly unlikely**.
3. If maintained at high level → may lead to **elimination of the disease** (e.g., diphtheria, poliomyelitis).
4. **Epidemic curve tends to decline** with buildup of herd immunity.
5. It is **neither possible nor necessary to achieve 100%** herd immunity to halt an epidemic/control disease.

> 🎯 **Herd immunity = community shield via immunization + herd structure.** 🔴 **100% not needed.** Diseases: polio & diphtheria eradicable by herd immunity; **tetanus NOT protected by herd immunity.** Maintained by **on-going immunization.**

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 407–408

<summary><b>Q7. What is immunization. Define and classify vaccines with examples. Why live vaccine is more potent than killed vaccine? (S8,Q3)</b></summary>

### Immunization
> A technique by which **immunizing agents are introduced into the body for the production of antibodies** to prevent disease.

**Classification:** 1) **Active immunization → Vaccine.** 2) **Passive immunization → Immunoglobulin, Antisera.**

### Vaccine (definition)
> An **immuno-biological substance designed to produce specific protection against a given disease** — stimulates production of protective antibodies and other immune mechanisms.

### Classification of vaccines (with examples)
1. **Live attenuated vaccines:** BCG (TB) · oral polio · yellow fever · measles · rubella · mumps · influenza · epidemic typhus (rickettsial).
2. **Inactivated (killed) vaccines:** pertussis · cholera · typhoid · plague · **Salk (IPV)** · rabies · influenza · **hepatitis B**.
3. **Toxoids:** **Tetanus toxoid** (tetanus) · **Diphtheria toxoid** (diphtheria).
4. **Extracted cellular fractions:** meningococcal · pneumococcal vaccine.
5. **Combination vaccines:** DPT · MMR · DT · DP · DPT+typhoid · DPTP.
   *(Newer: subunit & recombinant vaccines.)*

### Why live vaccine is more potent than killed vaccine
Live vaccines are more potent because:
1. **Live organisms multiply in the host** → resulting antigenic dose is **larger than what is injected**.
2. Live vaccines have **all major and minor antigenic components**.
3. Live vaccines **engage certain tissues** of the body.
4. Other mechanisms (persistence of latent virus).

> 🎯 Live = **multiply, more antigen, tissue engagement → one dose, lifelong/long duration.** Killed = multiple doses, shorter protection.

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 408–410

<summary><b>Q8. Name the diseases that are preventable by immunization. What are EPI and UCI. Why is it successful in this country? (S9,Q3)</b></summary>

### Diseases preventable by immunization
**6 classic EPI diseases:** Diphtheria · Pertussis (whooping cough) · Tetanus · Tuberculosis · Poliomyelitis · Measles *(+ Hepatitis-B, Haemophilus influenzae-b added → pentavalent).*

**Vaccine-preventable diseases other than EPI:** Cholera · plague · yellow fever · typhoid · rabies · mumps · rubella · chickenpox · rotaviral diarrhoea · cervical carcinoma (HPV).

### EPI (Expanded Programme on Immunization)
> WHO launched a **global immunization programme (May 1974 / officially May 1974, launched formally 1979)** to protect **all children of the world against six vaccine-preventable diseases — Tuberculosis, Diphtheria, Whooping cough, Tetanus, Poliomyelitis & Measles.** In 1992–93 **Hepatitis-B** was added.

### UCI (Universal Child Immunization)
> The EPI programme was reorganized and renamed as **UCI** — aim of **immunizing all children (under 1 year) against the EPI diseases** (global UCI goal 1990; adopted in BD as goal of **1985**).

### Why EPI is successful in Bangladesh
- Formally launched **7th April 1979**; government adopted the goal of UCI and developed national EPI policies.
- **Goals of UCI policy:** immunize all children under 1 year; immunize all women of child-bearing age including pregnant women; extend service delivery up to the community; involve community-level heath & FP workers as vaccinators; community participation.
- Achieving: **eradication of poliomyelitis, elimination of neonatal tetanus,** reduction of measles incidence by 90% & mortality by 95% — through >90% routine coverage, **NIDs**, supplementary immunization (MNT campaign), and AFP/EPI surveillance.
- Service delivery via **fixed centres (Govt/City corp/NGO)** + **outreach centres at village level**.

> 🎯 EPI diseases = **"T-D-W-T-P-M" (TB, Diphtheria, Whooping cough, Tetanus, Polio, Measles) + hepatitis-B/Hib.** Success keys = **NID + >90% coverage + outreach services.**

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 412–415

<summary><b>Q11. Give the immunization schedule for a woman of reproductive age. Why measles vaccine is given at the end of 9 month? (S12,Q3) · also (S23,Q3)</b></summary>

### TT (tetanus toxoid) schedule for women of reproductive age
| Dose | Minimum interval | Duration of protection |
|---|---|---|
| **TT-1** | At first contact | — |
| **TT-2** | 4 weeks after TT-1 | 3 years (from 15 days after TT-2) |
| **TT-3** | 6 months after TT-2 | 5 years (from 15 days after TT-3) |
| **TT-4** | 1 year after TT-3 | 10 years (from 15 days after TT-4) |
| **TT-5** | 1 year after TT-4 | **For life** |

**TT during pregnancy (if mother not immunized earlier):** 2 doses — 1st at **16–20 weeks**, 2nd at **20–24 weeks** of pregnancy; protects next 5 years. (Booster dose sufficient in previously immunized pregnant women.)

### Why measles vaccine is given at the end of 9 months
- Infants are protected by **maternal (maternally-transferred) antibodies up to ~6 months** (may persist beyond 9 months in some).
- Measles vaccine given **before 9 months would be neutralized/interfered by the maternal antibodies** → child may not develop immunity.
- Maternal antibodies start **declining from 6 months**, leaving the infant susceptible — hence 9 months is the optimal balance.
- (Also: **below 1 year, more deaths/severity** from measles → protect as early as possible after maternal Ab wanes.)

> 🎯 TT-5 = **"4 weeks, 6 mo, 1 yr, 1 yr" → protection 3 yr → 5 yr → 10 yr → LIFE.** Measles = **maternal Ab blocks vaccine before 9 months.**

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 418–419

<summary><b>Q12. What is combined vaccine. Give example and mention the usefulness of combined vaccine? (S13,Q3) · also (S24,Q3)</b></summary>

### Combined (mixed) vaccine
> If **more than one kind of immunizing agent is included in one vaccine**, it is called a **mixed or combined vaccine.**

*(Related terms: **monovalent** = one strain of a species; **polyvalent** = two or more strains of the same species.)*

### Examples
- **DPT** (Diphtheria + Pertussis + Tetanus)
- **MMR** (Measles + Mumps + Rubella)
- **Pentavalent** (DPT + Hepatitis-B + Hib)
- **MR** (Measles + Rubella)
- DT, DP, DPT+typhoid, DPTP (DPT + inactivated polio)

### Usefulness / advantages of combined vaccine
1. **Simplifies administration.**
2. **Reduces costs.**
3. **Minimizes the number of contacts** of the patient with the health system.
4. *(DPT specifically: 3 vaccines at a time, reduces cost, minimizes contacts, simplifies administration, may increase potency.)*

> 🎯 Combined vaccine = **one jab, many diseases → less cost, fewer contacts, simpler — but watch potency & reactions.**

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 411–412

<summary><b>Q13. What is booster dose. Mention the importance of booster dose. Name the Ig that crosses the placenta? (S14,Q3)</b></summary>

### Booster dose
> 🔴 **Booster dose** = an **additional dose of an already given vaccine, administered some time (months/years) after the primary series**, to **boost (restore/heighten) the immune response** when antibody level falls below the protective level.

*(Primary series primes the immune system; the booster dose produces a rapid **secondary (anamnestic) response** with higher, longer-lasting antibody titers.)*

### Importance of booster dose
1. **Restores waning antibody level** to protective range.
2. Produces a **rapid, higher and more prolonged (secondary) immune response**.
3. **Prevents breakthrough infection** and maintains herd protection.
4. Extends duration of protection (e.g., TT-2 → 3 years, TT-5 → lifelong).
5. Reduces disease severity if infection occurs.

*Examples: OPV booster, TT booster, DPT/DPT booster (18–24 months), MMR booster.*

### Ig that crosses the placenta
> 🔴 **IgG** — crosses the placenta and provides **natural passive immunity to the newborn.**

> 🎯 **Booster = "re-boost the waning Ab" (anamnestic response).** Placenta-crossing Ig = **IgG.**

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 406 (anamnestic/secondary response), 418 (TT schedule), 405–406 (IgG)

<summary><b>Q14. Define and classify immunity with example. What are the primary and secondary immune response? (S15,Q3)</b></summary>

### Immunity + classification
*(Definition & full classification with examples — see Q1.)*
**Innate (genetic, mechanical, humoral, cellular) + Acquired (Active: natural/artificial; Passive: natural/artificial).**

### Immune response (definition)
> **Immune response** = development of resistance to a foreign substance (antigen).

### Primary vs Secondary immune response
> **Primary:** response on the **first encounter** of a naive immunocompetent lymphocyte with an antigen.
> **Secondary:** response on a **second or subsequent contact** with the same antigen — more rapid & heightened (due to amplified **memory cells**).

| Feature | Primary response | Secondary response |
|---|---|---|
| **Latent period** | Longer (days) | Shorter (rapid) |
| **Rate of antibody production** | Slow | Rapid |
| **Peak antibody titer** | Lower | Higher |
| **Persistence of Ab titer** | Short | Long |
| **Affinity of antibody** | Low | High |
| **Predominant Ig class** | **IgM** | **IgG** |
| **Cross-reactive antibody** | — | Present |

> 🎯 **Primary = IgM, slow, low, short · Secondary = IgG, rapid, high, long (memory cells!).**

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 403–406

<summary><b>Q15. Define and classify immunity with example. List the hazards of vaccination? (S16,Q3)</b></summary>

### Immunity + classification
*(See Q1.)*

### Hazards of immunization / adverse effects following immunization (AEFI)
1. **Vaccine reactions** — local (pain, swelling, tenderness/redness, small nodules, sterile abscess) or **systemic** (fever, malaise, headache).
2. **Hypersensitivity reactions:**
   - **Anaphylactic shock** — bronchospasm, dyspnoea, pallor, hypotension, collapse.
   - **Serum sickness** (e.g., by ATS) — fever, rash, oedema, joint pain.
3. **Reactions due to programme error** — inadequate inactivation (no immunity), hepatitis-B, streptococcal/staphylococcal infection, local abscess.
4. **Coincidental events** — unrelated disease appears (vaccine shortens incubation period of harboured agent).
5. **Injection reactions** — fainting, hyperventilation, light-headedness, dizziness, tingling.
6. **Others** — damage to fetus (e.g., rubella vaccination in pregnancy); shift in age distribution of disease (measles/rubella/mumps mass vaccination).

**Contraindications to vaccination:** high fever · severe malnutrition · immunocompromised (AIDS, TB) · hypersensitivity. *(Live vaccines further contraindicated in: immunodeficiency, anaphylaxis, pregnancy, leukaemia/lymphoma, corticosteroid/radiation therapy.)*

> 🎯 AEFI = **local/systemic reaction + anaphylaxis + serum sickness + programme error + coincidence + injection reaction.** Live vaccine contraindicated in **pregnancy & immunodeficient.**

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 411, 413–414

<summary><b>Q20. What are vaccines used in EPI programme in our country? Enumerate the equipment essential to maintain cold chain effectively? (S21,Q3)</b></summary>

### Vaccines used in EPI (Bangladesh)
**BCG · OPV · Pentavalent (DPT+HBV+Hib) · Pneumococcal (PCV) · Measles · MR** *(see Q9).*

### Cold chain equipment (essential)
1. **Walk-in cold rooms (WIC)** — regional level; store vaccine up to 3 months; serve 4–5 districts.
2. **Deep freezers & Ice-lined refrigerators (ILR)** — make ice packs; **store OPV & measles vaccines.**
3. **Small deep freezers & ILR (140 L)** — prepare frozen ice packs for cold boxes/vaccine carriers.
4. **Cold boxes** — mainly for **transporting** vaccines; keep cold up to **1 week** without power.
5. **Vaccine carriers** — carry small quantities; safe up to **3 days**.
6. **Day carriers / flasks** — for a few hours (field sessions).
7. **Ice packs.**

**Cold chain at different levels:** Manufacturer → airport → **EPI HQ cold room** → **District (ILR & deep freeze)** → **Thana (ILR ~1 month)** → **Vaccine carrier → field (8-hour storage)** → **use. Transported between 2–8°C.**

<summary><b>Q23. Define and state the process of disinfection for cough and sputum of TB patient. Give example and mention the usefulness of combined vaccine? (S24,Q3)</b></summary>

### Disinfection (definition)
> Disinfection = **process of reduction of the number of contaminating organisms to a level that cannot cause infection** (some organisms & bacterial spores may survive) — unlike **sterilization** (absolute removal of all microbes including spores).

### Disinfection of sputum & cough of a TB patient
> 🔴 **Sputum** is the main source of infection in pulmonary TB (**open/smear-positive case**).
1. **Sputum collection:** collect in a **sputum pot/mug** (preferably containing a disinfectant — e.g., **cresol**) and keep covered.
2. **Disposal / disinfection of sputum:**
   - **Burning** the sputum (paper cups burnt) or of the collected sputum.
   - **Boiling** in water for 15–30 minutes.
   - Adding **bleaching powder / cresol** and leaving for a suitable contact time before disposal.
   - **Deep burial** of the sputum.
3. **Cough hygiene:** the patient must **cover the mouth & nose with a handkerchief/cloth during coughing and sneezing**; avoid spitting on floor; the sputum should **never be dried** (dust → droplet-nuclei spread).
4. All **articles (linen, utensils, bed-sharing surfaces)** used by the patient should be disinfected (boiling, washing).

*(Reason: TB bacilli are killed by heat (boiling), burning, and chemical disinfectants; sputum left to dry becomes infective dust.)*

### Combined vaccine — example & usefulness
*(Full details Q12.)* **Example:** DPT, MMR, Pentavalent, MR. **Usefulness:** simplifies administration · reduces cost · minimizes contacts with health system · (DPT) 3 vaccines at a time.

> 🎯 TB sputum = **collect in covered sputum pot → burn/boil/bleaching powder → deep burial + cover cough → never let it dry!**

> 📖 Ch-13 (Disinfection & Sterilization), Endeavour PDF pp. 431, 436 · Ch-05 (TB / control measures) + Ch-12 pp. 411–412

<summary><b>Q27. Why live vaccine is more potent than killed vaccine. How cold chain is maintained from the production site to vaccination centre? (S28,Q3)</b></summary>

### Live vaccine more potent than killed
1. **Multiply in the host** → antigenic dose larger than injected.
2. Carry **all major & minor antigenic components.**
3. **Engage certain tissues** of the body.
4. Other mechanisms (latent virus persistence).

### Cold chain maintenance (production site → vaccination centre)
> **Cold chain** = a **system of storage and transport of vaccines at low temperature** from the manufacturer to the actual vaccination site — a series of storage & transport links designed to keep vaccines within an acceptable range (**2–8°C**, OPV & measles in freezer compartment).

**Link-wise maintenance:**
1. **Manufacturer → airport:** transported as soon as available.
2. **Airport → EPI head-quarter cold room (WIC):** transported **between 2–8°C** by refrigerated vehicles.
3. **WIC (regional level; stores up to 3 months)** → **District (ILR & deep freezer):** in **cold boxes** by vehicle.
4. **District → Thana (ILR, stores ~1 month):** in cold boxes.
5. **Thana → field level:** in **vaccine carriers with ice packs** (8 hours storage).
6. **At session:** day carriers/flasks → **use of vaccine.**

**Equipment:** walk-in cold rooms, deep freezers/ILR, cold boxes, vaccine carriers, day carriers, ice packs.
**Why maintained:** to **preserve vaccine potency**; avoid loss of efficacy (above 8°C, sunlight, freezing of DPT/TT, expiry, reconstituted vaccine kept beyond the day).

> 📖 Ch-12, Endeavour PDF pp. 408–410 (potency), pp. 420–422 (cold chain)
