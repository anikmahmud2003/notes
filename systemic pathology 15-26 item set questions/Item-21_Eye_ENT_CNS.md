# Part A — Eye: Retinoblastoma & Uveal Melanoma (Q1–2)

## Q1. Short note: Retinoblastoma.

### Definition
🔴 **Retinoblastoma = the most common primary intraocular malignancy of children.** Cell of origin = a **neuronal progenitor** (NOT a bipotential retinoblast). Accounts for ~3% of childhood cancers.

### Epidemiology
- Incidence: ~1 in 15,000–20,000 live births
- Peak age: **<3 years** (90% diagnosed before age 5)
- No sex predilection
- **Bilateral cases** = ~40% (almost all germline)

### Pathogenesis — Two-Hit Hypothesis (Knudson)

🔴 **RB1 gene (chromosome 13q14) = the first identified tumor suppressor gene.**

| Feature | Hereditary (Bilateral) | Sporadic (Unilateral) |
|---|---|---|
| First hit | **Germline RB1 mutation** (inherited or de novo) — present in all cells | Somatic RB1 mutation in one retinal cell |
| Second hit | Somatic loss of remaining allele (LOH) in retinal cell | Second somatic mutation in same cell |
| Laterality | **Bilateral** (~90%) | **Unilateral** (~90%) |
| Age of onset | **Earlier** (<1 yr) | **Later** (2–4 yr) |
| Family history | ~25% inherited from parent; 75% de novo germline | None |
| Pinealoblastoma | **Trilateral** retinoblastoma (pineal gland involvement) — dismal prognosis | Very rare |
| Other cancers | ↑ Risk of osteosarcoma, soft tissue sarcoma, melanoma throughout life | Not increased |

### Morphology

#### Gross
- **White-white/pinkish mass** filling the eye
- May grow endophytically (into vitreous) or exophytically (into subretinal space)
- **Focal dystrophic calcification** is characteristic (visible on imaging)

#### Microscopy — EXAM FAVORITE

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Small round blue cells** | Hyperchromatic nuclei, scant cytoplasm; high N:C ratio |
| **Flexner-Wintersteiner rosettes** | 🔴 Tumor cells arranged around a **central lumen** (photoreceptor differentiation); pathognomonic of retinoblastoma |
| **Fleurettes** | Fleurette-like structures = more differentiated photoreceptor elements; better differentiation |
| **Homer Wright rosettes** | Also present (less specific — seen in medulloblastoma, neuroblastoma) |
| **Perivascular cuffing** | Viable tumor cells encircle blood vessels with **zones of necrosis** beyond |
| **Necrosis** | Extensive; calcification within necrotic zones |
| **Dystrophic calcification** | 🔴 Characteristic; visible on CT scan — key diagnostic clue |
| **Choroidal invasion** | Important prognostic factor |
| **Optic nerve invasion** | 🔴 Worst prognostic sign — risk of CNS spread |

### Clinical Features
- 🔴 **Leukocoria** (white pupillary reflex) — most common presentation (~60%)
- **Strabismus** (misaligned eyes) — second most common (~20%)
- Glaucoma (from iris neovascularization)
- Proptosis (advanced disease)
- Hyphema (blood in anterior chamber)

### Spread
- **Local:** optic nerve → brain; choroid → hematogenous
- **Distant:** bone marrow, bone, lymph nodes (rare)
- 🔴 **Extraocular extension** = poor prognosis

### Treatment
- **Small tumors:** laser photocoagulation, cryotherapy, brachytherapy
- **Large tumors:** chemoreduction (vincristine, etoposide, carboplatin) → conservative surgery
- **Advanced:** enucleation; external beam radiation (controversial in germline cases → secondary malignancies)
- **Trilateral:** systemic chemotherapy + radiation

### Prognosis
- 🔴 **Overall survival: >95%** with modern treatment
- Unilateral: >95%
- Bilateral: >90%
- **Poor prognosis:** optic nerve invasion beyond lamina cribrosa, choroidal invasion >3 mm, extraocular extension, trilateral

> 🎯 "Retinoblastoma = RB1 two-hit; Flexner-Wintersteiner rosettes + dystrophic calcification; leukocoria = most common presentation; trilateral = bilateral + pinealoblastoma."

## Q2. Compare uveal melanoma and conjunctival melanoma.

### Uveal Melanoma

🔴 **Most common primary intraocular malignancy of adults** (~5% of all melanomas).

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Age/Sex** | 50–70 yr; slight female predominance |
| **Risk factors** | Light iris color, OCA2/HERC2, BAP1 germline mutations; **NO UV link** |
| **Genetics** | 🔴 **GNAQ/GNA11** (85%) → MAPK activation; **monosomy 3 → BAP1 loss** (worst prognosis); no BRAF mutations |
| **Morphology** | Spindle cells (fusiform, better prognosis) vs **epithelioid cells** (spherical, worse); **vasculogenic mimicry** (looping slit-like spaces) |
| **Metastasis** | 🔴 **Hematogenous → LIVER** (tumor-specific tropism); rare lymphatic spread |
| **Prognosis** | 5-yr survival ~80%; cumulative mortality 40% at 10 yr; **dormancy** (mets appear years later) |
| **Treatment** | Eye-sparing **radiotherapy** = standard; enucleation for large tumors; **no effective therapy for metastatic disease** |
| **Staging** | Lateral extent (NOT depth), cell type, monosomy 3, gene expression profiling |

### Conjunctival Melanoma

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Age/Sex** | 50–70 yr; fair complexion |
| **Precursor** | 🔴 **Primary acquired melanosis with atypia (PAM) / conjunctival melanocytic intraepithelial neoplasia (C-MIN)** — 50–90% progress if untreated |
| **Genetics** | **BRAF V600 in ~40%**; UV-related |
| **Morphology** | Similar to cutaneous melanoma; may arise from nevus or de novo |
| **Metastasis** | 🔴 **Lymphatic → parotid/submandibular nodes** |
| **Prognosis** | ~25% fatal |
| **Treatment** | Surgical excision + sentinel node biopsy; immunotherapy for advanced |

### Comparison Table — EXAM FAVORITE

| Feature | **Uveal Melanoma** | **Conjunctival Melanoma** |
|---|---|---|
| **Most common primary intraocular malignancy of adults** | **Yes** | No (conjunctival) |
| **Precursor** | Uveal nevus (rarely transforms) | **PAM with atypia / C-MIN** |
| **UV link / BRAF** | **None / no BRAF** | **UV-related; BRAF V600 ~40%** |
| **Driver genetics** | **GNAQ/GNA11, monosomy 3, BAP1** | **BRAF** |
| **Spread** | **Hematogenous → LIVER** | **Lymphatics → parotid/submandibular** |
| **Fatality** | ~40% at 10 yr | **~25% fatal** |
| **Prognostic factor** | Lateral extent (NOT depth) | Breslow depth, ulceration |

> 🎯 "Uveal = GNAQ/GNA11 + liver mets + no BRAF; Conjunctival = BRAF + PAM precursor + lymphatic spread. Uveal is the most common primary intraocular malignancy of adults."

# Part B — Eye: Retinal & Lens Pathology (Q3–5)

## Q3. Describe the pathogenesis of diabetic retinopathy.

### Definition
🔴 **Diabetic retinopathy = the most common cause of blindness in working-age adults (20–74 yr).** A progressive microangiopathy affecting retinal blood vessels.

### Pathogenesis — Step-by-Step

```
Chronic hyperglycemia (diabetes mellitus)
    ↓
NONENZYMATIC GLYCOSYLATION → Advanced glycation end products (AGEs)
    ↓
BASEMENT MEMBRANE THICKENING of retinal capillaries
    ↓
PERICYTE LOSS (earliest histologic change)
    ↓
CAPILLARY OCCLUSION → retinal nonperfusion → ISCHEMIA
    ↓
↑ VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor = "vascular permeability factor")
    ↓
BLOOD-RETINAL BARRIER BREAKDOWN → VASOGENIC EDEMA
    +
RETINAL NEOVASCULARIZATION (new vessels grow on disc/surface)
    ↓
NEOVASCULAR MEMBRANES breach INTERNAL LIMITING MEMBRANE
    ↓
Posterior vitreous detachment → TRACTION → VITREOUS HEMORRHAGE
    +
Fibrovascular contraction → TRACTIONAL RETINAL DETACHMENT
    +
Anterior synechiae → NEOVASCULAR GLAUCOMA
```

### Two Stages

| Stage | Features | Key Pathology |
|---|---|---|
| **Nonproliferative (NPDR)** | Microaneurysms, dot/blot hemorrhages, hard exudates, cotton-wool spots, **macular edema** (commonest cause of visual loss in diabetics), IRMA | BM thickening, pericyte loss, capillary nonperfusion |
| **Proliferative (PDR)** | **Retinal neovascularization** (disc or surface), vitreous hemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment, neovascular glaucoma | Neovascularization breaching the ILM |

### Key Pathologic Features

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Microaneurysms** | 🔴 Earliest clinically visible lesion; focal outpouchings of capillary walls |
| **Pericyte loss** | 🔴 Earliest HISTOLOGIC change |
| **BM thickening** | 🔴 Hallmark of diabetes in the eye; also seen in ciliary body pars plicata |
| **Cotton-wool spots** | Nerve fiber layer infarcts (cytoid bodies = swollen axons + mitochondria) |
| **Hard exudates** | Lipid-filled macrophages in outer plexiform layer |
| **IRMA** | Intraretinal microvascular abnormalities — shunt vessels in ischemic retina |
| **Neovascularization** | New vessels from VEGF; fragile → hemorrhage |
| **Neovascular glaucoma** | Iris neovascular membrane → synechiae → angle closure |

### Treatment Logic
- 🔴 **Laser photocoagulation** ablates nonperfused retina → ↓VEGF → neovascularization regresses → proves **hypoxia drives neovascularization**
- **Intravitreal anti-VEGF** (bevacizumab, ranibizumab, aflibercept) = mainstay for macular edema and neovascularization

### Histologic Marker of Diabetes in the Eye
🔴 **Massive thickening of the ciliary body (pars plicata) epithelial basement membrane** (PAS-positive) — reminiscent of glomerular mesangial expansion.

> 🎯 "Pericyte loss = earliest histologic; microaneurysms = earliest clinical; VEGF drives neovascularization; laser ablation proves hypoxia; BM thickening of pars plicata = histologic marker of diabetes."

## Q4. What is cataract? Mention its types and causes.

### Definition
🔴 **Cataract = opacification of the crystalline lens**, congenital or acquired. The lens is a **closed epithelial system** — capsule envelops it, epithelium infoliates inside → lens grows throughout life → **no neoplasms of the lens are described**.

### Types and Causes

#### A. Congenital Cataracts

| Cause | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Genetic** | Autosomal dominant (most common); mutations in crystallin genes, connexin |
| **Infections** | **Rubella** (most common congenital infection causing cataract — bilateral), CMV, herpes, syphilis |
| **Metabolic** | **Galactosemia** (aldose reductase converts galactitol → osmotic damage), hypocalcemia |
| **Chromosomal** | Down syndrome, Turner syndrome |
| **Persistent fetal vasculature** | Unilateral, microphthalmos |

#### B. Acquired Cataracts

| Type | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Age-related nuclear sclerosis** | 🔴 Most common; **urochrome pigment** turns nucleus brown → distorts blue perception (Rembrandt's yellows); progressive |
| **Anterior subcapsular** | Fibrous metaplasia of lens epithelium after prolonged iris-lens contact (posterior synechiae) |
| **Posterior subcapsular** | Migration of lens epithelium posterior to equator; **corticosteroid-associated** |
| **Traumatic** | Direct lens injury → opacification |
| **Radiation** | Ionizing radiation, infrared (glassblowers' cataract) |
| **Diabetic** | Sorbitol accumulation (polyol pathway) → osmotic swelling; also associated with anterior subcapsular |
| **Drug-induced** | **Corticosteroids** (most important), chloroquine, amiodarone |
| **Uveitic** | Chronic intraocular inflammation → synechiae → cataract |

### Associations (Viva Favorite)

| Association | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Galactosemia** | Galactitol accumulation → osmotic damage |
| **Diabetes mellitus** | Polyol pathway + BM thickening |
| **Wilson disease** | Copper deposition (Kayser-Fleischer ring in Descemet) |
| **Atopic dermatitis** | Anterior subcapsular (anterior capsular phimosis) |
| **Corticosteroids** | Posterior subcapsular (most common drug cause) |
| **Hypoparathyroidism** | Hypocalcemia → calcification |

### Clinical Features
- **Painless, progressive visual loss** (most common symptom)
- **Leukocoria** in advanced cases (white pupil)
- Decreased visual acuity, glare, difficulty with night driving
- **"Second sight" phenomenon** — temporary improvement in near vision in myopic shift from nuclear sclerosis

### Treatment
- 🔴 **Surgical extraction** (phacoemulsification) leaving the capsule + **prosthetic intraocular lens (IOL) implantation**
- Most commonly performed ophthalmic surgery worldwide

> 🎯 "Cataract = lens opacity; nuclear sclerosis (urochrome) = most common age-related; corticosteroids = posterior subcapsular; galactosemia/diabetes/Wilson/atopy = associations; lens is a closed system → no neoplasms."

## Q5. Classify glaucoma and describe its pathogenesis.

### Definition
🔴 **Glaucoma = a group of diseases with characteristic visual field loss and optic nerve cupping**, most associated with **elevated intraocular pressure (IOP)**. It is the **second leading cause of blindness worldwide** (after cataract).

### Classification

#### A. Primary Glaucomas

| Type | Mechanism | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary open-angle (POAG)** 🔴🔴 | 🔴 **Most common form of glaucoma**; **increased resistance to outflow** within an open trabecular meshwork; angle is open | Painless, gradual; ↑ IOP; peripheral visual field loss → central; optic disc cupping |
| **Primary angle-closure (PACG)** | **Iris physically occludes** the trabecular meshwork; **pupillary block → iris bombé** | Shallow anterior chamber (hyperopia); acute episode = painful red eye + ↑↑ IOP; emergency |
| **Normal-tension glaucoma** | Optic nerve damage with **normal IOP** | Susceptible neurons; vascular factors |

#### B. Secondary Glaucomas

| Type | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Pseudoexfoliation** | 🔴 Most common secondary open-angle; fibrillar material in anterior segment + viscera clogs meshwork |
| **Neovascular** | Retinal ischemia → VEGF → iris fibrovascular membrane → contraction → synechiae occlude angle |
| **Phacolytic** | Leaking cataract → macrophages phagocytose lens proteins → clog meshwork |
| **Ghost cell** | Senescent RBCs after trauma → clog open meshwork |
| **Pigmentary** | Iris pigment released → clogs meshwork (pigment dispersion syndrome) |
| **Traumatic** | Hyphema, angle recession |
| **Steroid-induced** | Corticosteroids → ↑ IOP |

### Pathogenesis of POAG — Step-by-Step

```
↑ Intraocular pressure (IOP)
    ↓
Compression of retinal ganglion cell axons at the optic nerve head
    +
Vascular ischemia of the optic nerve head
    ↓
Retinal ganglion cell APOPTOSIS
    ↓
Nerve fiber layer thinning
    ↓
OPTIC NERVE CUPPING (unique to glaucoma = cupped AND atrophic)
    ↓
PERIPHERAL → CENTRAL visual field loss
    ↓
BLINDNESS if untreated
```

### Key Pathologic Features

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **↑ IOP** | Most important risk factor; but normal-tension glaucoma exists |
| **Ganglion cell loss** | Progressive; irreversible (no regeneration) |
| **Nerve fiber layer thinning** | Measurable by OCT |
| **Optic disc cupping** | 🔴 **Cupped AND atrophic = unique to glaucoma** |
| **Buphthalmos** | 🔴 Diffuse globe enlargement in infants/children (sclera distensible) |
| **Staphyloma** | Scleral thinning in adults from chronically elevated IOP |

### Open-Angle vs Angle-Closure — EXAM FAVORITE

| Feature | **Open-Angle** | **Angle-Closure** |
|---|---|---|
| Angle | **Open** — meshwork accessible | **Closed** — iris apposed to meshwork |
| Mechanism | ↑ Outflow resistance | Physical occlusion (pupillary block) |
| Onset | **Gradual** | **Acute** (emergency) or chronic |
| Symptoms | Painless peripheral vision loss | Painful red eye, headache, nausea, halos |
| IOP | Mildly ↑ | Markedly ↑↑ |
| Chamber depth | Normal | **Shallow** (hyperopia) |
| Treatment | **Prostaglandin analogs, β-blockers, laser trabeculoplasty** | **Peripheral iridotomy** (laser hole in iris) |

> 🎯 "POAG = most common; open angle + ↑ outflow resistance + painless; angle-closure = pupillary block + iris bombé + painful emergency; cupped AND atrophic disc = unique to glaucoma; buphthalmos in infants."

# Part C — Eye: Retinal Detachment, AMD & Optic Neuropathy (Q6–8)

## Q6. Classify retinal detachment. Describe the pathogenesis of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment.

### Definition
🔴 **Retinal detachment (RD) = separation of the neurosensory retina from the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE).** The potential space between retina and RPE is the **subretinal space** (embryonic vitreous cavity).

### Classification

| Type | Mechanism | Causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Rhegmatogenous** 🔴 | **Full-thickness retinal break** → liquefied vitreous seeps through tear into subretinal space | Aging vitreous liquefaction/collapse, trauma, high myopia |
| **Tractional** | Fibrovascular membranes (e.g., proliferative diabetic retinopathy, ROP) pull retina off | PDR, ROP, penetrating trauma |
| **Exudative (nonrhegmatogenous)** | Protein-rich fluid from choroid accumulates under retina **without a break** | Choroidal tumors, malignant HTN, VKH, inflammatory conditions |

### Pathogenesis of Rhegmatogenous RD — Step-by-Step

```
AGING → Vitreous liquefaction (synchysis) + posterior vitreous detachment (PVD)
    ↓
Vitreous traction on retina at sites of adhesion
    ↓
RETINAL TEAR (usually at vitreous base, lattice degeneration, or old artifact)
    ↓
LIQUEFIED VITREOUS seeps through tear into subretinal space
    ↓
PROGRESSIVE SEPARATION of neurosensory retina from RPE
    ↓
Photoreceptor outer segments severed from RPE → cannot be maintained
    ↓
PHOTORECEPTOR DEGENERATION → progressive visual loss
    ↓
If untreated → TOTAL RETINAL DETACHMENT → BLINDNESS
```

### Key Pathologic Features

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Lattice degeneration** | Most common predisposing lesion; peripheral retinal thinning with holes |
| **Retinal tear** | Full-thickness break; usually horseshoe-shaped |
| **Subretinal fluid** | Clear, protein-rich; separates retina from RPE |
| **Proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR)** | 🔴 Major complication; glial/RPE cells form epiretinal/subretinal membranes → contraction → fixed folds → surgical failure |

### Clinical Features
- **Sudden onset of floaters** (vitreous debris)
- **Flashing lights** (photopsia — vitreous traction on retina)
- **"Curtain" or "shadow"** across visual field (progressive detachment)
- **Scotoma** (blind spot)
- Fundoscopy: elevated, gray, opaque retina; folds

### Treatment
- **Laser photocoagulation / cryopexy** around tear (creates chorioretinal adhesion)
- **Scleral buckling** (indents sclera to approximate RPE to detached retina)
- **Pneumatic retinopexy** (gas bubble tamponade)
- **Vitrectomy** (for complex cases with PVR)

> 🎯 "Rhegmatogenous = tear + vitreous traction + subretinal fluid; exudative = no tear (tumor/HTN); PVR = worst complication; lattice degeneration = most common predisposing lesion."

## Q7. Compare dry and wet age-related macular degeneration (AMD).

### Definition
🔴 **AMD = damage to the macula (central vision); the most common cause of irreversible visual loss in the United States.** Cumulative incidence 8% in ≥75 yr.

### Comparison Table — EXAM FAVORITE

| Feature | **Dry (Atrophic) AMD** | **Wet (Neovascular) AMD** |
|---|---|---|
| **Frequency** | ~80–90% of AMD | ~10–20% (but causes 90% of severe vision loss) |
| **Key lesion** | **Drusen** (deposits between RPE and Bruch membrane) + **geographic atrophy of RPE** | **Choroidal neovascularization (CNV)** — vessels from choriocapillaris breach Bruch membrane |
| **Pathogenesis** | RPE dysfunction → photoreceptor loss; complement dysregulation (CFH) | Angiogenesis driven by **VEGF** from ischemic RPE/choroid |
| **Vision loss** | **Severe, gradual** (central scotoma) | **Rapid** (hemorrhage → macular scar) |
| **Fundoscopy** | Drusen (yellow deposits), RPE atrophy, pigment changes | Subretinal hemorrhage, fluid, fibrovascular membrane |
| **OCT** | RPE thinning, drusen, geographic atrophy | Subretinal fluid, CNV membrane |
| **Treatment** | Zinc + antioxidant vitamins (AREDS2) may slow progression; no cure | 🔴 **Intravitreal anti-VEGF** (ranibizumab, aflibercept, bevacizumab) = mainstay |
| **Prognosis** | Slow progression over years | Rapid without treatment; anti-VEGF improves outcomes |

### Pathogenesis of AMD — Detailed

```
AGING + GENETIC SUSCEPTIBILITY (CFH, ARMS2/HTRA1, complement genes)
    ↓
EXCESS COMPLEMENT ACTIVITY (loss-of-function CFH alleles)
    ↓
RPE dysfunction → ↓ phagocytosis of photoreceptor outer segments
    ↓
DRUSEN FORMATION (basal laminar deposits between RPE and Bruch membrane)
    ↓
Bruch membrane thickening → impaired nutrient/waste exchange
    ↓
CHOROIDAL ISCHEMIA → ↑ VEGF
    ↓
WET AMD: CNV grows through Bruch membrane → hemorrhage → fibrovascular scar
    ↓
DRY AMD: progressive RPE atrophy → photoreceptor loss → geographic atrophy
```

### Risk Factors

| Factor | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Age** | Strongest risk factor; doubles every 5 yr after 50 |
| **Genetics** | 🔴 **CFH (complement factor H)** — loss-of-function alleles; ARMS2/HTRA1 |
| **Smoking** | 2–4× risk; synergizes with genetic predisposition |
| **UV light** | Possible contributor |
| **Cardiovascular disease** | Hypertension, atherosclerosis |
| **Diet** | Low antioxidant intake |

### Wet AMD Mimics
- Pathologic myopia (Fuchs spot)
- Trauma to Bruch membrane
- Presumed ocular histoplasmosis syndrome
- Angioid streaks

> 🎯 "AMD = #1 irreversible blindness in US; dry = drusen + atrophy; wet = CNV + anti-VEGF; CFH/complement genes; smoking ↑ risk; wet = 10% but causes 90% severe vision loss."

## Q8. Differentiate papilledema from anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (AION).

### Papilledema

🔴 **Papilledema = optic nerve head edema from elevated CSF pressure; BILATERAL disc swelling.**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | ↑ ICP → transmitted along optic nerve sheath → venous stasis + blocked axoplasmic transport at disc |
| **Laterality** | **Bilateral** (always — raised CSF pressure affects both nerves) |
| **Disc appearance** | **Swollen + HYPEREMIC** |
| **Vision** | Acute phase usually **preserved** (may have transient visual obscurations) |
| **Causes** | Space-occupying lesions (tumor, abscess), idiopathic intracranial hypertension (pseudotumor cerebri), venous sinus thrombosis, meningeal disease |
| **Pathology** | Swollen axons with axoplasmic stasis; nerve fiber layer displacement laterally → blurred disc margins |

### Anterior Ischemic Optic Neuropathy (AION)

🔴 **AION = ischemic infarction of the optic nerve head (the eye's "stroke").**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | Ischemia → infarction of optic nerve head (posterior ciliary artery territory) |
| **Laterality** | Often **unilateral** (may be bilateral in temporal arteritis) |
| **Disc appearance** | **Swollen + PALE** (↓ perfusion) |
| **Vision** | **Lost** (permanent — optic nerve does not regenerate) |
| **Types** | **Arteritic** (temporal arteritis — emergency!) vs **Non-arteritic** (hypertension, diabetes, small disc) |
| **Pathology** | Segmental or total optic nerve infarction; axonal necrosis |

### Comparison Table — EXAM FAVORITE

| Feature | **Papilledema** | **AION** |
|---|---|---|
| **Laterality** | **Bilateral** | Usually unilateral (may be bilateral in arteritic) |
| **Mechanism** | Venous stasis + axoplasmic transport block | **Ischemia → infarction** |
| **Disc** | **Swollen + HYPEREMIC** | **Swollen + PALE** |
| **Vision** | Acute phase usually **preserved** | **Lost** (permanent) |
| **Pain** | Headache (↑ ICP) | May have pain (arteritic = headache, jaw claudication) |
| **Key test** | **MRI/MRV** to find cause of ↑ ICP | **ESR/CRP** (if arteritic); fluorescein angiography |
| **Emergency** | Depends on cause | 🔴 **Temporal arteritis = medical emergency → treat with high-dose corticosteroids IMMEDIATELY** |

### Temporal Arteritis (Giant Cell Arteritis)
- 🔴 **Can infarct BOTH optic nerves → TOTAL BLINDNESS**
- Age >50; temporal headache, jaw claudication, polymyalgia rheumatica
- **ESR markedly elevated** (>50 mm/hr)
- **Temporal artery biopsy** = gold standard (granulomatous vasculitis with giant cells)
- **Treatment:** High-dose prednisolone → prevent bilateral blindness

> 🎯 "Papilledema = bilateral, hyperemic, vision preserved; AION = unilateral, pale, vision lost. Temporal arteritis = bilateral emergency → steroids NOW."

# Part D — Eye: Sympathetic Ophthalmia & Phthisis Bulbi (Q9–10)

## Q9. What is sympathetic ophthalmia? Describe its pathogenesis and clinical features.

### Definition
🔴 **Sympathetic ophthalmia = bilateral granulomatous panuveitis occurring after penetrating injury to one eye.** The injured eye is the "exciting" eye; the contralateral eye is the "sympathizing" eye.

### Pathogenesis

```
PENETRATING INJURY to one eye
    ↓
Sequestration of RETINAL ANTIGENS (S-antigen, IRBP, rhodopsin)
    ↓
Breakdown of immune privilege (eye is an immune-privileged site)
    ↓
Activation of AUTOAGGRESSIVE T CELLS (CD4+ Th1/Th17) against retinal antigens
    ↓
T cells cross to the CONTRALATERAL EYE via blood/lymphatics
    ↓
DELAYED HYPERSENSITIVITY (Type IV) reaction in BOTH eyes
    ↓
BILATERAL GRANULOMATOUS PANUVEITIS
    ↓
If untreated → BILATERAL BLINDNESS
```

### Key Pathologic Features

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Dalen-Fuchs nodules** | Granulomatous aggregates beneath the RPE (epithelioid cells + macrophages) |
| **Non-granulomatous** | Plasma cells **ABSENT** (important distinguishing feature) |
| **Eosinophils** | May be present |
| **Choroidal infiltration** | Dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate with Dalen-Fuchs nodules |
| **Retinal involvement** | Choroid primarily; retina may be relatively preserved early |

### Clinical Features
- 🔴 **Penetrating injury** to one eye (even minor — surgery, laser) → **2 weeks to YEARS later**
- Bilateral blurry vision, floaters, photophobia
- **Anterior uveitis** (cells/flare in anterior chamber)
- **Keratic precipitates** (mutton-fat if granulomatous)
- **Vitreous cells**
- If untreated → bilateral retinal detachment → bilateral blindness

### Risk Factors
- Penetrating ocular trauma (most common)
- Intraocular surgery (especially vitreoretinal)
- Previous ocular inflammation

### Prognosis
- Better with early treatment
- Delayed treatment → poor visual outcome
- May have recurrent episodes

### Treatment
- 🔴 **Systemic immunosuppression** (corticosteroids, steroid-sparing agents)
- **Enucleation of the exciting eye** within 2 weeks may prevent sympathizing (controversial; if the injured eye has no visual potential)
- Regular monitoring of both eyes

> 🎯 "Sympathetic ophthalmia = bilateral granulomatous panuveitis after penetrating injury; Dalen-Fuchs nodules; plasma cells ABSENT; treat with immunosuppression; enucleate the exciting eye if no visual potential."

## Q10. What is phthisis bulbi? Mention its hallmarks.

### Definition
🔴 **Phthisis bulbi = an end-stage, small (atrophic), AND internally disorganized eye.** Results from severe ocular trauma, intraocular inflammation, chronic retinal detachment, or other devastating eye diseases.

### Key Hallmarks

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Microphthalmos** | 🔴 Small, shrunken eye (NOT the same as congenital microphthalmos) |
| **Ciliochoroidal effusion** | Exudate/blood between ciliary body–sclera and choroid–sclera; associated with **hypotony** (low IOP) |
| **Cyclitic membrane** | A fibrovascular membrane stretching across the eye between ciliary bodies |
| **Chronic retinal detachment** | Total, long-standing detachment with photoreceptor loss |
| **Optic nerve atrophy** | Pale, thinned disc |
| **Intraocular bone** | 🔴 **Osseous metaplasia of RPE** (the RPE differentiates into bone); pathognomonic |
| **Thickened sclera** | Especially posteriorly |
| **Hypotony** | Low IOP → globe collapse |
| **Square-shaped eye** | Hypotony + extraocular muscle pull → distorted globe shape |

### Causes
- Severe penetrating trauma
- Endophthalmitis/panophthalmitis
- Chronic retinal detachment
- Intraocular inflammation (uveitis)
- Neovascular glaucoma (end-stage)
- Intraocular tumors (treated/untreated)

### Differential from Congenital Microphthalmos
- Congenital microphthalmos = small eye but **NOT internally disorganized**
- Phthisis = small AND disorganized + intraocular bone + cyclitic membrane

> 🎯 "Phthisis bulbi = small + disorganized eye; intraocular bone (RPE osseous metaplasia) + ciliochoroidal effusion + cyclitic membrane + hypotony = hallmarks."

# Part E — ENT: Sinusitis, Otitis Media & Ear Pathology (Q11–14)

## Q11. Describe the pathogenesis of sinusitis. Mention its complications.

### Definition
🔴 **Sinusitis = inflammation of the paranasal sinuses**, usually secondary to obstruction of the sinus ostia → mucus stasis → secondary infection.

### Pathogenesis — Step-by-Step

```
PREDISPOSING FACTORS:
  - Upper respiratory infection (viral rhinitis)
  - Allergic rhinitis (mucosal edema)
  - Nasal polyps (mechanical obstruction)
  - Anatomical abnormalities (deviated septum, concha bullosa)
  - Ciliary dysfunction (Kartagener syndrome, cystic fibrosis)
  - Immunosuppression
    ↓
OSTIAL OBSTRUCTION (maxillary > frontal > ethmoid > sphenoid)
    ↓
MUCUS STAGNATION within sinus cavity
    ↓
BACTERIAL SUPERINFECTION
    ↓
ACUTE SINUSITIS (± chronic if recurrent/persistent)
    ↓
COMPLICATIONS (if untreated)
```

### Organisms
- **Acute:** *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *H. influenzae*, *M. catarrhalis*, *S. aureus*
- **Chronic:** Polymicrobial; anaerobes (*Fusobacterium*, *Bacteroides*)
- **Fungal:** *Aspergillus*, *Mucor* (in diabetics/immunosuppressed)

### Complications

| Complication | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Orbital spread** | Floor of orbit = roof of maxillary sinus; medial wall = **lamina papyracea** → orbital cellulitis, abscess |
| **Osteomyelitis** | Frontal bone (Pott's puffy tumor) |
| **Mucormycosis** | 🔴 Diabetics/ketoacidosis → black necrotic eschar in nose/sinus; angioinvasive → orbital/cerebral spread |
| **Epidural/subdural abscess** | Intracranial spread |
| **Septic thrombophlebitis** | Dural venous sinus thrombosis |
| **Brain abscess** | Frontal lobe (from frontal sinus) |

### Kartagener Syndrome
- 🔴 **Ciliary dysfunction → chronic sinusitis + bronchiectasis + situs inversus**
- Autosomal recessive; dynein arm defect → immotile cilia
- Part of **primary ciliary dyskinesia** spectrum

> 🎯 "Sinusitis = ostial obstruction → mucus stasis → infection; mucormycosis in diabetics (black eschar); Kartagener = sinusitis + bronchiectasis + situs inversus; orbital spread via lamina papyracea."

## Q12. Classify otitis media. Describe its complications.

### Definition
🔴 **Otitis media = inflammation of the middle ear**, the most common infection in infants and children.

### Classification

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Acute otitis media (AOM)** | 🔴 Most common; painful, fever, bulging erythematous TM; **S. pneumoniae** (most common), nontypeable *H. influenzae*, *M. catarrhalis* |
| **Otitis media with effusion (OME)** | Serous fluid in middle ear without signs of infection; "glue ear" |
| **Chronic otitis media** | Persistent or recurrent TM perforation; **Pseudomonas**, *S. aureus*, fungus |
| **Chronic suppurative otitis media** | Active infection + perforation + purulent discharge |
| **Mastoiditis** | Extension into mastoid air cells → pain, swelling behind ear |

### Pathogenesis

```
Eustachian tube dysfunction (shorter, more horizontal in children)
    ↓
Negative middle ear pressure → serous effusion
    ↓
Bacterial ascending from nasopharynx
    ↓
ACUTE OTITIS MEDIA
    ↓
If untreated → TM perforation, chronic disease, complications
```

### Complications

| Complication | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **TM perforation** | Pain relief but conductive hearing loss; may heal spontaneously |
| **Conductive hearing loss** | Ossicular damage or TM perforation |
| **Cholesteatoma** | 🔴 Non-neoplastic cyst lined by keratinizing squamous epithelium → **ERODES ossicles, labyrinth, bone** |
| **Labyrinthitis** | Infection spreading to inner ear → vertigo, sensorineural hearing loss |
| **Mastoiditis** | Extension to mastoid → swelling, erythema behind ear |
| **Intracranial spread** | Meningitis, epidural/subdural abscess, brain abscess (temporal lobe), lateral sinus thrombosis |
| **Facial nerve palsy** | Nerve runs through middle ear → compression/inflammation |
| **Petrositis** | Infection of petrous temporal bone |

### Diabetic Consideration
🔴 **Pseudomonas aeruginosa otitis media in diabetics = aggressive necrotizing otitis media** → may extend to skull base → osteomyelitis → cranial nerve palsies

> 🎯 "AOM = most common in children; S. pneumoniae; cholesteatoma = keratin cyst that erodes bone; Pseudomonas in diabetics = necrotizing; intracranial spread = meningitis/abscess."

## Q13. What is cholesteatoma? Describe its pathogenesis and complications.

### Definition
🔴 **Cholesteatoma = a non-neoplastic, keratinizing squamous epithelium-lined cyst filled with desquamated keratin debris** in the middle ear/mastoid. Despite being benign, it is locally destructive.

### Pathogenesis

#### Primary (Congenital)
- **Epidermoid cyst** from squamous epithelial rest in middle ear (from squamous metaplasia of TM epithelium or migration of external canal skin)

#### Secondary (Acquired) — Most Common
```
Chronic otitis media / TM perforation
    ↓
Retraction pocket formation (usually pars flaccida)
    ↓
Ingrowth of squamous epithelium through TM perforation
    ↓
Keratin accumulation within middle ear
    ↓
Progressive enlargement
    ↓
PROTEOLYTIC ENZYMES + PRESSURE EROSION
    ↓
Destruction of ossicles, labyrinth, bony structures
```

### Morphology
- **White, pearly, glistening mass** behind the TM or in mastoid
- Lined by **keratinizing squamous epithelium**
- Filled with **lamellated keratin debris ± cholesterol crystals**
- Underlying **granulation tissue** with inflammation
- **Bone erosion** at margins

### Complications

| Complication | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Conductive hearing loss** | 🔴 **Ossicular erosion** (incus most vulnerable) |
| **Sensorineural hearing loss** | Labyrinthine fistula → inner ear damage |
| **Chronic suppurative otitis media** | Active infection with purulent discharge |
| **Labyrinthine fistula** | Horizontal semicircular canal most common site → positive fistula test |
| **Facial nerve palsy** | Compression/inflammation of CN VII |
| **Meningitis** | Intracranial spread |
| **Brain abscess** | Temporal lobe or cerebellar |
| **Lateral sinus thrombophlebitis** | Septic thrombosis of sigmoid sinus |

### Clinical Features
- **Chronic, painless, foul-smelling otorrhea** (ear discharge)
- **Progressive conductive hearing loss**
- **Painless TM perforation** (usually posterior/superior)
- Cholesteatoma visible as white mass on otoscopy

### Treatment
- 🔴 **Surgical excision** (canal wall up or down mastoidectomy)
- Must be completely removed — **recurrence is common if incomplete**
- Requires lifelong follow-up

> 🎯 "Cholesteatoma = keratin-filled cyst that erodes bone (ossicles → hearing loss, labyrinth → vertigo, skull → intracranial complications); foul-smelling otorrhea; surgery is the only treatment."

## Q14. What is otosclerosis? Describe its pathogenesis.

### Definition
🔴 **Otosclerosis = abnormal bone deposition around the oval window fixing the stapes footplate**, causing progressive **conductive hearing loss**. The most common cause of conductive hearing loss in young adults.

### Pathogenesis — Step-by-Step

```
GENETIC PREDISPOSITION (autosomal dominant, incomplete penetrance)
    ↓
abnormal bone remodeling at the oval window
    ↓
Phase 1: OSTEoclasis (bone resorption) → vascular spongiotic bone ("otospongiotic" phase)
    ↓
Phase 2: New bone formation (osteoblastic activity) → dense sclerotic bone
    ↓
FIXATION of stapes footplate to the oval window
    ↓
IMMOBILIZATION of ossicular chain
    ↓
CONDUCTIVE HEARING LOSS (mechanical阻塞 of sound conduction)
```

### Key Features

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Inheritance** | **Autosomal dominant** with incomplete penetrance |
| **Sex** | F > M (2:1) |
| **Age of onset** | 20–40 yr |
| **Laterality** | Usually **bilateral** (both ears) |
| **Site** | **Anterior footplate** of stapes (near fissula ante fenestram) |
| **Histology** | **Blue mantles** (basophilic cement lines) around new bone; irregular bony remodeling; giant cells in resorption phase |
| **Hearing loss** | Progressive **conductive** (stapes fixation); may have sensorineural component if cochlea involved ("cochlear otosclerosis") |
| **Schwartze sign** | 🔴 Pink/red discoloration of the TM (vascular spongiotic bone visible through promontory) |

### Clinical Features
- **Painless, progressive bilateral hearing loss** (over decades)
- **Positive family history**
- **Schwartze sign** (faint pink blush on TM)
- Tinnitus, vertigo (rare)
- **Carhart notch** on audiometry (bone conduction dip at 2–4 kHz — artifact from stapes fixation)

### Treatment
- **Stapedectomy / stapedotomy** — replace fixed stapes with prosthesis; curative
- **Hearing aids** — alternative if surgery not desired
- **Sodium fluoride** — controversial; may slow progression

> 🎯 "Otosclerosis = AD; stapes footplate fixation → conductive hearing loss; blue mantles on histology; Schwartze sign; stapedectomy = treatment; most common cause of conductive loss in young adults."

# Part F — ENT: Nasopharyngeal Tumors (Q15–16)

## Q15. Describe the pathogenesis and clinical features of nasopharyngeal carcinoma.

### Definition
🔴 **Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) = malignancy arising from nasopharyngeal epithelium, strongly associated with EBV.** Most common in southern Chinese and African populations.

### Pathogenesis

```
GENETIC SUSCEPTIBILITY (HLA-A2, HLA-B46 in Chinese)
    +
EBV INFECTION (latent, with LMP-1 + EBER expression)
    +
ENVIRONMENTAL COFACTORS:
  - Nitrosamine-rich diets (salted fish, fermented foods)
  - Smoking
  - Occupational fumes
    ↓
Epithelial cell transformation
    ↓
NASOPHARYNGEAL CARCINOMA (nonkeratinizing undifferentiated type)
```

### EBV Association
- 🔴 **EBER-1 RNA** (by in situ hybridization) — most sensitive marker
- **LMP-1** (latent membrane protein 1) — oncogenic; activates NF-κB, JAK/STAT
- LMP-2A — promotes cell survival
- EBNA1 — Epstein-Barr nuclear antigen

### Histologic Types

| Type | Frequency | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Nonkeratinizing (undifferentiated)** 🔴🔴 | ~80% | **"Syncytial" growth** — large cells with vesicular nuclei, indistinct borders; **heavy benign T-lymphocyte infiltrate** (old name: *lymphoepithelioma*); **most radiosensitive** |
| **Keratinizing SCC** | ~15% | Keratin pearls; **least radiosensitive** |
| **Basaloid SCC** | ~5% | Aggressive variant |

### Clinical Features
- 🔴 **Cervical lymph node metastasis** in up to **70%** at presentation (often the FIRST symptom)
- **Nasal obstruction, epistaxis**
- **Otalgia, serous otitis media** (Eustachian tube obstruction)
- **Cranial nerve palsies** (CN V, VI → cavernous sinus involvement)
- **Headache** (skull base invasion)

### Treatment
- 🔴 **Radiotherapy** = primary treatment for nonkeratinizing type
- **Chemoradiotherapy** for advanced disease
- Nonkeratinizing: 5-yr survival **70–98%**
- Keratinizing: only ~20%

> 🎯 "NPC = EBV + southern Chinese/African; nonkeratinizing undifferentiated = lymphoepithelioma = most radiosensitive; 70% present with cervical node mets; LMP-1 + EBER = EBV markers."

## Q16. What is nasopharyngeal angiofibroma? Describe its clinical features.

### Definition
🔴 **Nasopharyngeal angiofibroma = a benign, locally aggressive, highly vascular tumor** arising from the posterolateral wall of the nasopharynx (near the sphenopalatine foramen).

### Key Features

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Age/Sex** | 🔴 **Adolescent males** (10–20 yr); virtually never in females |
| **Growth** | Locally invasive but does NOT metastasize |
| **Vascularity** | Extremely high — **massive hemorrhage** during surgery/biopsy |
| **Genetics** | **CTNNB1/β-catenin mutations** (activating); may be syndromic with **FAP** (familial adenomatous polyposis) |
| **Recurrence** | ~20% after surgery; fatal 9% |
| **Sites** | Posterolateral nasopharynx → extends to pterygopalatine fossa, orbit, skull base |

### Clinical Features
- 🔴 **Epistaxis** (most common presenting symptom — often massive)
- **Nasal obstruction**
- **Epistaxis** in an **adolescent male** = nasopharyngeal angiofibroma until proven otherwise
- **Proptosis** (orbital extension)
- **Conductive hearing loss** (middle ear involvement)
- **Mass visible on nasopharyngoscopy** — pink/red, pulsatile

### Treatment
- 🔴 **Preoperative embolization** (reduce vascularity) + **surgical excision**
- **NOT biopsied** in clinic (risk of catastrophic hemorrhage)
- Hormonal therapy (estrogen/antiandrogen) may shrink tumor in some cases
- Radiation for residual/recurrent disease

> 🎯 "Nasopharyngeal angiofibroma = adolescent male + epistaxis + nasopharyngeal mass; CTNNB1; highly vascular → embolize + resect; NEVER biopsy in clinic."

# Part G — CNS: Brain Tumors & Meningitis (Q17–20)

## Q17. Classify primary brain tumors. Describe the morphological features of glioblastoma.

### Classification of Primary Brain Tumors

🔴 **Brain tumors are classified by cell of origin and WHO grade (I–IV).**

#### A. Gliomas (Most Common Primary Brain Tumors)

| Tumor | WHO Grade | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Diffuse astrocytoma** | II | IDH-mutant; nuclear atypia only; infiltrative; survival >5 yr |
| **Anaplastic astrocytoma** | III | IDH-mutant; + mitoses; worse prognosis |
| **Glioblastoma (GBM)** 🔴🔴 | IV | **Most common primary malignant brain tumor**; palisading necrosis + microvascular proliferation |
| **Oligodendroglioma** | II/III | **IDH-mutant + 1p/19q codeletion**; fried-egg cells; **best prognosis of gliomas** |
| **Pilocytic astrocytoma** | I | Children, cerebellum; cystic + mural nodule; Rosenthal fibers; KIAA1549-BRAF fusion |
| **Ependymoma** | II/III | Perivascular pseudorosettes; 4th ventricle (children), spinal cord (adults) |

#### B. Other Primary Tumors

| Tumor | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Meningioma** | Most common benign intracranial tumor; dural-based; psammoma bodies; female 3:2; NF2 |
| **Medulloblastoma** | Children, cerebellum; Homer Wright rosettes; SHH/WNT subtypes; CSF spread |
| **Schwannoma** | CN VIII (vestibular); NF2; Antoni A/B; Verocay bodies |
| **Primary CNS lymphoma** | EBV+ in AIDS; perivascular CD20+ B cells |
| **Choroid plexus tumors** | Papilloma (children: lateral ventricle), carcinoma |

#### C. Non-neoplastic Mass Lesions
- Brain abscess, demyelinating plaques, vascular malformations, parasitic cysts

### Glioblastoma — Detailed

🔴 **GBM = the most common and most aggressive primary malignant brain tumor in adults.**

#### WHO Classification (2021)

| Type | Molecular | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary GBM** | IDH-wild-type; +7/−10; TERT-promoter mutation; EGFR amplification | ~90% (de novo, older adults) |
| **Secondary GBM** | IDH-mutant; TP53; ATRX loss | ~10% (from lower-grade glioma, younger) |

#### Morphology — EXAM FAVORITE

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Palisading necrosis** | 🔴 Tumor cells align in rows around zones of necrosis (pseudopalisading); pathognomonic |
| **Microvascular proliferation** | 🔴 Glomeruloid bodies (endothelial proliferation) driven by VEGF |
| **High cellularity** | Pleomorphic astrocytes with atypical mitoses |
| **Infiltrative growth** | Extends well beyond visible margins (impossible to resect completely) |
| **Ring enhancement** | MRI: central necrosis with peripheral contrast enhancement |
| **GFAP positive** | Tumor cells express GFAP (astrocytic lineage) |

#### Molecular Markers

| Marker | Significance |
|---|---|
| **IDH status** | 🔴 IDH-wt = primary (worse); IDH-mut = secondary (better) |
| **+7/−10** | Chromosome 7 gain / 10 loss — primary GBM |
| **TERT-promoter** | Primary GBM |
| **EGFR amplification** | Primary GBM |
| **MGMT promoter methylation** | 🔴 Predicts response to **temozolomide** (alkylating chemotherapy) |
| **ATRX loss** | Secondary GBM |

#### Prognosis
- 🔴 **Mean survival ~15 months** with standard treatment (surgery + radiation + temozolomide)
- IDH-mutant: 2–4 yr
- IDH-wild-type: 6 mo–2 yr
- MGMT methylation → better temozolomide response

> 🎯 "GBM = palisading necrosis + microvascular proliferation; IDH-wt = primary (+7/−10, TERT, EGFR); IDH-mut = secondary (TP53, ATRX); MGMT methylation → temozolomide response; mean survival ~15 months."

## Q18. Compare schwannoma and neurofibroma.

### Comparison Table — EXAM FAVORITE

| Feature | **Schwannoma** | **Neurofibroma** |
|---|---|---|
| **Cell of origin** | **Schwann cells** (nerve sheath) | **Mixed**: Schwann cells + perineurial cells + fibroblasts + mast cells |
| **Encapsulation** | 🔴 **Encapsulated** | **NOT encapsulated** |
| **Relationship to nerve** | **Displaces nerve** (can be shelled out) | 🔴 **Intertwined within nerve** (cannot be separated without cutting nerve) |
| **Most common site** | 🔴 **Vestibular branch of CN VIII** (acoustic neuroma); also spinal nerve roots | Skin/subcutaneous; may arise from any peripheral nerve |

| **Verocay bodies** | 🔴 Palisading nuclei around eosinophilic fibrillary processes (Antoni A) | Absent |
| **S-100** | 🔴 **Strongly positive** (all cells) | Positive (less diffuse) |
| **Plexiform neurofibroma** | Absent | 🔴 **Pathognomonic of NF1**; plexiform = multiple nerves involved |
| **Malignant transformation** | Rare (~1%) | 🔴 **5–10% in NF1** → **Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST)** |
| **NF association** | 🔴 **NF2** (bilateral vestibular schwannomas = diagnostic) | 🔴 **NF1** (plexiform neurofibroma) |
| **Genetics** | **NF2/merlin (chr 22)** loss; also TRAF7, KLF4, AKT1 | **NF1/neurofibromin (chr 17)** loss → ↑RAS → ↑MAPK |
| **Hearing loss** | 🔴 **Unilateral sensorineural** (most common presentation of vestibular schwannoma) | Uncommon unless compression |
| **Treatment** | Surgical excision; stereotactic radiosurgery | Observation if asymptomatic; excision if symptomatic |

| Feature | **NF2** | **NF1** |
|---|---|---|
| Gene | NF2/merlin (chr 22) | NF1/neurofibromin (chr 17) |

| Other | — | Lisch nodules (iris hamartomas), sphenoid wing dysplasia, tibial pseudarthrosis |

> 🎯 "Schwannoma = encapsulated, Antoni A/B, Verocay bodies, displaces nerve, NF2 (bilateral vestibular); Neurofibroma = not encapsulated, within nerve, plexiform = NF1, malignant transformation 5–10%."

## Q19. Describe the pathogenesis and clinical features of meningitis.

### Definition
🔴 **Meningitis = inflammation of the leptomeninges (arachnoid + pia mater) and subarachnoid space.** Can be caused by bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites.

### Routes of Infection into the CNS

| Route | Example |
|---|---|
| **Hematogenous** 🔴 | Most common; bacteremia → choroid plexus → CSF |
| **Direct implantation** | Trauma, neurosurgery, OM绕耳感染 |
| **Local extension** | Sinusitis, otitis media, dental infection |
| **Along peripheral nerves** | Rabies, herpes zoster |

### Classification by Organism

#### A. Acute Bacterial (Pyogenic) Meningitis 🔴🔴

**Organisms by Age — THE EXAM FAVORITE:**

| Age Group | Organism |
|---|---|
| **Neonates** | **E. coli, Group B Streptococcus** |
| Infants | H. influenzae (↓ by vaccination) |
| **Adolescents/Young adults** | **Neisseria meningitidis** |
| **Elderly** | **Streptococcus pneumoniae, Listeria monocytogenes** |
| Immunocompromised | Klebsiella, Listeria, Cryptococcus |

**Pathogenesis:**
```
Nasopharyngeal colonization (N. meningitidis, S. pneumoniae)
    ↓
Bacteremia (transient → sustained)
    ↓
Survival in CSF (low complement, low immunoglobulins)
    ↓
Multiplication in subarachnoid space
    ↓
Release of bacterial cell wall components (LPS, peptidoglycan)
    ↓
Massive NEUTROPHILIC INFILTRATION + CYTOKINE STORM
    ↓
PURULENT EXUDATE fills subarachnoid space
    ↓
Complications: ↑ ICP, vasculitis, cranial nerve palsies, hydrocephalus
```

**CSF Findings:**

| Parameter | Bacterial Meningitis |
|---|---|
| Appearance | **Cloudy/purulent** |
| Opening pressure | **↑↑** (>300 mmH₂O) |
| WBC count | 🔴 **↑↑↑ (1,000–90,000/mm³)**; **Neutrophils predominate** |
| Protein | 🔴 **↑↑↑** (>100 mg/dL) |
| Glucose | 🔴 **Markedly ↓** (<40 mg/dL or <40% of serum) |
| Gram stain | Positive in 60–90% |
| Culture | Gold standard |

#### B. Aseptic (Viral) Meningitis

| Parameter | Viral Meningitis |
|---|---|
| Appearance | Clear |
| WBC count | **↑ (10–500/mm³)**; **Lymphocytes predominate** |
| Protein | Mildly ↑ |
| Glucose | 🔴 **Nearly always NORMAL** |
| Most common cause | **Enteroviruses (~80%)** |

#### C. Tuberculous Meningitis

| Parameter | TB Meningitis |
|---|---|
| Onset | Subacute (weeks) |
| Pathology | 🔴 **Basal exudate** (gelatinous, at base of brain); obliterative endarteritis → infarction; tuberculomas |
| WBC | Mononuclear pleocytosis |
| Protein | ↑↑↑ |
| Glucose | Moderately ↓ or normal |

### Complications

| Complication | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome** | 🔴 Meningococcal/pneumococcal septicemia → **hemorrhagic infarction of adrenal glands** |
| **Hydrocephalus** | Arachnoid fibrosis → obstructed CSF resorption |
| **Cerebral edema** | Vasogenic → herniation |
| **Subdural empyema** | Complication of sinusitis/otitis media |
| **Cranial nerve palsies** | Base of brain inflammation → CN III, VI, VII |
| **Sequelae** | Hearing loss, intellectual disability, seizures, hydrocephalus |

### Clinical Features (Bacterial)
- **Triad:** fever + headache + neck stiffness (meningismus)
- **Kernig sign:** pain on knee extension with hip flexed
- **Brudzinski sign:** involuntary knee/hip flexion on neck flexion
- **Altered consciousness** (if severe)
- **Petechial rash** (meningococcal — N. meningitidis)

> 🎯 "Meningitis organisms by age: neonate = E. coli/GBS; youth = Neisseria; old = Pneumococcus/Listeria. Bacterial CSF = neutrophils + ↓ glucose + ↑↑ protein; viral = lymphocytes + normal glucose. Waterhouse-Friderichsen = adrenal hemorrhage."

## Q20. Describe the examination and interpretation of CSF.

### CSF Formation and Circulation

🔴 **CSF is produced by the choroid plexus** → circulates through ventricular system → exits via **foramina of Luschka and Magendie** into cisterna magna → absorbed by **arachnoid granulations** over convexities.

**Production rate:** ~500 mL/day; normal volume ~150 mL; turnover ~3–4 times/day.

### Collection — Lumbar Puncture

| Parameter | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Site** | 🔴 **L3–L4 or L4–L5 interspace** (below conus medullaris to avoid cord injury) |
| **Position** | Lateral decubitus (for opening pressure) or sitting |
| **Needle** | Atraumatic (pencil-point) preferred |
| **Contraindications** | Raised ICP with mass effect (risk of herniation), local infection, coagulopathy |
| **First tube** | Cell count + differential |
| **Second tube** | Glucose + protein |
| **Third tube** | Microbiology (Gram stain, culture, India ink, AFB) |
| **Fourth tube** | Cell count (comparison with first tube to distinguish traumatic tap) |

### Normal CSF Values

| Parameter | Normal Value |
|---|---|
| **Appearance** | Clear, colorless |
| **Opening pressure** | 60–200 mmH₂O (lateral decubitus) |
| **WBC** | 🔴 **0–5 cells/µL** (all lymphocytes/monocytes) |
| **RBC** | 0 |
| **Protein** | 🔴 **15–45 mg/dL** |
| **Glucose** | 🔴 **50–80 mg/dL** (>60% of serum glucose) |
| **CSF/serum glucose ratio** | >0.6 |
| **Chloride** | 120–130 mEq/L |
| **Oligoclonal bands** | Absent |
| **Gram stain** | No organisms |
| **Culture** | No growth |

### CSF Findings in Disease — COMPREHENSIVE TABLE

| Condition | Appearance | Cells | Protein | Glucose | Special |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bacterial meningitis** | Cloudy | **↑↑↑ Neutrophils** (1,000–90,000) | **↑↑↑** | 🔴 **↓↓** | Gram stain +, culture + |
| **Viral meningitis** | Clear | ↑ Lymphocytes (10–500) | Mildly ↑ | **Normal** | Enteroviruses most common |
| **TB meningitis** | Clear/yellow | ↑ Lymphocytes (10–500) | **↑↑↑** | **↓** | AFB stain, PCR, adenosine deaminase ↑ |
| **Fungal meningitis** | Clear/viscous | ↑ Lymphocytes | ↑↑ | **↓** | India ink + (Cryptococcus); cryptococcal antigen |
| **MS** | Clear | Normal/mild ↑ | Normal/mild ↑ | Normal | 🔴 **Oligoclonal IgG bands** (in CSF but not serum) |
| **Subarachnoid hemorrhage** | **Xanthochromic** (yellow) | RBCs present | ↑ | Normal | **Xanthochromia** = bilirubin from RBC breakdown |
| **Traumatic tap** | Bloody → clears in later tubes | RBCs decrease from tube 1→4 | Slightly ↑ | Normal | **No xanthochromia** (fresh blood) |
| **Guillain-Barré** | Clear | Normal | 🔴 **↑↑** | Normal | **Albuminocytologic dissociation** (↑ protein, normal cells) |
| **Brain abscess** | Cloudy | Mixed (PMNs + lymphs) | ↑↑ | Normal/↓ | Organisms on culture |
| **Neoplastic** | Clear/xanthochromic | Malignant cells | ↑ | ↓ | **Cytology** positive |

### Special CSF Tests

| Test | Indication | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Oligoclonal bands** | MS | 🔴 Few activated B-cell clones; in CSF but NOT in serum = intrathecal IgG production |
| **India ink** | Cryptococcal meningitis | Encapsulated yeasts in CSF |
| **AFB stain** | TB meningitis | Acid-fast bacilli |
| **Gram stain** | Bacterial meningitis | Identifies organism |
| **Cytology** | Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis | Malignant cells |
| **PCR** | Viral meningitis | Enterovirus, HSV, VZV |
| **Cryptococcal antigen** | Cryptococcal meningitis | High sensitivity/specificity |
| **Lactate** | Bacterial vs viral | ↑ in bacterial; normal in viral |
| **Adenosine deaminase** | TB meningitis | ↑ in TB |

### Xanthochromia — Important Concept
- 🔴 **Xanthochromia = yellow discoloration of CSF supernatant** after centrifugation
- Caused by **bilirubin** from RBC breakdown (takes 2–4 hours to develop)
- 🔴 **Present in SAH** (spontaneous — aneurysm) but **NOT in traumatic tap** (fresh blood)
- Key differentiator between SAH and traumatic LP

### CSF Glucose Interpretation
- **Low glucose:** bacterial, TB, fungal, carcinomatous meningitis, subarachnoid hemorrhage
- **Normal glucose:** viral, early bacterial, MS
- **Mechanism of low glucose:** bacterial metabolism + impaired glucose transport across inflamed choroid plexus

### Protein Interpretation
- **Very high (>1000 mg/dL):** TB meningitis, brain abscess, spinal block (Froin syndrome)
- **Moderately high (100–500):** bacterial, fungal, carcinomatous
- **Mildly high (50–100):** viral, early infection
- **Albuminocytologic dissociation:** GBS (high protein, normal cells)

> 🎯 "CSF: bacterial = neutrophils + ↓ glucose + ↑↑ protein; viral = lymphocytes + normal glucose; TB = lymphocytes + ↓ glucose + ↑↑↑ protein; MS = oligoclonal bands; SAH = xanthochromia; GBS = albuminocytologic dissociation."

# Part H — CNS: Degenerative Diseases & Cerebrovascular Disease (Q21–24)

## Q21. Compare Alzheimer disease and Parkinson disease.

### Alzheimer Disease (AD)

🔴 **Most common cause of dementia in older adults.**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Epidemiology** | Prevalence doubles every 5 yr: 1% (60–64) → 40%+ (85–89); 5–10% familial |
| **Two hallmarks** | 🔴 **Aβ plaques** (extracellular) + **tau neurofibrillary tangles** (intracellular) |
| **Aβ pathway** | APP → β-secretase + γ-secretase → **Aβ42** (aggregates); γ-secretase = presenilin-1/2 |
| **Tau** | Hyperphosphorylated → loses microtubule binding → tangles; **tangle burden correlates better with dementia than plaque burden** |
| **Key gene** | 🔴 **ApoE ε4** (↑ risk + ↓ age of onset); APP (chr 21); PSEN1 (chr 14); PSEN2 (chr 1) |
| **Morphology** | Cortical atrophy (frontal/temporal/parietal); **neuritic plaques** (Aβ core + dystrophic neurites); **neurofibrillary tangles** (flame-shaped); **CAA** (Aβ40 in vessels) |
| **Selective vulnerability** | Hippocampus/entorhinal cortex earliest; spares primary motor/sensory cortex |
| **CSF** | ↑ phospho-tau + ↓ Aβ42 |

### Parkinson Disease (PD)

🔴 **Hypokinetic movement disorder; loss of dopaminergic neurons in substantia nigra.**

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Epidemiology** | Most common movement disorder; 1–2% over 60 yr; M > F |
| **Clinical triad** | 🔴 **Tremor (pill-rolling), rigidity, bradykinesia** |
| **Key gene** | 🔴 **SNCA (α-synuclein)**; **LRRK2** (most common AD cause); PARK2 (parkin, AR); GBA (most important risk factor) |
| **Morphology** | 🔴 **Pallor of substantia nigra + locus ceruleus**; **Lewy bodies** = intracytoplasmic eosinophilic inclusions (α-synuclein) with dense core + pale halo |
| **Toxins** | MPTP (meperidine contaminant → acute parkinsonism) |
| **Treatment** | 🔴 **L-DOPA** (corrects dopamine deficit; does not reverse progression) |
| **Dementia** | 30–40% develop PD dementia; Lewy body dementia = cortical Lewy bodies |

### Comparison Table

| Feature | **Alzheimer Disease** | **Parkinson Disease** |
|---|---|---|
| **Type** | Cognitive decline | Movement disorder |
| **Key protein** | **Aβ + tau** | **α-synuclein (Lewy bodies)** |
| **Brain region** | Hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, neocortex | **Substantia nigra**, locus ceruleus |
| **Gross** | Cortical atrophy, hydrocephalus ex vacuo | Pallor of substantia nigra |
| **Key gene** | ApoE ε4, APP, PSEN1/2 | SNCA, LRRK2, GBA |
| **Most common cause of** | **Dementia** in older adults | **Movement disorder** |
| **Treatment** | Cholinesterase inhibitors, memantine | L-DOPA |

> 🎯 "AD = Aβ plaques + tau tangles + ApoE ε4; PD = α-synuclein Lewy bodies + substantia nigra pallor + L-DOPA; tangles correlate better with AD dementia than plaques."

## Q22. What is cerebrovascular disease? Classify its types.

### Definition
🔴 **Cerebrovascular disease = any disorder of blood vessels supplying the brain**, encompassing ischemia, hemorrhage, and vascular malformations. **3rd leading cause of death** in the US (after heart disease + cancer).

### Classification

#### A. Ischemic Stroke (80–85% of all strokes)

| Type | Mechanism | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Thrombotic** | Atherosclerosis of large arteries (carotid bifurcation, MCA, basilar) | Gradual onset; often with warning TIAs; hypertension + diabetes |
| **Embolic** 🔴 | Cardiac source (atrial fibrillation, mural thrombi, valvular disease) or arterial (carotid atheroma) | **Most common cause of stroke**; sudden onset; MCA territory most common |
| **Lacunar** | 🔴 Hypertension → **arteriolosclerosis** (small vessel disease, 40–900 µm) → small cavitary infarcts **<15 mm** | Putamen > globus pallidus > thalamus > internal capsule |
| **Watershed (border zone)** | 🔴 **Severe hypotension** (cardiac arrest) → ischemia at most distal arterial territories | Wedge-shaped, often **bilateral**; ACA/MCA border |
| **Global hypoxia/ischemia** | Cardiac arrest, shock, CO poisoning | **Selective vulnerability:** hippocampal CA1, Purkinje cells, cortical layers III/V |

#### B. Hemorrhagic Stroke (15–20%)

| Type | Mechanism | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypertensive ganglionic** | 🔴 HTN → hyaline arteriolosclerosis → rupture of deep penetrating arteries | **Putamen 50–60%**, thalamus, pons, cerebellum |
| **CAA lobar** | Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (Aβ40 in vessels) | Elderly; lobar hemorrhage; **microbleeds**; ApoE ε2/ε4 risk |
| **Berry aneurysm SAH** | 🔴 Saccular aneurysm rupture at circle of Willis | **"Worst headache of my life"**; vasospasm days later |
| **AVM hemorrhage** | Arteriovenous malformation (KRAS) | Young adults; MCA territory; seizures + ICH |
| **Cavernous malformation** | Dilated channels; familial forms | Cerebellum, pons; low-flow |

#### C. Venous Thrombosis
- Superior sagittal sinus / deep cerebral vein thrombosis
- Hypercoagulable states, neoplasms, infections
- Often hemorrhagic infarction

### The Ischemic Penumbra — Key Concept
- 🔴 **Penumbra** = viable but ischemic brain tissue surrounding the infarct core
- Potentially salvageable with early reperfusion
- **Excitotoxicity:** ischemia → glutamate release → Ca²⁺ influx via **NMDA receptors** → neuronal injury

### Temporal Evolution of Cerebral Infarct

| Time | Gross | Microscopy |
|---|---|---|
| **0–6 h** | Little change | **"Red neurons"** (earliest marker of irreversible injury) |
| **48 h** | Pale, soft, swollen; gray-white junction indistinct | Neutrophils peak; cytotoxic + vasogenic edema |
| **2–10 days** | Gelatinous/friable; clear boundary | **Foamy macrophages** predominate; reactive gliosis |
| **10 days–3 wk** | Liquefies → fluid-filled cavity | Neovascularization; cavitation |
| **Healed** | Dense glial scar + cavity | Gliosis + perivascular connective tissue |

### Berry Aneurysm — Key Details

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Location** | 🔴 **~90% at major branch points of anterior circulation** (circle of Willis) |
| **Frequency** | ~2% of population; **multiple in 20–30%** |
| **Wall** | **Absent smooth muscle + intimal elastic lamina** (developmental defect) |
| **Risk of rupture** | 1.3%/yr overall; 🔴 **>10 mm = ~50%/yr** |
| **Associations** | ADPKD, **Ehlers-Danlos type IV**, NF1, Marfan, smoking, HTN |
| **Complications** | **Vasospasm** (days 3–14 → secondary ischemia); hydrocephalus |

### Clinical Features of Stroke
- **Sudden onset** focal neurological deficit
- Deficits follow vascular territory, not cause
- **MCA territory** most commonly affected (direct extension of internal carotid)
- **Watershed:** bilateral, often cognitive deficits
- **Lacunar:** may be silent or cause pure motor/sensory syndromes

### Treatment Principles
- 🔴 **Ischemic stroke:** Thrombolysis (tPA within 4.5 hr) for nonhemorrhagic; thrombectomy for large vessel occlusion
- **Hemorrhagic stroke:** Blood pressure control; surgical evacuation for large hematomas
- **SAH:** Nimodipine (prevent vasospasm); surgical clipping or endovascular coiling of aneurysm
- **Secondary prevention:** Antiplatelets (ischemic), statins, BP control

> 🎯 "CVA = 3rd leading cause of death; embolism > thrombosis; MCA most common; lacunar <15 mm = HTN; watershed = hypotension; ganglionic hemorrhage = HTN/putamen; lobar = CAA; berry aneurysm = circle of Willis, vasospasm. 'Red neurons' at 6–12 h."

## Q23. Describe the types of cerebral edema and herniation syndromes.

### Cerebral Edema — Two Types

| Feature | **Vasogenic** | **Cytotoxic** |
|---|---|---|
| **Location** | **Extracellular** (intercellular spaces) | **Intracellular** |
| **Mechanism** | 🔴 **Blood-brain barrier disruption** → increased vascular permeability | Cell membrane injury (neurons/glia/endothelium) → ionic gradient failure |
| **Causes** | Tumors, inflammation, focal ischemia | Generalized hypoxia, metabolic derangements |
| **Fluid composition** | Plasma ultrafiltrate (protein-poor) | Water + sodium |
| **Distribution** | White matter > gray matter (finger-like projections) | Affects all cell types equally |
| **Reversibility** | More reversible (if BBB recovers) | Often irreversible if severe |

### Herniation Syndromes — "SUT"

🔴 **Herniation = displacement of brain tissue from one compartment to another due to mass effect.**

| Type | Anatomy | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Subfalcine (cingulate)** | Unilateral hemisphere expansion → cingulate gyrus displaced under falx cerebri | Compression of **anterior cerebral artery** → secondary infarct |
| **Transtentorial (uncal, mesial temporal)** | Medial temporal lobe pushed against free margin of tentorium | 🔴 **3rd nerve compression → ipsilateral pupillary dilation + impaired ocular movements**; PCA compression → visual cortex infarct; contralateral peduncle compression → hemiparesis **ipsilateral** to herniation; **Duret hemorrhages** (midline linear hemorrhage in midbrain + pons) |
| **Tonsillar** | Cerebellar tonsils displaced through **foramen magnum** | 🔴 **Brainstem compression → compromise of respiratory + cardiac centers in medulla — LIFE-THREATENING** |

### Duret Hemorrhages
- 🔴 **Linear/flame-shaped hemorrhages in midline of midbrain + pons**
- Caused by tearing of **penetrating vessels** during downward transtentorial herniation
- **Bilateral**; often fatal
- Pathognomonic of uncal herniation

### Raised ICP — Causes

| Cause | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Generalized brain edema** | Diffuse hypoxia, metabolic encephalopathy, PRES |
| **Increased CSF volume** | Hydrocephalus (communicating/obstructive), choroid plexus tumor |
| **Focally expanding masses** | Tumor, abscess, hematoma |

### Clinical Features of Raised ICP
- Headache (worse in morning, with coughing/straining)
- Nausea and vomiting
- **Papilledema** (bilateral disc swelling)
- Altered consciousness
- **Cushing reflex** (hypertension + bradycardia + irregular respiration)
- Herniation → death

> 🎯 "Vasogenic = extracellular, BBB leak; cytotoxic = intracellular, membrane failure. SUT = Subfalcine (ACA) → Uncal (3rd nerve + Duret) → Tonsillar (FATAL). Duret hemorrhages = pathognomonic of uncal herniation."

## Q24. What is hydrocephalus? Classify its types.

### Definition
🔴 **Hydrocephalus = accumulation of CSF within the ventricular system → ventricular enlargement.** Most cases = impaired flow or resorption; overproduction is rare.

### Classification

| Type | Mechanism | Causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Communicating (non-obstructive)** | 🔴 Ventricular system stays in continuity with subarachnoid space; **entire system dilates** | Arachnoid fibrosis after meningitis, CSF overproduction (choroid plexus tumor), impaired arachnoid granulation resorption |
| **Obstructive (non-communicating)** | 🔴 Focal block within the ventricular system | **Aqueductal stenosis** (most common), 3rd ventricle mass, 4th ventricle mass |
| **Hydrocephalus ex vacuo** | 🔴 Compensatory ventricular enlargement from **loss of brain parenchyma** (NOT ↑ pressure) | Alzheimer disease, other atrophying conditions |

### Key Differences

| Feature | Communicating | Obstructive |
|---|---|---|
| **Block location** | Outside ventricles (arachnoid granulations) | Within ventricles (aqueduct, foramina) |
| **Ventricular dilation** | **All ventricles dilated uniformly** | Ventricles proximal to obstruction dilated; distal normal/small |
| **Most common site of block** | Arachnoid granulations | **Aqueduct of Sylvius** |
| **CSF pressure** | ↑ | ↑ |

### Infant vs Adult Presentation

| Feature | Infant | Adult |
|---|---|---|
| **Skull** | **Head enlarges** (sutures not closed) | Head circumference unchanged (sutures closed) |
| **Fontanelle** | Bulging, tense | Already closed |
| **Symptoms** | Macrocephaly, irritability, poor feeding, developmental delay | Headache, nausea, papilledema, gait disturbance, dementia |

### Causes of Hydrocephalus

| Category | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Congenital** | Aqueductal stenosis, Dandy-Walker, Arnold-Chiari II, myelomeningocele, AV malformation |
| **Acquired** | Meningitis (arachnoid fibrosis), hemorrhage (SAH), tumor (compression), trauma |
| **Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH)** | Elderly; **triad: dementia + gait disturbance + urinary incontinence**; ventricles enlarged disproportionate to atrophy; treatable with **VP shunt** |

### Complications
- **Brain damage** from compression
- **Herniation** if acute
- **Developmental delay** in children
- **Dementia** in adults (if chronic)

### Treatment
- 🔴 **VP (ventriculoperitoneal) shunt** — most common; diverts CSF from ventricles to peritoneal cavity
- **Endoscopic third ventriculostomy** — alternative for obstructive hydrocephalus (aqueductal stenosis)
- **Treat underlying cause** when possible

> 🎯 "Hydrocephalus = too much CSF in ventricles; communicating = whole system dilated (meningitis fibrosis); obstructive = focal block (aqueductal stenosis); ex vacuo = atrophy (NOT true hydrocephalus); infant = head enlarges; NPH = dementia + gait + incontinence."
