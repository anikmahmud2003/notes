# Part A — Testis (Q1–6)

## Q1. Classify tumours of the testis.

### Overview
**Germ cell tumours (GCT) = ~95% of testicular tumours** — the most common cancer in Caucasian males aged **15–45 yr**. Any solid testicular mass is treated as **malignant until proven otherwise**; biopsy is avoided (risk of tumour spillage) and **radical orchiectomy** is the standard first step.

### 1. Germ cell tumours (WHO 2016)

| Group | Types |
|---|---|
| **Seminomatous** | **Seminoma** (~50% of GCTs) |
| **Non-seminomatous (NSGCT)** | **Embryonal carcinoma** · **Yolk sac tumour** (endodermal sinus) · **Choriocarcinoma** · **Teratoma** (mature/immature) |
| **Mixed GCT** | ~60% of GCTs contain >1 type (e.g., teratoma + embryonal + yolk sac) |
| **Spermatocytic tumour** | Separate entity — men usually **>65 yr** |

- **Seminoma** — most common single type; radiosensitive; best prognosis.
- **Embryonal carcinoma** — anaplastic, aggressive; usually a component of mixed tumours.
- **Yolk sac tumour** — commonest tumour of infants/children up to **3 years**; secretes **AFP**; Schiller-Duval bodies.
- **Choriocarcinoma** — the **most malignant**; secretes **hCG**; often a tiny primary with widespread haemorrhagic metastases.
- **Teratoma** — derivatives of >1 germ layer; **benign before puberty, malignant after** (genetics, not histology, decide behaviour).
- **Spermatocytic tumour** — old men (>65), three cell populations, **gain of 9q**, never metastasizes.

### 2. Sex cord–stromal tumours
- **Leydig cell tumour** — elaborates androgens → gynecomastia/precocious puberty; **Reinke crystalloids** (~25%); ~10% malignant in adults.
- **Sertoli cell tumour** — usually hormonally silent; associated with **Carney complex, Peutz-Jeghers, FAP**.
- **Gonadoblastoma** — germ cells + gonadal stroma; almost always in **dysgenetic gonads**; germ cell component can → seminoma.

### 3. Other tumours
- **Testicular lymphoma** — the **most common testicular tumour in men >60 yr**; often bilateral **DLBCL**; high CNS recurrence.
- Metastatic/secondary tumours (rare).
- **Paratesticular:** adenomatoid tumour (benign, mesothelial, upper pole of epididymis — most common benign paratesticular tumour); rhabdomyosarcoma (children), liposarcoma (adults).

### Pathogenesis chain (memorise)
```
Primordial germ cell (acquired differentiation defect)
   → KIT-activating mutations → GCNIS (germ cell neoplasia in situ)
   → i(12p) + other events → Invasive GCT
```
- **GCNIS** found in ~90% of testes with germ cell neoplasms; arises **in utero**, dormant until puberty.
- **~70% of individuals with GCNIS develop invasive GCT within 7 years.**
- **Isochromosome 12p (i(12p))** is **invariably present in invasive GCTs** regardless of histologic type.
- **Klinefelter syndrome → 50× risk of MEDIASTINAL GCT but NO testicular tumours.**

> 🎯 GCT = 95% of testicular tumours · i(12p) in all invasive GCTs · "Any solid testicular mass = malignant until proven otherwise."

## Q2. What is cryptorchidism?

### Definition
**Cryptorchidism = failure of the testis to descend from the abdomen into the scrotum.**
- Seen in **~1% of 1-year-old boys**; usually **unilateral** (bilateral in 25%).
- Most arrested testes lie **in the inguinal canal**; abdominal arrest only **5–10%**.

### Two phases of normal descent
1. **Transabdominal phase** — controlled by **müllerian-inhibiting substance (MIS)**.
2. **Inguinoscrotal phase** — **androgen-dependent**, mediated by androgen-induced **calcitonin gene–related peptide (CGRP)** from the genitofemoral nerve.

### Histologic changes (begin by ~2 years of age)
```
Basement membrane thickening → loss of spermatogonia
   → Sertoli-cell-only tubules → hyaline cords
```
- The abnormally high temperature progressively damages the germinal epithelium.
- **Leydig cells are spared** (appear prominent) → androgen production usually maintained.
- Similar changes may occur in the **contralateral descended testis** → cryptorchidism is a **marker of an intrinsic defect in gonadal development** (part of the **testicular dysgenesis syndrome**: cryptorchidism + hypospadias + poor sperm quality).

### Consequences
1. **Sterility** — loss of spermatogenesis.
2. **3- to 5-fold higher risk of testicular cancer** (most commonly seminoma) — persists even after orchiopexy → lifelong self-examination.
3. **Inguinal hernia** (10–20%) + increased trauma/torsion risk.

### Treatment
**Orchiopexy at 6–12 months** — 🔴 reduces but does **NOT eliminate** the cancer/sterility risk.

> 🎯 Cryptorchidism = sterility + 3–5× cancer risk · orchiopexy at 6–12 months.

## Q3. Short note on Seminoma.

### Definition & epidemiology
Seminoma is the **most common testicular germ cell tumour (~50%)**, reproducing the morphology of **primordial germ cells / early gonocytes**. Peak incidence **4th decade**. The classic "good-prognosis" GCT.

### Gross & microscopic features
- Cut surface: **homogeneous gray-white, lobulated**; 🔴 **hemorrhage and necrosis conspicuously absent** (gross contrast with NSGCT).
- Histology: **sheets/nests of uniform cells** with **clear cytoplasm and distinct cell borders** ("fried-egg" cells), separated by **fibrous septa carrying a lymphocytic infiltrate**, sometimes with **epithelioid granulomas**.
- Usually well circumscribed; **does NOT penetrate the tunica albuginea**.

### Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
🔴 **KIT (CD117)+, OCT3/4+, podoplanin+ (D2-40); cytokeratin (CK) NEGATIVE** — the exact reverse of embryonal carcinoma (OCT3/4+, CK+, KIT−, podoplanin−).

### Tumour markers
- **~15% of seminomas contain syncytiotrophoblasts → mild hCG elevation.**
- 🔴 **AFP is NEVER elevated in pure seminoma** — an elevated AFP means a non-seminomatous (yolk sac) component is present somewhere.

### Spread & prognosis
- Spread mainly **lymphatic** → retroperitoneal **para-aortic** nodes → mediastinal → supraclavicular; **hematogenous spread is late**.
- **Prognosis = the best of all testicular tumours** — highly radiosensitive + chemosensitive; **>95% of stage I–II cases are cured**.
- Synonyms: ovary = **dysgerminoma**; CNS midline = **germinoma**.

> 🎯 Seminoma = KIT/OCT3/4/podoplanin +, CK − · "AFP elevated ⇒ NOT pure seminoma."

## Q4. Short note on Teratoma.

### Definition
A germ cell tumour containing **derivatives of more than one germ layer** (endoderm, mesoderm, ectoderm) — neural tissue, muscle, cartilage, bone, squamous epithelium with skin adnexa, thyroid-like follicles, bronchial and gut epithelium — in a fibrous/myxoid stroma. Elements may be **mature or immature**.

### Prepubertal vs postpubertal — the critical contrast
🔴 Whether a teratoma is benign or malignant depends **not on histology but on the age of the patient (i.e., the genetic background):**

| Feature | Prepubertal | Postpubertal |
|---|---|---|
| Behaviour | **BENIGN** | **MALIGNANT** |
| GCNIS / i(12p) | **Absent** (no germ-cell neoplastic pathway) | **Present** (i(12p)) — invasive GCT pathway |
| Included forms | **Dermoid cysts** (hair, teeth, skin); can mix with yolk sac tumour | 5–10 cm heterogeneous cut surface (cysts, cartilage) |
| Prognosis | Excellent after excision | Malignant — may metastasize |

### Teratoma with somatic-type malignancy
- A **secondary non-germ-cell cancer** (SCC, mucinous adenocarcinoma, sarcoma) arises within the teratoma.
- **Chemoresistant** — cure only by complete surgical resection.
- **Retains i(12p)** → proves clonal origin from the teratoma.

> 🎯 "Prepubertal teratoma = benign; postpubertal teratoma = malignant — the genetics, not the tissue, decide."

## Q5. How does seminoma differ from non-seminomatous testicular tumour (NSGCT)?

Both arise from the same precursor (GCNIS) and both carry **i(12p)** — they differ in differentiation, gross appearance, markers, invasiveness, and prognosis.

| Feature | **Seminoma** | **NSGCT** (e.g., embryonal carcinoma) |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | **Most common GCT (~50%)** | Less common as pure; often mixed |
| Peak age | **4th decade** | **20–30 yr** (a decade earlier) |
| Cut surface | Homogeneous **gray-white, lobulated**; no hemorrhage/necrosis | **Variegated — hemorrhage + necrosis** |
| Histology | Sheets of uniform cells, clear cytoplasm, distinct borders, fibrous septa + **lymphocytic infiltrate** (± granulomas) | Alveolar/tubular/papillary patterns; **large anaplastic cells**, indistinct borders |
| Invasion | Usually does **NOT penetrate tunica albuginea** | Locally aggressive — through **tunica albuginea → epididymis/spermatic cord** |
| IHC | **KIT+, OCT3/4+, podoplanin+; CK −** | **OCT3/4+, CK+; KIT −, podoplanin −** |
| Markers | ~15% mild hCG (syncytiotrophoblasts); **AFP never** | **AFP + hCG elevated in >80%** (yolk sac → AFP; choriocarcinoma → hCG) |
| Spread | Nodal (para-aortic), **late** hematogenous | **Earlier + more frequent** lymphatic & hematogenous |
| Prognosis | **Best** — radiosensitive + chemosensitive | **More aggressive** (choriocarcinoma worst) |

### Clinical relevance
- Seminoma is exquisitely **radiosensitive** — often curable with surgery + RT/chemo even when node-positive.
- NSGCT treated primarily with **chemotherapy (BEP)**; less radiosensitive; metastasize earlier.
- 🔴 **AFP elevation immediately proves the tumour is NOT a pure seminoma.**

> 🎯 "Both have i(12p); the difference is differentiation, invasiveness, markers, and chemo/radiosensitivity."

## Q6. Name the testicular tumour markers and their uses.

| Marker | Produced by | Clinical value |
|---|---|---|
| **hCG** | **Syncytiotrophoblastic elements** — **choriocarcinoma (markedly elevated)**; ~15% of seminomas have syncytiotrophoblasts → mild elevation | Marker of choriocarcinoma / syncytiotrophoblast |
| **AFP** | **Yolk sac tumour** (eosinophilic hyaline globules; also α1-antitrypsin) | Marker of yolk sac component |
| **LDH** | Correlates with **tumour burden** (mass of tumour cells) | Prognosis / bulk |

### Key facts
- 🔴 **Both AFP and hCG are elevated in >80% of NSGCTs at diagnosis.**
- Pure seminoma can show **mild hCG rise** but **never AFP rise** — elevated AFP excludes pure seminoma.
- Markers have **no role in screening**; their value is in managing established disease.

### Uses
1. **Diagnosis / tumour typing** — the marker pattern hints at components before histology.
2. **Staging** — 🔴 persistent elevation after orchiectomy = residual/metastatic disease (half-lives: hCG ~24–36 h, AFP ~5–7 days).
3. **Assessing tumour burden** — LDH rises with total tumour mass.
4. **Monitoring therapy & follow-up** — a rising marker signals recurrence before imaging.

> 🎯 "AFP = yolk sac, hCG = syncytiotrophoblast (choriocarcinoma), LDH = tumour load."

# Part B — Prostate (Q7–12)

## Q7. What are the diseases of the prostate?

| Category | Diseases |
|---|---|
| **1. Inflammatory** | **Prostatitis** — acute bacterial, chronic bacterial, chronic abacterial (chronic pelvic pain syndrome), granulomatous |
| **2. Hyperplastic** | **BPH** — most common benign disease in men >50 yr |
| **3. Neoplastic** | **Prostatic adenocarcinoma** (most common), ductal adenocarcinoma, small-cell (neuroendocrine) carcinoma, sarcomas (rare) |
| **4. Secondary/metastatic** | **Urothelial (bladder) cancer** — most common secondary tumour of the prostate |
| **5. Calcification/stones** | **Corpora amylacea / prostatic calculi** — common, incidental |

### Prostatitis — 4 types (quick)
- **Acute bacterial:** *E. coli* & gram-negatives; fever, dysuria, exquisitely tender boggy gland; 🔴 **biopsy CONTRAINDICATED (risk of sepsis)**.
- **Chronic bacterial:** recurrent UTIs from the same organism; antibiotics penetrate poorly.
- **Chronic abacterial (chronic pelvic pain syndrome):** 🔴 **the most common form**; identical symptoms, **>10 leukocytes/HPF** in secretions but **cultures negative**.
- **Granulomatous:** most common US cause = **BCG instillation** for bladder cancer; fungal in immunocompromised.

### Anatomy reminder (why zones matter)
- Prostate has **4 zones — peripheral, central, transition, periurethral**.
- 🔴 **Most hyperplasia → transition zone; most carcinoma → peripheral zone** ("Peripheral = carcinoma, Transition = hyperplasia").

---

## Q8. What are the tumours of the prostate?

Primary prostate tumours are almost always **epithelial**; by far the most important is **acinar (usual) adenocarcinoma**.

| Tumour | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Prostatic adenocarcinoma** | **Most common**; most common cancer in US men; ~70% peripheral zone; Gleason grading; osteoblastic bone mets |
| **Ductal adenocarcinoma** | Periurethral large-duct lesions mimic **urothelial cancer** (hematuria + obstruction); overall **poorer prognosis** |
| **Small-cell (neuroendocrine) carcinoma** | 🔴 **Most aggressive variant** — rapidly fatal; emerges after antiandrogen therapy (androgen-independent clone) |
| **Squamous / adenosquamous** | After hormone therapy or de novo |
| **Colloid (mucinous) carcinoma** | >25% mucinous secretions |
| **Secondary (urothelial) carcinoma** | **Most common secondary tumour** — direct invasion from bladder, or bladder CIS extending down prostatic ducts/acini |
| **Mesenchymal / sarcoma** | Rare; rhabdomyosarcoma (children), leiomyosarcoma (adults) |

### Precursor — PIN (prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia)
- Architecturally benign large branching acini lined by **atypical cells with prominent nucleoli** (cytologically carcinoma), but 🔴 **retaining (at least partly) a basal cell layer + intact basement membrane**.
- Present in **~80%** of prostates removed for carcinoma; same (peripheral) zone as cancer.

> 🎯 Most important: **adenocarcinoma** (vast majority), **small-cell** (most lethal), **urothelial** (most common secondary).

## Q9. Give the pathogenesis of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH).

### Hormonal driver
BPH is a **hormonally driven proliferation** of the transition-zone glands and stroma. The key player is **dihydrotestosterone (DHT)**, the most potent prostatic androgen, with a supporting role for **estrogens**.

### The DHT pathway
```
Testosterone → (type 2 5α-reductase, mainly in STROMAL cells) → DHT
```
- **Type 2 5α-reductase** is expressed mainly in **stromal cells**; **type 1** produces DHT in liver/skin (blood-borne extra source).
- DHT binds the **androgen receptor (AR)** with higher affinity and forms a more stable complex → efficient nuclear translocation.
- The DHT–AR signal **upregulates the FGF family + TGFβ**: stromal-cell **FGFs = paracrine drivers of epithelial growth**; **TGFβ** mitogenic for fibroblasts, inhibitory for epithelium — a classic stromal–epithelial interaction.

### The estrogen side
- With ageing the relative **estrogen level rises** and tips the balance toward proliferation: 🔴 **ERα is proliferative, ERβ is antiproliferative**.
- Mechanisms: effects on apoptosis, aromatase, PGE2 paracrine signalling.

### Epidemiology
- Most common benign prostatic disease in men **>50 yr**; ~30% of white American men have moderate–severe symptoms.
- 🔴 **Histologic BPH in up to 90% of men by age 80.**
- 🔴 **BPH is NOT a premalignant lesion** — it does not evolve into carcinoma.

> 🎯 DHT (via type 2 5α-reductase in stroma) + estrogen excess → transition-zone nodular hyperplasia.

---

## Q10. Write down the morphology of BPH.

### Gross
- Prostate weight increases **3–5× (60–100 g**; normal ~20 g).
- 🔴 **Transition + periurethral zones** are affected → lateral lobes compress the urethra into a **slit-like orifice** (hence obstruction).
- Cut surface shows discrete **nodules**: **glandular** (yellow-pink, soft, exuding milky fluid) vs **fibromuscular** (pale gray, firm).

### Microscopy
- Small-to-cystic glands, each lined by **two cell layers** — inner columnar secretory + outer cuboidal/flattened **basal** (🔴 the key difference from carcinoma, which lacks a basal layer).
- Glands show **papillary infoldings**; stroma is bland spindle cells (fibromuscular).
- **Corpora amylacea** (laminated inspissated secretions) common.
- Marked enlargement → **prostatic infarcts** with adjacent **squamous metaplasia**.

### Clinical consequences
- Bladder outlet obstruction → **bladder hypertrophy, trabeculation, diverticula**, distension, **residual urine** (source of infection).
- Symptoms: **frequency, nocturia, hesitancy, overflow dribbling, dysuria**; ↑ risk of bladder/kidney infections; acute retention requiring catheterization.

> 🎯 BPH = transition zone + two cell layers + papillary infoldings; carcinoma = peripheral zone + single layer + no basal layer.

---

## Q11. Difference between benign prostatic hyperplasia and malignant prostatic hyperplasia (carcinoma).

| Feature | **BPH** | **Prostatic carcinoma** |
|---|---|---|
| **Zone** | **Transition + periurethral** | **Peripheral zone (~70%)** → palpable on DRE |
| **Age** | >50 yr (histologic in 90% by 80) | Older men; autopsy 20% in 50s → 70% at 70–80 |
| **Nature** | **Benign**, NOT premalignant | **Malignant** — invasive + metastatic |
| **Gross** | Nodular enlargement, soft | Gritty, firm, ill-defined |
| **Gland layers** | **Two cell layers** (basal + luminal), papillary infoldings | **Single layer**, tightly packed, **no basal layer** |
| **Nuclei** | Bland, no nucleoli | Enlarged, **prominent nucleoli** |
| **Perineural invasion** | Absent | **Specific feature** |
| **IHC** | Basal markers (p63/34βE12) **positive**; AMACR negative | Basal markers **negative**; **AMACR + (82–100%)** |
| **PSA** | Mildly ↑ (with gland enlargement) | ↑ (localized & advanced) — organ-specific, not cancer-specific |
| **Spread** | None (local obstruction) | Perineural, seminal vesicles, **osteoblastic bone mets** |
| **Grading** | — | Gleason score + Grade Groups |
| **Treatment** | α-blockers, 5α-reductase inhibitors, TURP | Surgery/radiation ± hormonal; androgen deprivation for mets |

### The one feature that decides everything
🔴 **BPH glands retain the basal cell layer; carcinoma glands lose it** — the morphological rule AND the basis of IHC (basal markers p63/34βE12 vs AMACR). Anatomically, BPH is central and obstructs; cancer is peripheral and is usually asymptomatic (found on DRE or PSA).

> 🎯 "Presence/absence of the basal layer = the key morphological and IHC distinction."

## Q12. Short note on Prostate Specific Antigen (PSA).

### What is PSA?
PSA is a protein produced almost exclusively by **prostatic epithelium**. It is normally present in semen as an **androgen-regulated serine protease that liquefies the seminal coagulum**; only minute amounts leak into the blood.

### When PSA rises
- Rises in **prostate cancer** (localized AND advanced), but also in benign conditions that damage/stimulate the prostate: **BPH, acute/chronic prostatitis, prostatic infarction, instrumentation (biopsy/cystoscopy), and recent ejaculation**.
- 🔴 **PSA is organ-specific but NOT cancer-specific** → an elevated level is a clue, not a diagnosis; sensitivity/specificity for screening are suboptimal.

### The screening controversy
- PSA screening detects many **clinically insignificant** cancers → **overdiagnosis + overtreatment** (incontinence/impotence risks).
- The **UK recommends AGAINST routine PSA screening**; other bodies advise shared decision-making.
- 🔴 **Serial PSA after diagnosis = excellent for monitoring recurrence/progression** — a rising PSA predicts relapse before symptoms/imaging.
- Localized cancer is usually asymptomatic → found on a **DRE nodule or elevated PSA** → confirmed by **transrectal US-guided needle biopsy**.

### Treatment context
- Localized high-risk → radical prostatectomy or radiation ± hormonal (>90% live 15 yr); low-volume low-grade → **active surveillance**.
- Metastatic → **androgen deprivation** (orchiectomy / LHRH agonists) → eventually castration-resistant (disease progression + death).

> 🎯 "PSA = organ-specific, NOT cancer-specific — good for monitoring, controversial for screening."

# Part C — Semen Analysis & Male Infertility (Q13–22)

## Q13. What are the indications of semen analysis?

Semen analysis = laboratory examination of the ejaculate (volume, sperm concentration/count, motility, morphology, vitality, pH, liquefaction) standardized against **WHO reference values**.

1. **Male factor infertility** — the standard first investigation when a couple fails to conceive (usually after ~1 year of unprotected intercourse).
2. **Post-vasectomy confirmation** — to verify azoospermia (requires 2 consecutive negative samples before declaring sterility).
3. **After vasectomy reversal / orchidopexy** — to confirm re-establishment of sperm flow.
4. **Assessment of gonadal toxicity** — after chemotherapy, radiotherapy, occupational/heat exposure.
5. **Pre-IVF/ICSI planning** — results decide IUI vs IVF vs ICSI.
6. **Sperm donor screening** — quality control of donor samples.
7. **Medicolegal / forensic** cases — detection and confirmation of semen.

---

## Q14. How is the specimen collected for semen analysis?

- Method: **masturbation** into a **sterile, wide-mouthed, non-toxic container**, ideally at the laboratory (no transport delay).
- **No lubricants or condoms** — most lubricants are spermicidal; plain sterile container only.
- 🔴 **The complete sample must be collected** — the first (sperm-rich) fraction carries the majority of sperm; losing it invalidates the test.

### Timing & transport
- Deliver **within 1 hour**, kept at **20–37 °C** during transport — cold or heat destroys motility.
- Label with **name, date, time of collection and abstinence period**.
- An abnormal first result must be **confirmed by a repeat test 2–4 weeks apart** (2 samples minimum).

> 🎯 "The first portion of the ejaculate contains most of the sperm — losing it is the commonest cause of a falsely low count."

---

## Q15. Mention the importance of semen analysis.

- **Cornerstone of male fertility evaluation** — the first and most informative test for the infertile couple.
- **Diagnoses the cause of male infertility** — objectively identifies oligospermia, azoospermia, asthenozoospermia, teratozoospermia, necrospermia, etc.
- **Confirms the success of vasectomy** — showing azoospermia is essential before stopping contraception.
- **Guides the choice of treatment** — normal parameters → timed intercourse/IUI; severe defects → IVF/ICSI.
- **Monitors therapy** — varicocele repair, hormonal treatment, recovery after chemotherapy.
- **Screens sperm donors** — quality control.
- **Medicolegal value** — detection of semen in forensic cases.

> 🎯 A **single abnormal report must be repeated** because of day-to-day variability.

---

## Q16. What are the prerequisites of semen analysis?

- **Abstinence: 2–7 days** (ideally 2–3 days). 🔴 Too long an abstinence → low motility + more dead/abnormal forms; too short → low count.
- Avoid **alcohol, drugs, recent febrile illness** — fever transiently suppresses spermatogenesis; because spermatogenesis takes ~74 days, the effect appears ~2–3 months later and takes that long to recover.
- **No lubricants / spermicidal agents.**
- **Complete collection** — first (sperm-rich) fraction must not be lost.
- **Sterile, wide-mouthed, non-toxic container** (ideally pre-weighed).
- Deliver **within 1 hour at 20–37 °C**.
- Proper **labeling** — name, date, time, abstinence period.
- **Repeat samples** if abnormal — 2 samples, 2–4 weeks apart.

> ⚠️ Wrong abstinence, lost first fraction, or delay >1 hour invalidate the result.

---

## Q17. Write a normal semen analysis report.

| Parameter | WHO 2021 (6th ed.) | WHO 2010 (5th ed.) |
|---|---|---|
| **Volume** | ≥ 1.4 mL | ≥ 1.5 mL |
| **pH** | ≥ 7.2 | ≥ 7.2 |
| **Liquefaction** | Within 60 min | Within 60 min |
| **Sperm concentration** | ≥ 16 million/mL | ≥ 15 million/mL |
| **Total sperm number** | ≥ 39 million/ejaculate | ≥ 39 million/ejaculate |
| **Total motility** | ≥ 42% | ≥ 40% |
| **Progressive motility (PR)** | ≥ 30% | ≥ 32% |
| **Vitality** | ≥ 54% | ≥ 58% |
| **Normal morphology** | ≥ 4% | ≥ 4% |
| **Leukocytes** | < 1 million/mL | < 1 million/mL |

### How to read the report
- Low volume (hypo-/aspermia) → short abstinence or retrograde ejaculation; low count = oligospermia; low motility = asthenozoospermia; low normal forms = teratozoospermia; raised leukocytes → infection/prostatitis.
- 🔴 A value below the lower limit on **two consecutive samples** is abnormal — never report a man infertile on a single sample.

> 🎯 **Memory: "15, 39, 40, 32, 58, 4"** — concentration 15 M/mL, total 39 M, total motility 40%, progressive 32%, vitality 58%, morphology 4%.

---

## Q18. Define: Oligospermia, Azoospermia, Necrospermia.

- **Oligospermia (oligozoospermia):** sperm concentration below the lower reference limit — **<15 million/mL** (WHO 2010) / **<16 million/mL** (WHO 2021). "Few sperm" — does not distinguish production vs transport problem.
- **Azoospermia:** **no spermatozoa in the ejaculate** — confirmed on the **centrifuged deposit**, on **≥2 samples**. Divided into **obstructive** (normal production, blocked ducts) vs **non-obstructive** (testicular failure — Klinefelter, Sertoli-only, post-chemo).
- **Necrospermia (necrozoospermia):** all (or nearly all) spermatozoa are **dead** — vitality below reference (**<54%** WHO 2021 / **<58%** WHO 2010). Interpret carefully — an immotile sperm may simply be dead in the sample rather than in the body.

| Term | Essential point |
|---|---|
| **Oligospermia** | Too few sperm (concentration ↓) |
| **Azoospermia** | No sperm at all (obstructive or testicular failure) |
| **Necrospermia** | Sperm present but all/nearly all dead |

> **Related terms:** aspermia (no ejaculate), hypospermia (low volume), cryptozoospermia (sperm only after centrifugation).

---

## Q19. Give the properties of normozoospermia.

**Normozoospermia** = a sample in which **all parameters are within WHO reference ranges**.

- **Physical:** volume ≥1.5 mL (2010)/≥1.4 mL (2021); pH ≥7.2; liquefies within 60 min (normally 15–30 min); opalescent/whitish.
- **Count:** concentration ≥15 million/mL (2010)/≥16 million/mL (2021); total ≥39 million/ejaculate.
- **Motility:** total ≥40% (2021: ≥42%); progressive (PR) ≥32% (2021: ≥30%). Graded PR/NP/IM.
- **Vitality:** ≥58% live (2021: ≥54%) — eosin/nigrosin or hypoosmotic swelling.
- **Morphology:** normal forms ≥4% by **strict (Kruger) criteria**.
- **Extraneous:** leukocytes <1 million/mL; no significant agglutination; culture-negative; no blood.

> 🎯 "Every parameter normal = normozoospermia. Any single value below its limit renames the sample."

---

## Q20. What is: Normozoospermia, Hyperzoospermia, Teratozoospermia, Asthenozoospermia?

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Normozoospermia** | All semen parameters within WHO reference values (normal) |
| **Hyperzoospermia** | Sperm concentration **above the upper reference** (commonly >250 million/mL) |
| **Teratozoospermia** | **Abnormal sperm morphology** — normal forms <4% (strict Kruger criteria) |
| **Asthenozoospermia** | **Reduced sperm motility** — total motility <40% (2010) / <42% (2021); progressive <30–32% |

- Concentration, motility and morphology are the three "essential" sperm parameters.
- They are **often reduced together** → **oligoasthenoteratozoospermia (OAT)** = the commonest abnormal pattern.

> 🎯 OAT = all three defects together = commonest abnormal pattern.

## Q21. Causes of male infertility.

Classified by **level of the defect** — pretesticular / testicular / post-testicular.

### 1. Pretesticular (hypothalamo–pituitary–gonadal)
- **Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism** — Kallmann syndrome (anosmia + GnRH deficiency), pituitary tumours, trauma, radiotherapy.
- **Hyperprolactinemia**, thyroid/adrenal disease.
- **Drugs:** anabolic steroids, opiates, spironolactone, cimetidine, ketoconazole, alcohol, marijuana.

### 2. Testicular (intrinsic / primary gonadal failure)
- **Chromosomal:** **Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)** — the commonest genetic cause of azoospermia (small firm testes, gynecomastia, tall stature).
- **Varicocele** — dilated pampiniform plexus; venous stasis raises scrotal temperature; 🔴 **most common correctable cause**.
- **Cryptorchidism**, torsion, trauma.
- **Orchitis** — especially **mumps orchitis** after puberty; irradiation and chemotherapy.
- **Sertoli-cell-only syndrome**, maturation arrest, anorchia.
- Systemic disease: cirrhosis, CRF, malnutrition, febrile illness.

### 3. Post-testicular (ductal obstruction / ejaculatory)
- **Obstructive azoospermia:** CBAVD (congenital bilateral absence of vas deferens — **CFTR mutations**), epididymal obstruction (post-infection), ejaculatory duct obstruction.
- **Vasectomy** — commonest intentional obstruction.
- **Retrograde ejaculation** — diabetic neuropathy, prostatectomy, α-blockers, spinal cord injury.
- **Immunologic:** antisperm antibodies → agglutination, poor penetration.
- Sexual/ejaculatory dysfunction, hypospadias, erectile dysfunction.

> 🎯 Fertility needs (a) gonadotrophin drive, (b) intact spermatogenesis, (c) patent ducts + forward ejaculation.

## Q22. How will you investigate a case of male infertility?

Follows a logical order: **history → repeated semen analysis → hormones → imaging → genetics → biopsy**.

1. **History + clinical examination** — drugs, past fever, surgery (hernia, vasectomy), occupational/thermal exposure; testicular volume (small firm testes → Klinefelter), palpate for varicocele, gynaecomastia.
2. **Repeat semen analysis** — 2 samples, 2–4 weeks apart; confirm azoospermia on centrifuged deposit.
3. **Hormonal profile** — **FSH, LH, testosterone, prolactin**. 🔴 **High FSH + small testes → primary testicular failure; low FSH/LH + low testosterone → hypogonadotropic (pretesticular).**
4. **Imaging** — scrotal US + Doppler (varicocele, mass, epididymis); transrectal US (ejaculatory duct obstruction); pituitary MRI when FSH/LH low.
5. **Post-ejaculatory urine** — sperm in urine confirms **retrograde ejaculation**.
6. **Genetic testing** — karyotype (Klinefelter 47,XXY), **CFTR** (CBAVD), **Y-chromosome microdeletions (AZF)** before ICSI.
7. **Testicular biopsy** — the key test in azoospermia: distinguishes **obstructive** (normal spermatogenesis — sperm retrievable by PESA/TESE) from **non-obstructive** (Sertoli-only or maturation arrest).
8. **Antisperm antibodies** — MAR test (after vasectomy reversal / unexplained infertility).
9. **Specialized** — sperm DNA fragmentation index, functional tests (ICSI planning).

> 🎯 "FSH is the key discriminator: normal FSH + azoospermia → obstruction; high FSH → primary testicular failure."
