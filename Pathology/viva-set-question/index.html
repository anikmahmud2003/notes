Answer — Classification of Testicular Tumours

GCTs = ~95% of testicular tumours; the most common cancer in Caucasian males aged 15–45 yr. Any solid testicular mass is treated as malignant until proven otherwise — a biopsy is avoided because of the risk of tumour spillage, and a radical orchiectomy is the standard first step.

1. Germ cell tumours (GCT) — WHO 2016

These arise from the germ cells (the cells that would normally form sperm). Two broad families exist based on which developmental stage the tumour cells resemble: seminomatous (like early germ cells) and non-seminomatous (like embryonic stem cells or cells differentiated toward yolk sac/placenta/mature tissues).

  • ~70% of individuals with GCNIS develop an invasive GCT within 7 years.
  • i(12p) (isochromosome of the short arm of chromosome 12) is invariably present in invasive GCTs, whatever the histologic type — it is the key cytogenetic hallmark.

Q2Testis · Congenital
What is cryptorchidism?
Tap to reveal

Answer — Cryptorchidism (undescended testis)

Cryptorchidism = failure of the testis to descend from the abdomen into the scrotum. It is seen in about 1% of 1-year-old boys (premature infants have a higher rate), and it is usually unilateral (bilateral in 25%). Most arrested testes lie in the inguinal canal; pure abdominal (intra-abdominal) arrest occurs in only 5–10%.

Normal descent — two phases
  • Transabdominal phase — controlled by müllerian-inhibiting substance (MIS); the testis moves from the abdomen toward the internal ring.
  • Inguinoscrotal phase — androgen-dependent, mediated by androgen-induced calcitonin gene–related peptide (CGRP) released from the genitofemoral nerve; the testis travels through the canal into the scrotum.
Histologic changes (begin by ~2 years of age)
Basement-membrane thickening→ Loss of spermatogonia→ Sertoli-cell-only tubules→ Hyaline cords
  • The abnormal temperature of the undescended testis progressively damages the germinal epithelium. Leydig cells are spared (they appear prominent), so androgen production is usually maintained.
  • Similar changes can occur in the contralateral descended testis — this is why cryptorchidism is regarded as a marker of an intrinsic defect in gonadal development, not just a mechanical problem.
Consequences
  • Sterility — loss of spermatogenesis; the longer the testis remains undescended, the greater the damage.
  • 3- to 5-fold higher risk of testicular cancer (most commonly seminoma) — even after orchiopexy the risk remains increased, so lifelong self-examination is advised.
  • Associated inguinal hernia (10–20%) and increased risk of trauma and torsion.
  • Treatment: orchiopexy at 6–12 months — reduces but does NOT eliminate the cancer/sterility risk.
Mechanism in brief
The undescended testis is exposed to an abnormally high temperature. Over time the tubular basement membrane thickens, spermatogonia die off leaving Sertoli-cell-only tubules (hence sterility), while the persisting immature germ cells can transform into GCNIS and later seminoma. Because the whole germ-cell population is abnormal, even the normally descended fellow testis may be affected.
Source: Robbins & Kumar Basic Pathology, Ch. 21 (Testis).
Q3Testis · Seminoma
Short note on Seminoma.
Tap to reveal

Answer — Seminoma

Seminoma is the most common testicular germ cell tumour (~50%). It reproduces the morphology of primordial germ cells / early gonocytes and is the classic "good-prognosis" GCT.

Epidemiology
  • Peak incidence 4th decade (about a decade later than embryonal carcinoma).
  • Risk factors overlap with GCTs in general — cryptorchidism, prior GCT, family history.
Gross & microscopic features
  • Cut surface: homogeneous gray-white and lobulated; hemorrhage and necrosis are conspicuously absent (an important gross contrast to NSGCTs).
  • Histology: sheets and nests of uniform cells with clear cytoplasm and distinct cell borders (the classic "fried-egg" appearance), separated by fibrous septa carrying a lymphocytic infiltrate, sometimes with epithelioid granulomas.
  • The tumour is usually well circumscribed and does NOT penetrate the tunica albuginea.
Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
  • KIT (CD117)+, OCT3/4+, podoplanin+ (D2-40); cytokeratin (CK) NEGATIVE.
  • This profile cleanly separates seminoma from embryonal carcinoma (which is OCT3/4+ but CK+, KIT−, podoplanin−).
Markers
  • ~15% of seminomas contain syncytiotrophoblastic cells → mild hCG elevation.
  • AFP is NEVER elevated in pure seminoma — an elevated AFP means there is a non-seminomatous (yolk sac) component somewhere.
Spread & prognosis
  • Spread is mainly lymphatic — first to the retroperitoneal para-aortic nodes, then mediastinal and supraclavicular; hematogenous spread is late.
  • Prognosis is the best of all testicular tumours — highly radiosensitive and chemosensitive; >95% of stage I–II cases are cured.
  • Synonyms: in the ovary the equivalent is dysgerminoma; in the CNS midline it is called germinoma.
Memory hook: seminoma = KIT/OCT3/4/podoplanin +, CK − — the exact reverse of embryonal carcinoma.
Source: Robbins & Kumar Basic Pathology, Ch. 21 (Testicular GCT).
Q4Testis · Teratoma

Answer — Testicular Teratoma

A teratoma is a germ cell tumour that contains derivatives of more than one germ layer (endoderm, mesoderm, ectoderm) — e.g., neural tissue, muscle, cartilage, bone, squamous epithelium with skin adnexa, thyroid-like follicles, bronchial and gut epithelium — all embedded in a fibrous or myxoid stroma. The elements may be mature or immature (fetal-type tissue).

Prepubertal vs postpubertal — the critical contrast

Whether a teratoma is benign or malignant depends not on its microscopic appearance but on the age of the patient, i.e., on its genetic background:

FeaturePrepubertalPostpubertal
BehaviourBENIGNMALIGNANT

Included formsDermoid cysts (hair, teeth, skin); can mix with yolk sac tumour5–10 cm heterogeneous cut surface (cysts, cartilage)

Teratoma with somatic-type malignancy
  • In a small proportion, a secondary non-germ-cell cancer arises within the teratoma — e.g., squamous cell carcinoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma, or sarcoma.
  • Such tumours are chemoresistant — cure is only possible by complete surgical resection.
  • They retain i(12p), proving they are clonally derived from the teratoma and not an independent primary.
Mechanism in brief
In the prepubertal testis, teratomas arise from germ cells that do not enter the GCNIS/i(12p) pathway — they are effectively benign developmental tumours. In the postpubertal testis, the teratoma shares the invasive GCT genotype (GCNIS + i(12p)), so even histologically "mature" elements carry malignant potential — the genetics, not the tissue of differentiation, decide the behaviour.
Source: Robbins & Kumar Basic Pathology, Ch. 21 (Teratoma).

Serum tumour markers are proteins secreted by specific tumour components. In testicular GCTs they are used to identify the tumour type, stage the disease, monitor response, and detect recurrence.

Key facts to remember
  • Both AFP and hCG are elevated in >80% of NSGCTs at diagnosis.

  • Markers do not have a role in screening; their main value is in the management of established disease.

  • Diagnosis / tumour typing — the marker pattern hints at the tumour components before histology is available.
  • Staging — persistent elevation after orchiectomy = residual or metastatic disease; the half-lives (hCG ~24–36 h, AFP ~5–7 days) guide interpretation.
  • Assessing tumour burden — LDH rises with the total mass of tumour.
  • Monitoring therapy and follow-up — a rising marker after treatment signals recurrence before it is visible on imaging.
Memory: AFP = yolk sac; hCG = syncytiotrophoblast (choriocarcinoma); LDH = tumour load.

Answer — Diseases of the Prostate

The prostate is affected by a spectrum of diseases — inflammatory, hyperplastic, neoplastic and secondary. Recognizing the category is the first step in clinical evaluation, and each category has a distinct age, presentation and morphology.

CategoryDiseases
1. InflammatoryProstatitis — acute bacterial, chronic bacterial, chronic abacterial (chronic pelvic pain syndrome), granulomatous

3. NeoplasticProstatic adenocarcinoma (most common), ductal adenocarcinoma, small-cell (neuroendocrine) carcinoma, sarcomas (rare)

Prostatitis — 4 types (quick)
  • Acute bacterial: E. coli & other gram-negatives; fever, dysuria, exquisitely tender boggy gland; biopsy CONTRAINDICATED (risk of sepsis).
  • Chronic bacterial: recurrent UTIs from the same organism; antibiotics penetrate the prostate poorly, so infection persists and seeds the urinary tract.
  • Chronic abacterial: the most common form; identical symptoms but no UTI history, >10 leukocytes/HPF in secretions with negative cultures.
  • Granulomatous: most common US cause = BCG instillation for bladder cancer; also fungal in immunocompromised patients.
Anatomy reminder (why zones matter)
  • The prostate has no true capsule and is divided into 4 zones — peripheral, central, transition, periurethral.
  • Most hyperplasia → transition zone; most carcinoma → peripheral zone. This anatomical split is the basis of Q11 (BPH vs cancer).

Q9Prostate · BPH
Give the pathogenesis of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH).
Tap to reveal

Answer — BPH Pathogenesis (DHT + Estrogens)

BPH is a hormonally driven proliferation of the transition-zone glands and stroma. The key player is dihydrotestosterone (DHT), the most potent androgen of the prostate, with a supporting role for estrogens.

The DHT pathway
Testosterone→ type 2 5α-reductase (stromal cells) → DHT
  • Type 2 5α-reductase is expressed mainly in stromal cells of the prostate; type 1 produces DHT in the liver/skin, giving a blood-borne extra-prostatic source of DHT.
  • DHT binds the androgen receptor (AR) with higher affinity and forms a more stable receptor complex than testosterone → efficient nuclear translocation.
  • The DHT–AR signal upregulates the FGF family and TGFβ: stromal-cell FGFs act as paracrine drivers of epithelial growth, while TGFβ is mitogenic for fibroblasts and inhibits epithelium — a classic stromal–epithelial interaction.
The estrogen side
  • With ageing, relative estrogen levels rise and tip the balance toward proliferation: ERα is proliferative while ERβ is antiproliferative.
  • Mechanisms include effects on apoptosis, aromatase activity and PGE2 paracrine signalling.
Epidemiology
  • Most common benign prostatic disease in men >50 yr; ~30% of white American men have moderate–severe symptoms.
  • Histologic BPH is present in up to 90% of men by age 80.
  • BPH is NOT a premalignant lesion — it does not evolve into carcinoma.
Mechanism in brief
Ageing shifts the androgen/estrogen balance: rising testosterone is converted to DHT in the stroma by type 2 5α-reductase; DHT–AR signaling drives stroma and, via FGF paracrine loops, epithelium to proliferate. Relative estrogen excess further stimulates growth through ERα, giving the mixed glandular + fibromuscular nodules of BPH.
Source: Robbins & Kumar Basic Pathology, Ch. 21 (BPH).
Q10Prostate · BPH

Answer — BPH Morphology

BPH produces nodular enlargement of the prostate, driven by the hormonal mechanisms described in Q9. The morphology is best described at two levels — gross (macroscopic) and microscopic.

Gross
  • Prostate weight increases 3–5× (60–100 g, normal ~20 g).
  • The transition + periurethral zones are affected — the lateral lobes compress the urethra into a slit-like orifice, which is why obstruction is the dominant symptom.
  • On the cut surface there are discrete nodules that vary in composition: glandular nodules (yellow-pink, soft, exuding milky fluid) vs fibromuscular nodules (pale gray, firm).
Microscopy
  • Glands are small to cystic, and each is lined by two cell layers — an inner columnar secretory layer and an outer cuboidal/flattened basal layer (this is the key difference from carcinoma, which lacks a basal layer).
  • The glands show papillary infoldings into their lumina, and the stroma is composed of bland spindle cells (fibromuscular).
  • Corpora amylacea (concentrically laminated inspissated secretions) are common.
  • Marked enlargement may produce prostatic infarcts, with adjacent squamous metaplasia of surviving glands.
Clinical consequences
  • Bladder outlet obstruction → bladder hypertrophy, trabeculation and diverticula, distension and residual urine (a source of infection).
  • Symptoms: frequency, nocturia, hesitancy, overflow dribbling, dysuria; risk of bladder/kidney infections; acute urinary retention requiring catheterization.
Key discriminator vs cancer: BPH glands keep the basal cell layer and are not single-layer glands.
Source: Robbins & Kumar Basic Pathology, Ch. 21 (BPH).

The one feature that decides everything
  • BPH glands retain their basal cell layer; carcinoma glands lose it — this is both the morphological rule and the basis of IHC (basal markers p63/34βE12 vs AMACR).

Q12Prostate · PSA
Short note on Prostate Specific Antigen (PSA).
Tap to reveal

Answer — PSA (Prostate-Specific Antigen)

PSA is a protein produced almost exclusively by prostatic epithelium. It is normally present in semen, where it acts as an androgen-regulated serine protease that liquefies the seminal coagulum after ejaculation; only minute amounts leak into the blood.

When PSA rises
  • It rises in prostate cancer (localized AND advanced), but also in benign conditions that damage or stimulate the prostate: BPH, acute/chronic prostatitis, prostatic infarction, instrumentation (biopsy/cystoscopy), and recent ejaculation.
  • PSA is therefore organ-specific but NOT cancer-specific — an elevated level is a clue, not a diagnosis.
  • Because of this overlap, its sensitivity and specificity as a screening test are suboptimal.
The screening controversy
  • PSA screening detects many cancers that are clinically insignificant — men who would never have been harmed by their tumour → overdiagnosis and overtreatment (with incontinence/impotence risks of therapy).
  • For this reason the UK recommends AGAINST routine PSA screening; other bodies advise shared decision-making.
  • However, serial PSA measurement after diagnosis is excellent for monitoring recurrence and progression — a rising PSA after treatment predicts relapse before symptoms or imaging.
  • Localized cancer is usually asymptomatic and is discovered on a DRE nodule or elevated PSA, then confirmed by transrectal ultrasound-guided needle biopsy.
Treatment context (why PSA matters in management)
  • Localized high-risk → radical prostatectomy or radiation ± hormonal therapy (>90% live 15 yr); low-volume low-grade → active surveillance.
  • Metastatic disease → androgen deprivation (orchiectomy or LHRH agonists), which eventually fails → castration-resistant prostate cancer.
  • PSA level/velocity is used to stratify risk, decide imaging, and judge response to all of these treatments.
Source: Robbins & Kumar Basic Pathology, Ch. 21 (PSA).

Q13Semen · Indications
What are the indications of semen analysis?

Answer — Indications of Semen Analysis

Semen analysis is the laboratory examination of the ejaculate — volume, sperm concentration/count, motility, morphology, vitality, pH and liquefaction — standardized against WHO reference values. It is requested in the following situations:

  • Male factor infertility — the standard first investigation when a couple fails to conceive (usually after ~1 year of unprotected intercourse). It separates male from female causes.
  • Post-vasectomy confirmation — to verify azoospermia after vasectomy (requires 2 consecutive negative samples before declaring sterility).
  • Evaluation after vasectomy reversal / orchidopexy — to confirm re-establishment of sperm flow and monitor recovery.
  • Assessment of gonadal toxicity — after chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or significant environmental/occupational exposure to toxins and heat.
  • Pre-IVF/ICSI planning — results decide the assisted-reproduction strategy (IUI vs IVF vs ICSI).
  • Sperm donor screening — to ensure donor samples meet quality standards for use.
  • Medicolegal / forensic cases — detection and confirmation of semen for legal purposes.
Source: WHO Laboratory Manual for the Examination and Processing of Human Semen, 6th ed., 2021.
Q14Semen · Collection
How is the specimen collected for semen analysis?
Tap to reveal

Answer — Collection of the Specimen

The reliability of a semen report depends completely on correct collection. Small errors — a lost drop, a wrong container, or delay — can turn a normal sample into an apparently abnormal one.

  • Method: masturbation into a sterile, wide-mouthed, non-toxic container, ideally at the laboratory so there is no transport delay.
  • No lubricants or condoms — most lubricants are spermicidal and will kill sperm; plain sterile container only.
  • The complete sample must be collected — the first (sperm-rich) fraction of the ejaculate carries the majority of sperm; losing it invalidates the test.
Timing & transport
  • Deliver to the laboratory within 1 hour of collection, keeping the sample at 20–37 °C (body temperature) during transport — cold or heat destroys motility.
  • Label the container with name, date, time of collection and abstinence period.
  • Because semen shows natural day-to-day variation, an abnormal first result must be confirmed by repeating the test 2–4 weeks apart (2 samples minimum).
The first portion of the ejaculate contains the majority of sperm — losing it is the commonest cause of a falsely low count.
Source: WHO Laboratory Manual, 6th ed., 2021.
Q15Semen · Importance

Answer — Importance of Semen Analysis

Semen analysis is the single most important laboratory test in the evaluation of male fertility, and it also serves several non-fertility purposes.

  • Cornerstone of male fertility evaluation — it is the first and most informative test for the infertile couple; a normal result directs attention to the female partner or to functional/sperm-quality problems.
  • Diagnoses the cause of male infertility — it objectively identifies oligospermia, azoospermia, asthenozoospermia, teratozoospermia, necrospermia, etc., which guides the next steps.
  • Confirms the success of vasectomy — showing azoospermia after vasectomy is essential before the man can be considered sterile and stop contraception.
  • Guides the choice of treatment — normal parameters may allow timed intercourse or IUI; severe oligo/astheno/teratozoospermia leads to IVF or ICSI.
  • Monitors therapy — e.g., improvement after varicocele repair, hormonal treatment, or recovery of spermatogenesis after chemotherapy.
  • Screens sperm donors — quality control of samples used in assisted reproduction.
  • Medicolegal value — detection of semen in forensic cases.
A normal semen analysis is reassuring, but a single abnormal report must be repeated because of day-to-day variability.
Source: WHO Laboratory Manual, 6th ed., 2021.
Q16Semen · Prerequisites
What are the prerequisites of semen analysis?
Tap to reveal

Answer — Prerequisites (Patient Preparation)

For a semen analysis to be valid, the patient must be prepared and the sample collected correctly. If the pre-analytical conditions are wrong, the whole report becomes unreliable regardless of laboratory skill.

  • Abstinence: 2–7 days (ideally 2–3 days) — this controls the "storage" of sperm in the epididymis. Too long an abstinence lowers motility and raises dead/abnormal forms; too short lowers the count, since the epididymis is not re-filled.
  • Avoid alcohol, drugs and recent febrile illness — a fever transiently suppresses spermatogenesis, and because spermatogenesis takes ~74 days to complete, the effect appears ~2–3 months later and takes that long to recover.
  • No lubricants or spermicidal agents during collection — most lubricants kill sperm and would invalidate the test.
  • Complete collection — the first (sperm-rich) fraction must not be lost; losing it falsely lowers the count.
  • Use a sterile, wide-mouthed, non-toxic container (ideally a pre-weighed one) — avoids spillage, contamination and weight errors for volume measurement.
  • Deliver within 1 hour, kept at 20–37 °C during transport — both cold and heat destroy sperm motility.
  • Proper labeling — name, date, time of collection, and abstinence period, so the lab can interpret the sample.
  • Repeat samples if abnormal — 2 samples taken 2–4 weeks apart, because normal day-to-day biological variability can produce a single abnormal result by chance.
Wrong abstinence time, lost first fraction, or delay >1 hour invalidate the result.
Source: WHO Laboratory Manual, 6th ed., 2021.

Memory: "15, 39, 40, 32, 58, 4" — concentration 15 M/mL, total 39 M, total motility 40%, progressive 32%, vitality 58%, morphology 4%.

Define: Oligospermia, Azoospermia, Necrospermia.
Tap to reveal

Answer — Definitions

These terms describe abnormalities of the spermatozoa themselves and are defined against the WHO lower reference limits. The examiner wants the exact definition plus the meaning of each.

  • Oligospermia (oligozoospermia): sperm concentration below the lower reference limit — < 15 million/mL (WHO 2010) / < 16 million/mL (WHO 2021). The term means "few sperm" — it does not distinguish whether the problem is production (testicular) or transport (obstruction).

Q19Semen · Normozoospermia
Give the properties of normozoospermia.
Tap to reveal

Answer — Properties of Normozoospermia

Normozoospermia = a semen sample in which all parameters are within the WHO reference ranges (i.e., a completely normal report). The properties below are the checks the laboratory performs and that define the condition.

  • Physical properties: volume ≥ 1.5 mL (2010) / ≥ 1.4 mL (2021); pH ≥ 7.2 (slightly alkaline); liquefies within 60 min (normally 15–30 min); appearance opalescent/whitish, initially viscous then liquefied.
  • Count: sperm concentration ≥ 15 million/mL (2010) / ≥ 16 million/mL (2021), i.e., total ≥ 39 million per ejaculate.
  • Motility: total motility ≥ 40% (2021: ≥ 42%); progressive motility (PR) ≥ 32% (2021: ≥ 30%). Motility is graded as progressive (PR), non-progressive (NP) or immotile (IM).
  • Vitality: ≥ 58% live sperm (2021: ≥ 54%) — assessed by eosin/nigrosin or hypoosmotic swelling; a low vitality explains low motility.
  • Morphology: normal forms ≥ 4% by strict (Kruger) criteria — measuring head, midpiece and tail defects.
  • Extraneous cells: leukocytes < 1 million/mL; no significant sperm agglutination, no microorganisms (culture-negative), no blood.
Simply put: every parameter normal = normozoospermia. Any single parameter below its limit renames the sample (oligo-, astheno-, terato-zoospermia).

Q21Semen · Infertility causes
Causes of male infertility.

Answer — Causes of Male Infertility

Male infertility is classically divided by the level of the defect: before the testis (hormonal), at the testis (production failure), or after the testis (transport/ejaculation). Most causes fall into the first two groups, but the commonest single identifiable cause is the correctable varicocele.

1. Pretesticular (hypothalamo–pituitary–gonadal)
  • Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism — Kallmann syndrome (anosmia + GnRH deficiency), pituitary tumours, trauma, radiotherapy — too little FSH/LH means the testis is not stimulated.
  • Hyperprolactinemia (prolactinomas suppress LH/FSH), thyroid and adrenal disease.
  • Drugs: anabolic steroids (suppress gonadotrophins), opiates, spironolactone, cimetidine, ketoconazole; alcohol and marijuana.
2. Testicular (intrinsic / primary gonadal failure)
  • Chromosomal: Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) — the commonest genetic cause of azoospermia (small firm testes, gynecomastia, tall stature).
  • Varicocele — dilated pampiniform plexus; venous stasis raises scrotal temperature → impaired spermatogenesis; most common correctable cause.
  • Cryptorchidism (undescended testis — germ-cell damage from abdominal temperature), torsion, trauma.
  • Orchitis — especially mumps orchitis after puberty; irradiation and chemotherapy (gonadotoxic).
  • Sertoli-cell-only syndrome, germ-cell maturation arrest, anorchia ("vanishing testis" syndrome).
  • Systemic disease: cirrhosis, chronic renal failure, malnutrition, and any febrile illness (transient suppression).
3. Post-testicular (ductal obstruction / ejaculatory)
  • Obstructive azoospermia: CBAVD — congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens (CFTR mutations), epididymal obstruction (post-infection), ejaculatory duct obstruction.
  • Vasectomy — the commonest intentional obstruction (reversible by re-anastomosis).
  • Retrograde ejaculation — diabetic neuropathy, prostatectomy, α-blockers, spinal cord injury; sperm pass backwards into the bladder.
  • Immunologic: antisperm antibodies after infection/injury or vasectomy reversal → sperm agglutination and poor penetration.
  • Sexual/ejaculatory dysfunction and penile factors (hypospadias, erectile dysfunction).
Mechanism in brief
Fertility needs (a) gonadotrophin drive (pretesticular), (b) intact spermatogenesis in seminiferous tubules (testicular), and (c) patent ducts plus forward ejaculation (post-testicular). A defect at any level — hormone deficiency, tubular damage (heat, toxins, karyotype), or ductal block — interrupts the delivery of viable sperm.
Source: WHO Manual; Robbins Basic Pathology (Male reproductive system).

How will you investigate a case of male infertility?
Tap to reveal

Answer — Investigation of Male Infertility

The investigation follows a logical order — history → repeated semen analysis → hormones → imaging → genetics → biopsy. The aim is to identify the level of the defect (pretesticular / testicular / post-testicular) so the right treatment or ART can be chosen.

  • History + clinical examination: drug use, past fever, surgery (hernia, vasectomy), occupational/thermal exposure; examine testicular volume (small firm testes → Klinefelter), palpate for varicocele, check secondary sexual characters and gynaecomastia.
  • Repeat semen analysis — 2 samples, 2–4 weeks apart (single samples are unreliable because of day-to-day variability); confirm azoospermia on the centrifuged deposit.
  • Hormonal profile: FSH, LH, testosterone, prolactin. High FSH + small testes → primary testicular failure; low FSH/LH + low testosterone → hypogonadotropic (pretesticular).
  • Imaging: scrotal ultrasound + Doppler (varicocele, testicular mass, epididymal pathology); transrectal US (ejaculatory duct obstruction, seminal vesicle size); MRI of the pituitary (tumour) when FSH/LH are low.
  • Post-ejaculatory urine — finding sperm in urine confirms retrograde ejaculation.
  • Genetic testing: karyotype (Klinefelter 47,XXY), CFTR mutations (CBAVD — also screen the partner), and Y-chromosome microdeletions (AZF) before ICSI (the deletion can be inherited by male offspring).
  • Testicular biopsy — the key test in azoospermia: it distinguishes obstructive (normal spermatogenesis — sperm can be retrieved by PESA/TESE) from non-obstructive (Sertoli-cell-only or maturation arrest).
  • Antisperm antibodies — MAR test (mixed antiglobulin reaction) for unexplained infertility and after vasectomy reversal.
  • Specialized: sperm DNA fragmentation index, functional tests — usually reserved for planning ICSI.
FSH is the key discriminator: normal FSH with azoospermia → suspect obstruction; high FSH → primary testicular failure.

Q1Item 25 · Biopsy vs Autopsy
What is biopsy and autopsy? How histopathology helps the autopsy procedure?
Tap to reveal

Answer — Biopsy, Autopsy & Histopathology

Biopsy and autopsy are two complementary ways of obtaining tissue for diagnosis — one from the living patient, one from the dead — and histopathology is the laboratory discipline that turns that tissue into diagnostic information in both settings.

  • Biopsy = removal of a small piece of tissue from a living patient for diagnostic examination (e.g., tumour typing/grading, confirming inflammation, transplant surveillance).
  • Autopsy (post-mortem examination / necropsy) = systematic examination of a dead body to determine the cause of death, document the extent of disease, and assess the quality of medical care.
Autopsy types
  • Clinical / academic (hospital): done with consent; goals = confirm diagnosis, quality assurance, teaching and research.
  • Medicolegal / forensic: ordered by the coroner/authority in unnatural, suspicious or sudden deaths; answers legal questions (natural vs unnatural death, injury dating).
  • Complete vs limited — e.g., organ-restricted examination (heart/brain only).
How histopathology helps the autopsy
  • Confirms the gross (macroscopic) diagnosis and corrects discrepancies between clinical and post-mortem findings.
  • Identifies the cause of death when gross findings are inconclusive — e.g., early myocardial infarction is grossly invisible for hours but shows coagulation necrosis microscopically within ~4–6 h; also pneumonia, myocarditis, sepsis, pulmonary embolism.
  • Detects clinically silent disease — occult tumours, incidental findings (e.g., small pulmonary emboli).
  • Documents disease extent — staging, spread and grading of a known disease.
  • Negative (exclusionary) findings — e.g., ruling out suspected infection or neoplasm.
  • Quality assurance / audit — comparing antemortem vs post-mortem diagnoses (correlates the accuracy of clinical tests) and monitoring the effect of therapy.
  • Medicolegal — establishing natural vs unnatural death and dating injuries (age of wound by histology).
Mechanism in brief
An autopsy proceeds as: external examination → internal (organ) examination with gross photography and sampling → histopathology of the sampled organs. Microscopy then confirms the cause of death (especially early/subtle lesions), quantifies disease, and produces the definitive autopsy report — so histopathology is the final arbiter of the autopsy's conclusions.
Source: Bancroft & Gamble, Theory and Practice of Histological Techniques; Robbins Basic Pathology.

Q3Item 25 · Histopathology steps
State the steps in histopathology.
Tap to reveal

Answer — Steps in Histopathology (tissue → section)

The tissue removed at biopsy reaches the microscope only after a sequence of preparation steps. Each step prepares the sample for the next, and together they convert a soft, watery tissue into thin, stained sections that preserve architecture.

Fixation→ Dehydration→ Clearing→ Wax impregnation→ Embedding→ Sectioning→ Staining→ Mounting→ Microscopy & report
  • 1. Fixation — preserve tissue, prevent autolysis/putrefaction and harden it (10% neutral buffered formalin, ~24 h).
  • 2. Dehydration — remove water with ascending grades of alcohol (70% → 95% → 100% ethanol); water would not mix with the paraffin wax.
  • 3. Clearing — replace alcohol with a clearing agent miscible with wax (xylene); tissue becomes translucent and the alcohol no longer blocks wax penetration.
  • 4. Wax impregnation — infiltrate with molten paraffin wax (44–60 °C oven) so the tissue is fully permeated by a cuttable medium.
  • 5. Embedding — orient the tissue in a wax block that sets hard for cutting.
  • 6. Sectioning — the microtome cuts ribbons of 3–5 µm sections which are floated onto slides.
  • 7. Staining — routine H&E (or special stains) makes components visible.
  • 8. Mounting — coverslip with a resinous mountant (e.g., DPX) to protect and preserve the section.
  • 9. Reporting — microscopic examination under the microscope + a written histopathology report.
Why the order matters
Water and paraffin are immiscible, so the tissue must be carried stepwise: fixative cross-links proteins → alcohol replaces water → xylene replaces alcohol → wax replaces xylene. Skipping or shortening a step causes poor wax penetration and cutting artifacts.
Source: Bancroft & Gamble, Theory and Practice of Histological Techniques.
Q4Item 25 · Frozen section

Tap to reveal

Answer — Frozen Section

A frozen section is a rapid tissue diagnosis made while the patient is still on the operating table, by freezing the fresh tissue instead of going through the multi-day paraffin process.

  • Principle: fresh (unfixed) tissue is frozen (cryostat at −20 °C), cut into thin sections, and stained with a quick H&E / toluidine blue — a diagnosis can be given in ~10–20 minutes.
Indications
  • Margin assessment — breast lumpectomy, skin (BCC/SCC), soft-tissue sarcoma: the surgeon wants to know in-surgery whether the excision is clear.
  • Lymph node staging — sentinel lymph node for metastasis (breast, melanoma), which determines whether to do a full node dissection.
  • Tissue identification — e.g., confirming parathyroid vs lymph node vs fat during neck/thyroid surgery.
  • Determining adequacy of the specimen (is there enough diagnostic tissue?).
Uses
  • Guides the surgical decision in the same sitting and avoids a second operation.
Limitations
  • Ice-crystal artifacts distort cell morphology (bubbly cytoplasm, crushed cells).
  • Thicker sections + poorer morphology than paraffin sections — subtle lesions are hard to grade.
  • Sampling error — only a small portion is examined.
  • Limited special stains/IHC, and the tissue is not permanently preserved.
The frozen-section report is provisional — permanent paraffin sections are the gold standard for the final diagnosis.
Source: Bancroft & Gamble; Robbins Basic Pathology.

Answer — Tissue Preservatives & Processing

Tissue preservatives = agents that keep tissue from deteriorating after removal, preventing autolysis (self-digestion by the tissue's own enzymes) and putrefaction (decay by bacteria), so the sample remains fit for study.

Common preservatives
  • 10% neutral buffered formalin — the most common (it is both a preservative and a fixative).
  • Alcohol (ethanol/methanol) — used for cytology smears (wet fixation).
  • Glutaraldehyde — the fixative of choice for electron microscopy.
  • Cold (4 °C) / freezing (−20/−80 °C) — short-term preservation without chemical alteration.
  • Compound fixatives — Bouin's, Carnoy's, Zenker's (used for specific structures/enzymes).
Aims of preservation
  • Prevent autolysis and putrefaction from the moment of removal.
  • Preserve morphology close to the living state.
  • Keep the tissue suitable for later processing and staining.
Steps of tissue processing
Fixation→ Dehydration (ascending alcohol)→ Clearing (xylene)→ Wax impregnation→ Embedding→ Sectioning (microtome)→ Staining→ Mounting
What each step does
  • Dehydration removes water (ascending 70% → 100% alcohol); clearing removes alcohol (xylene) and makes the tissue miscible with wax; impregnation fills the tissue with molten paraffin; embedding creates a firm block; sectioning cuts 3–5 µm slices; staining (H&E) makes structures visible; mounting preserves the section under a coverslip.

Answer — Fixation & Fixatives

Fixative = a chemical (or physical) agent used to fix (preserve) tissue so that morphology is retained close to the living state and the tissue is hardened for sectioning. Fixation is the first and most important step of tissue handling.

Aims of fixation
  • Preserve cells/tissues close to the living state (stop all ongoing cellular activity).

Q7Item 25 · Processing
Mention the steps of tissue processing. Why tissue must undergo processing for histopathology?
Tap to reveal

Answer — Tissue Processing & Its Purpose

Tissue processing is the series of steps that converts a fixed, watery biopsy into a paraffin block from which thin sections can be cut and stained. The whole point is to replace the water in the tissue with a solid supporting medium — paraffin wax.

Steps (in order)
Fixation→ Dehydration→ Clearing→ Wax impregnation→ Embedding→ Sectioning→ Staining→ Mounting
  • Fixation — formalin preserves & hardens the tissue (stops autolysis).
  • Dehydration — ascending alcohols (70% → 100%) remove all water, because water and wax do not mix.
  • Clearing — xylene replaces alcohol; the tissue becomes translucent and is now miscible with molten wax.
  • Wax impregnation — molten paraffin infiltrates the tissue in a 44–60 °C oven, filling every space.
  • Embedding — tissue is oriented in a wax block that sets hard.
  • Sectioning — the microtome cuts ribbons of 3–5 µm sections onto slides.
  • Staining + Mounting — H&E (or special stains), then a coverslip for permanence.
Why tissue must undergo processing
  • Fresh tissue is soft, watery and colorless — it cannot be cut into thin slices (it would crush under the blade) and shows no contrast under the microscope.
  • Processing replaces water with paraffin wax, producing a firm block that supports the tissue while it is sliced into micron-thin sections.
  • Wax acts as the support medium — the tissue keeps its architecture intact during cutting.
  • It enables staining (fixed, sectioned tissue takes up dyes) and gives permanent, archivable slides for later review.
Mechanism in brief
Each step removes one barrier: fixative cross-links proteins (stops decay), alcohol removes water, xylene removes alcohol, molten wax replaces xylene so the tissue becomes a solid, cuttable block. Without processing, tissue would crush under the microtome blade and be too thick to see detail.
Source: Bancroft & Gamble.

Answer — Exfoliative & Abrasive Cytology

Both techniques examine surface cells — the difference is whether those cells came off by themselves (exfoliative) or had to be mechanically removed (abrasive). Neither requires a needle.

Exfoliative cytology
  • Study of spontaneously shed cells that are cast off from a surface into a cavity or fluid.
  • Specimens: vaginal (Pap smear), sputum, urine, pleural/peritoneal/pericardial fluids, CSF, nipple discharge, ascitic fluid.
  • Applications: cervical cancer screening (Pap test), diagnosis of malignant effusions, urothelial carcinoma detection in urine, lung cancer screening (sputum cytology).
  • Advantages: simple, non-invasive, excellent for screening populations; limitations: cannot localize or grade the lesion, and relies on cells actually being shed.

  • Cells are mechanically removed (scraped/brushed) from a surface by an instrument — a more active collection than exfoliation.
  • Methods: cervical scrape/scraping (spatula), bronchial brush, gastric brushing, oral mucosal scrape, endoscopic brushing.
  • Applications: cervical dysplasia screening, bronchial/endobronchial lesions, GI lesions.
  • Advantages: directly samples the suspicious surface (higher yield than waiting for shedding); limitation: samples surface cells only — cannot tell how deep a lesion invades.
Both are non-needle cytologic techniques (vs FNAC which aspirates).

Q3Item 26 · FNAC
What is FNAC? Mention the advantages of FNAC.

Answer — FNAC & Its Advantages

FNAC (fine needle aspiration cytology) = a cytologic technique in which a fine needle (22–25 G) aspirates cells from a palpable or image-guided lesion, and the aspirate is smeared, stained and examined microscopically. It is the standard first-line tissue test for superficial lumps.

Advantages
  • Rapid — same-day / OPD diagnosis (patient need not be admitted).
  • Minimally invasive — no major surgery; usually no anesthesia needed (or only local).
  • Cost-effective — far cheaper than surgical biopsy.
  • Low complication rate — minimal scarring, quick recovery, few adverse events.
  • Repeatable — can be done again for monitoring (post-chemotherapy, thyroid follow-up).
  • Wide applicability — breast, thyroid, lymph node, salivary gland, liver, lung, kidney, pancreas, soft tissue.
  • Guided sampling of deep organs — USG/CT/EUS-guided FNAC reaches lesions not palpable at all.
  • Good for screening/triaging — separates benign from suspicious/malignant and directs who needs a full biopsy.
  • Provides material for ancillary tests — a cell block allows IHC, molecular testing and microbiology.
Major limitation: cells only, no architecture → can't reliably grade tumours or assess invasion.
Source: Orell & Sterrett; Koss.
Q4Item 26 · Procedure
Describe shortly the procedure of FNAC.
Tap to reveal

Answer — FNAC Procedure

The FNAC procedure is a sequence of localization → aspiration → smear-making. Mastery of two points decides the yield: where you sample and how you handle the syringe pressure.

  1. Localization — palpable lesions are located by palpation/fixing with one hand; deep lesions by USG/CT/EUS guidance.
  2. Preparation — explain the procedure to the patient, position comfortably, disinfect the skin.
  3. Needle & syringe — 22–25 G needle on a 10–20 mL syringe, often held in a syringe holder/pistol for one-handed control.
  4. Insertion & aspiration — insert into the lesion; apply negative pressure; make small multidirectional movements within the lesion to dislodge cells from different areas.
  5. Release pressure before withdrawal — crucial step: pressure is released while the needle is still in the tissue so cells are not sucked into the syringe hub, where they are lost.
  6. Withdraw — apply pressure/ice over the puncture site to prevent hematoma formation.
  7. Smearing — expel a drop onto slides; make both air-dried smears (→ Giemsa/MGG) and wet-fixed in 95% ethanol smears (→ Pap/H&E).
  8. Staining & reporting — stain, examine microscopically and issue a report.
  9. Special handling: cell block (for IHC), culture (if infection is suspected), flow cytometry (lymphoma), and on-site adequacy assessment.
Key error to avoid: maintaining negative pressure while withdrawing the needle pulls the sample into the syringe, where it is lost.
Source: Orell & Sterrett.

Answer — Needle Used & Complications

The defining feature of FNAC is the fine (thin) needle: it limits trauma and bleeding, which in turn keeps complications low.

Needle
  • A fine needle = small-caliber needle with an outer diameter ≤ ~1 mm — usually 22–25 gauge (sometimes 21–27 G).
  • Decidedly thinner than core-biopsy needles (14–18 G). Mounted on a 10–20 mL syringe, often with a pistol-style holder.
Why a fine needle?
  • Minimal trauma — less bleeding, less pain, fewer complications.
  • High cellular yield relative to blood contamination (thin lumen limits hemorrhage into the aspirate).
  • Safe in vascular organs (thyroid, liver); reduces the already rare needle-tract seeding risk.
Complications
  • Bleeding / hematoma at the puncture site — the most common, usually trivial.
  • Infection — rare, minimized by skin disinfection.
  • Pneumothorax — with lung/chest-wall lesions.
  • Vasovagal reaction — fainting, hypotension (patient anxiety/pain).
  • Tumour seeding along the needle tract — very rare with fine needles.
  • Thyroid: rare hemorrhage, transient voice change.
  • Inadequate / unsatisfactory sample — non-diagnostic; needs a repeat or a biopsy.
Overall FNAC is very safe; the commonest practical problem is not a complication but an inadequate sample.
Source: Orell & Sterrett; Koss.

Answer — Sampling Deep-Seated Organs (Liver, Lung)

Deep lesions cannot be felt, so the sample is taken by imaging guidance. The same fine-needle principle is used, with the modality chosen by the organ and the equipment available.

  • Palpable lesions: direct FNAC. Deep lesions require image guidance — the choice below.

  • Liver: USG-guided; USG also shows the vessels to be avoided — a needle track through the lesion plus a rim of normal liver is targeted.
  • Lung: CT-guided for peripheral lesions, bronchoscopic (brush/wash/TBNA) for central ones; a radiologist screens for pneumothorax after the procedure.

Lung/chest lesions carry a risk of pneumothorax — image guidance reduces but does not eliminate it.

Answer — Cytology Fixatives

Fixation in cytology is quick-fixation of individual smeared cells — its overriding goal is to stop air-drying, because air-drying ruins nuclear detail for Papanicolaou/H&E staining.

  • 95% (95–100%) ethanol — the most common cytologic fixative; used for wet-fixed smears (Pap, H&E).
  • Methanol — good nuclear preservation (used in some hematology smears).
  • Acetone — used for some enzyme and immunostains.
  • Coating / spray fixative (Carbowax / polyethylene-glycol in alcohol) — sprayed on the smear; forms a thin plastic-like film preventing drying when a jar of alcohol is unavailable (e.g., for transport).
  • Alcohol-formalin mixtures — used for cell blocks (mini-histology).
What is a coating fixative?
  • A fixative in aerosol/spray form that deposits a protective plastic film over the smear on contact.
  • It fixes the cells (alcohol) and prevents air-drying artifacts, preserving nuclear detail for later Pap/H&E staining.
  • Essential for smears collected where a liquid fixative jar is impractical — OPD, outreach and field settings.
Air-dried = Giemsa; ethanol-fixed = Pap/H&E. Never air-dry the smear you plan to Pap-stain.
Source: Koss; Bancroft & Gamble.

Answer — Biopsy vs FNAC: Which is Better?

The short answer: biopsy is better for a definitive, final diagnosis; FNAC is better as a rapid first step. The deciding factor is whether the diagnosis depends on tissue architecture (which only a biopsy preserves).

Which is better for diagnosis?
  • Biopsy is better for definitive diagnosis because it preserves tissue architecture — allowing assessment of invasion, grading, and a full IHC panel (e.g., distinguishing in-situ from invasive carcinoma, subtyping sarcomas).
  • FNAC is better for screening/triage, deep inaccessible lesions, high-risk/elderly patients, and rapid follow-up — it answers "is this benign or malignant?" fast.
  • In practice the two are complementary: FNAC first, then a biopsy if the diagnosis needs architecture or grading.
Mechanism in brief
Biopsy preserves the architectural context (basement membrane, stroma, margins, glands) that defines benign vs malignant and invasive vs in-situ; FNAC collects dispersed cells, losing that context — so it can say "malignant cells present" but not always "invasion present." Hence biopsy remains the gold standard when architecture decides the diagnosis.
