# Part A — Morphological Classification & Risk Factors (Q1–2)

## Q1. Classify carcinoma breast according to morphology. Name the stromal tumors of the breast.

### Morphological Classification of Carcinoma of the Breast

🔴 **Almost all breast malignancies are adenocarcinomas.** All arise from the **terminal duct lobular unit (TDLU)**. By convention, "lobular" = biologically related to LCIS; "ductal" = cannot be classified as a special type.

### I. Carcinoma In Situ (Non-invasive)

| Type | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Ductal Carcinoma In Situ (DCIS)** | Clonal epithelial proliferation confined within ducts by basement membrane; myoepithelial cells preserved; detected by mammography (calcifications). Patterns: **comedo** (high-grade + central necrosis), **cribriform** (cookie-cutter spaces), **micropapillary** (no fibrovascular cores), **papillary** (true fibrovascular cores). Untreated ~1%/yr progress to invasive. Mastectomy cures >95%. |
| **Lobular Carcinoma In Situ (LCIS)** | Dyscohesive cells from **E-cadherin/CDH1 loss**; incidental finding; no calcifications; bilateral 20–40%; risk marker (not obligate precursor); ER+/PR+, HER2−; no Paget disease. |

### II. Invasive Carcinoma

#### A. Invasive Carcinoma of No Special Type (NST) — ~70–80%
- 🔴 **Most common type**; presents as hard, irregular mass with **desmoplastic stromal reaction** (grating sound like cutting water chestnut).
- **Nottingham Histologic Grade:** tubule formation + nuclear pleomorphism + mitotic rate → Grade 1 (well-differentiated) / Grade 2 (moderately) / Grade 3 (poorly differentiated).

#### B. Special Histologic Types

| Subgroup | Types | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Luminal (ER+/HER2−)** | Invasive lobular, mucinous (colloid), tubular, papillary | Lobular: CDH1 loss, single-file "Indian filing," minimal desmoplasia; mets to peritoneum/leptomeninges/GI/ovaries. Mucinous: cells floating in mucin lakes. Tubular: pure tubules, excellent prognosis. |
| **HER2-prone** | Apocrine, micropapillary | Apocrine: sweat-gland appearance. Micropapillary: hollow balls of cells in fluid spaces. |
| **TNBC** | Medullary pattern, metaplastic | Medullary: soft pushing-border mass, dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, BRCA1-associated, better prognosis. Metaplastic: spindle-cell and matrix-producing. |
| **Favorable types** | Tubular, adenoid cystic, secretory, mucinous | All carry excellent prognosis regardless of grade. |

#### C. Inflammatory Carcinoma
- 🔴 **Dermal lymphovascular plugging** by carcinoma → erythema, swelling, peau d'orange. "Inflammatory" is a misnomer — usually no inflammation. No discrete mass. 3-yr survival 3–10%.

#### D. Paget Disease of the Nipple
- 1–4% of breast cancers; malignant Paget cells from DCIS track via lactiferous sinuses into nipple skin; unilateral pruritic scaly eruption (eczema mimic). 50–60% have palpable mass → invasive carcinoma (usually ER−/HER2+).

### Stromal Tumors of the Breast

🔴 **Two stromas, two families:**

| Tumor | Origin | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Fibroadenoma** | Intralobular stroma | **Most common benign breast tumor**; MED12 mutations (~2/3); 20s–30s; well-circumscribed rubbery nodule with slit-like spaces; pericanalicular/intracanalicular patterns; hormonally responsive. |
| **Phyllodes tumor** | Intralobular stroma | Leaf-like protrusions; 6th decade; higher cellularity, mitoses, stromal overgrowth; MED12 + TERT mutations; low-grade = local recurrence; high-grade = hematogenous stromal mets only (axillary dissection contraindicated). |
| **Myofibroblastoma** | Interlobular stroma | Only breast tumor equally common in males. |
| **Fibromatosis** | Interlobular stroma | Clonal fibroblasts; locally aggressive, never metastasizes; associated with FAP/Gardner syndrome. |
| **Angiosarcoma** | Interlobular stroma | <0.05% of breast malignancies; sporadic (young) or post-radiation (older, 5–10 yr after RT). |
| **Lipoma** | Interlobular stroma | Benign fat-containing tumor. |

> 🎯 "Fibroadenoma = most common benign; phyllodes = leaf-like, high-grade stromal mets; angiosarcoma = only sarcoma with any frequency."

## Q2. Mention risk factors of carcinoma breast. How will you diagnose a case of carcinoma cervix?

### Risk Factors of Carcinoma Breast

#### Non-modifiable Risk Factors

| Factor | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Age & Sex** | Female >> Male (100:1); risk ↑ with age; median diagnosis ~62 yr |
| **Genetic / Family History** | BRCA1 (40–90% lifetime risk, TNBC), BRCA2 (30–60%, ER+), TP53/Li-Fraumeni, PTEN/Cowden, CDH1, PALB2, ATM, CHEK2 |
| **Personal History** | Prior breast cancer → 3–4× risk of contralateral cancer |
| **Reproductive History** | Early menarche (<12 yr), late menopause (>55 yr), nulliparity, first pregnancy >30 yr |
| **Breast Density** | Extremely dense breast tissue → 4–6× risk (obscures mammographic detection + biologically active stroma) |
| **Radiation Exposure** | Prior chest radiation (e.g., mantle RT for Hodgkin lymphoma) before age 30 |

#### Modifiable Risk Factors

| Factor | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT)** | Combined estrogen-progestin → ↑ risk (reverts to baseline after stopping) |
| **Oral Contraceptives** | Slight ↑ risk (reverts after stopping) |
| **Obesity** | Postmenopausal: adipose aromatase → ↑ estrogen; premenopausal obesity is somewhat protective |
| **Alcohol** | Dose-dependent ↑ risk |
| **Physical Inactivity** | Sedentary lifestyle → ↑ risk |
| **Diet** | High-fat diet (controversial) |

#### Protective Factors
- Early first pregnancy, multiple pregnancies, breastfeeding, tamoxifen, oophorectomy, physical exercise.

### Pathogenesis of Sporadic Breast Cancer
🔴 **Estrogenic stimulation + age** are the most important risk factors. Estrogen drives proliferation → more cells "at risk" for DNA damage. Proof: **tamoxifen reduces luminal cancers; postmenopausal HRT increases them.**

#### 3 Molecular Pathways:
1. **ER-positive (50–65%):** Flat epithelial atypia → ADH/ALH → DCIS → invasive luminal cancer; PIK3CA mutations, 1q gain, 16q loss.
2. **HER2+ (~20%):** HER2 amplification on 17q; no definite precursor.
3. **TNBC (~15%):** BRCA1 pathway (germline or epigenetic); TP53 mutated in majority.

### Diagnosis of Carcinoma Cervix

#### Clinical Presentation
- Abnormal vaginal bleeding (postcoital, intermenstrual, postmenopausal)
- Vaginal discharge (watery, bloody, foul-smelling)
- Pelvic pain (advanced disease)
- Contact bleeding

#### Investigation Protocol

| Step | Investigation | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Screening** | **Pap smear (conventional/Liquid-based cytology)** | Detect cervical dysplasia/CIN; done every 3 yr (ages 21–65) or every 5 yr with HPV co-testing |
| **2. HPV testing** | DNA detection of high-risk HPV types (16, 18) | HPV causes >99% of cervical cancers; co-testing with Pap smear |
| **3. Colposcopy** | Visual inspection with acetic acid/Lugol's iodine | Identify acetowhite areas → direct biopsy of suspicious lesions |
| **4. Biopsy** | Punch biopsy / cone biopsy (LEEP/LLETZ) | **Histopathological examination = gold standard** for diagnosis and staging |
| **5. IHC** | p16 (surrogate for HPV E7 oncoprotein), Ki-67 | p16 diffuse strong positive = high-risk HPV; differentiates CIN from reactive changes |
| **6. Imaging** | MRI pelvis, CT abdomen/pelvis, PET-CT | Staging (FIGO staging); assess parametrial invasion, lymph node involvement, distant mets |
| **7. Blood tests** | SCC antigen, CBC, renal/liver function | SCC antigen = tumor marker for monitoring; baseline before treatment |
| **8. Cystoscopy/Proctoscopy** | Direct visualization | Assess bladder/rectal invasion in advanced disease |

#### Staging (FIGO)
- Stage I: Confined to cervix
- Stage II: Extends to vagina/parametrium
- Stage III: Pelvic wall extension/hydronephrosis
- Stage IV: Bladder/rectal invasion or distant mets

> 🎯 "Pap smear = screening; colposcopy + biopsy = diagnosis; p16 IHC = HPV surrogate; MRI = staging."

# Part B — Inflammatory Disorders & Breast Lump (Q3–4)

## Q3. Name inflammatory disorders of the breast. Discuss breast abscess.

### Inflammatory Disorders of the Breast

| Disorder | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Acute puerperal mastitis** | Lactational; bacterial (S. aureus most common); erythema, warmth, tenderness, fever; treat with antibiotics + continued breastfeeding; can progress to abscess |
| **Non-puerperal mastitis** | Non-lactational; periductal inflammation; often associated with smoking; central subareolar location; recurrent → periductal fibrosis |
| **Breast abscess** | Complication of mastitis; fluctuant collection; requires incision and drainage |
| **Granulomatous mastitis** | Idiopathic granulomatous mastitis (IGM) — mimic carcinoma; well-defined mass; non-caseating granulomas; must exclude TB, sarcoid, fungal |
| **Fat necrosis** | Trauma → enzymatic fat saponification → oil cysts, dystrophic calcification; may mimic carcinoma clinically and radiologically |
| **Sclerosing lymphangitis** | Cord-like induration of superficial lymphatics; associated with sexual activity |
| **Radiation mastitis** | Late effect of breast radiation; fibrosis, telangiectasia |

### Breast Abscess — Detailed Discussion

#### Definition
🔴 **Breast abscess = a localized collection of pus within the breast parenchyma**, most commonly a complication of **acute puerperal mastitis** (lactational) or **non-puerperal mastitis** (non-lactational, central subareolar).

#### Etiology / Predisposing Factors
- **Lactational:** cracked nipples → bacterial entry → **S. aureus** (most common organism); also Streptococcus, E. coli.
- **Non-lactational:** smoking (damages ducts), retroareolar duct ectasia, periductal mastitis → mixed anaerobes + aerobes; recurrent abscesses common.

#### Pathogenesis
```
Bacterial entry (cracked nipple / duct obstruction)
   → Acute suppurative inflammation
   → Neutrophilic infiltration + tissue necrosis
   → Walled-off pus collection = ABSCESS
   →如果不treated → chronic abscess, fistula formation
```

#### Clinical Features
- **Pain, swelling, redness, warmth** in the breast
- **Fluctuant, tender mass** with surrounding erythema
- Fever, malaise, leukocytosis
- Nipple discharge (purulent in lactational)
- axillary lymphadenopathy (reactive)

#### Diagnosis
- **Clinical examination** — fluctuant, tender mass
- **Ultrasound** — confirms fluid collection (hypoechoic/anechoic collection)
- **Aspiration** — diagnostic and therapeutic; send pus for culture and sensitivity
- **Mammography** — may mimic carcinoma; needed to rule out underlying malignancy (especially in non-lactational)
- **Culture** — identifies organism and guides antibiotic therapy

#### Management
1. **Antibiotics** — empirical (flucloxacillin or cephalosporin) → targeted based on culture
2. **Incision and drainage (I&D)** — the mainstay of treatment for established abscess
3. **Aspiration (needle)** — alternative for small abscesses; may need repeat
4. **Continue breastfeeding** (lactational) — to prevent milk stasis
5. **Stop smoking** (non-lactational) — reduces recurrence
6. **Underlying pathology** — rule out carcinoma (especially non-lactational, non-puerperal); biopsy if persistent mass

#### Complications
- Chronic abscess / recurrent abscess
- Fistula formation (especially non-lactational)
- Mammary duct fistula
- Cosmetic deformity
- Rarely: sepsis, spread to chest wall

> 🎯 "Breast abscess = I&D + antibiotics + culture; non-lactational → think smoking + rule out underlying carcinoma."

## Q4. Mention the causes of breast lump in females. What is triple assessment?

### Causes of Breast Lump in Females

| Category | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Benign epithelial** | **Fibrocystic changes** (most common cause of breast lump), fibroadenoma, intraductal papilloma, fat necrosis, breast abscess |
| **Benign stromal** | Fibroadenoma, phyllodes tumor (benign/borderline/malignant), myofibroblastoma, fibromatosis |
| **Malignant epithelial** | Invasive ductal carcinoma (NST), invasive lobular carcinoma, DCIS, Paget disease, inflammatory carcinoma |
| **Malignant stromal** | Phyllodes tumor (malignant), angiosarcoma |
| **Other** | Lipoma, lymphoma, metastases to breast (rare — melanoma, ovarian), gynecomastia (males) |

### Triple Assessment

🔴 **Triple assessment = the gold standard clinical approach to evaluate any breast lump** — combining three independent modalities to achieve near-100% diagnostic accuracy.

| Component | Method | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Clinical Assessment** | History (age, family history, symptoms, duration, changes) + Clinical breast examination (inspection, palpation of lump and axillary lymph nodes) | Characterize lump (size, location, consistency, mobility, skin changes, nipple retraction, axillary nodes) |
| **2. Imaging** | **Mammography** (≥40 yr or high-risk) + **Ultrasound** (all ages, first-line for <40 yr) ± **MRI** (high-risk screening, preoperative assessment) | Detect mass, calcifications, architectural distortion; distinguish solid vs cystic; BI-RADS classification |
| **3. Tissue Diagnosis** | **FNAC** (fine-needle aspiration cytology) or **Core needle biopsy** (preferred — preserves architecture, allows IHC) or **Excisional biopsy** | Histopathological examination for definitive diagnosis; IHC (ER, PR, HER2, Ki-67) for molecular subtyping |

#### Why Triple Assessment Works
- **Clinical + imaging alone** have significant false-positive and false-negative rates
- **Adding tissue diagnosis** raises accuracy to **>99%**
- If **all three agree benign** → safe observation
- If **any component is suspicious/malignant** → proceed to histological diagnosis and staging

#### BI-RADS Classification (Breast Imaging-Reporting and Data System)
| Category | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Incomplete — need more imaging | Recall for additional views/ultrasound |
| 1 | Negative | Routine screening |
| 2 | Benign | Routine screening |
| 3 | Probably benign | Short-interval follow-up (6 months) |
| 4 | Suspicious | **Tissue sampling required** |
| 5 | Highly suggestive of malignancy | **Tissue sampling + staging** |
| 6 | Known malignancy | Already biopsy-proven |

> 🎯 "Triple assessment = clinical + imaging + tissue → near-100% accuracy. Core biopsy > FNAC for architecture and IHC."

# Part C — Fibroadenoma & Molecular Subtypes (Q5–6)

## Q5. Mention the morphological features of fibroadenoma of breast.

### Definition
🔴 **Fibroadenoma = the most common benign tumor of the female breast**, a biphasic neoplasm composed of **proliferating intralobular stroma** and **epithelium**. Driven by **somatic MED12 mutations** (~2/3 of cases).

### Epidemiology
- Peak incidence: **20s–30s years**
- Most common breast mass in women <35 years
- Frequently **multiple** and **bilateral** (25–50%)
- Hormonally responsive — grow during pregnancy, regress after menopause

### Gross Morphology
- 🔴 **Well-circumscribed, rubbery, grayish-white, bulging nodule**
- Size: variable (0.5–15 cm; average 2–3 cm)
- Cut surface: **bulging, white-gray, glistening, with slit-like spaces** lined by epithelium
- In older women → **hyalinized stroma + atrophic epithelium**
- May have cystic change, calcification (popcorn calcification on mammography)

### Microscopic Morphology
| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Biphasic pattern** | Proliferating **intralobular stroma** + compressed **epithelium-lined ducts** |
| **Pericanalicular pattern** | Stroma proliferates **around** ducts → open, oval/elongated spaces |
| **Intracanalicular pattern** | Stroma proliferates **within** and compresses ducts → **branching, slit-like, elongated spaces** (more common) |
| **Epithelium** | Two cell layers — inner luminal columnar + outer myoepithelial; may show usual ductal hyperplasia, apocrine metaplasia, sclerosing adenosis |
| **Stroma** | Myxoid in young patients → hyalinized with age; low cellularity; no atypia; rare mitoses |
| **Stromal overgrowth** | Rarely — if stroma predominates, consider phyllodes tumor |

### "Complex" Fibroadenoma Features
- Cysts >0.3 cm, sclerosing adenosis, epithelial calcifications, papillary apocrine change
- Slightly increased cancer risk (higher with complex features)

### Clinical Features
- **Firm, mobile, painless, well-defined mass** ("breast mouse" — rolls under fingers)
- Usually discovered incidentally or during self-examination
- Rapid growth during pregnancy can mimic carcinoma
- **Cyclosporin A** (post-renal transplant) → multiple bilateral fibroadenomas

### Treatment
- Observation if small and asymptomatic
- Surgical excision or vacuum-assisted biopsy if large, symptomatic, or growing
- Recurrence is uncommon after complete excision

> 🎯 "Fibroadenoma = rubbery, mobile, circumscribed, slit-like spaces; MED12; pericanalicular/intracanalicular; breast mouse."

## Q6. Mention the molecular subtypes of invasive breast cancer.

### Overview
🔴 **Invasive breast cancer is classified into 3 major molecular subtypes** based on gene expression profiling, each with distinct genetics, biology, metastatic pattern, and therapy. All arise from the TDLU.

### Molecular Subtypes

| Feature | **Luminal (ER+/HER2−)** | **HER2+** | **TNBC (ER−/HER2−)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **% of cancers** | **50–65%** (Luminal A 40–55% + Luminal B ~10%) | **~20%** | **~15%** |
| **mRNA profile counterpart** | Luminal A (low proliferation) / Luminal B (high proliferation) | HER2-enriched | Basal-like |
| **Key mutations** | **PIK3CA** (45% LumA, 29% LumB), **TP53** (12% LumA, 29% LumB) | **TP53** (70–80%), HER2 amplification (17q) | **TP53** (70–80%), BRCA1 (germline or epigenetic) |
| **Special histology** | Tubular, lobular (grade 1–2), mucinous, papillary (LumA); grade 3 lobular (LumB) | Apocrine, micropapillary | Medullary pattern, metaplastic |
| **Typical patient** | **Older women, men**; screening-detected | **Young women**; TP53 carriers | **Young women, African heritage, BRCA1 carriers** |
| **Chemo response** | **<10%** complete response | ER−: **30–60%** complete response | **~30%** complete response |
| **Metastatic pattern** | **Bone (70%)** > viscera (25%) > brain (<10%) | Bone (70%), viscera (45%), **brain (30%)** | Bone (40%), viscera (35%), **brain (25%)** |
| **Relapse pattern** | **Low rate over many years**; long survival even with bone mets | **Bimodal** — early + late (10 yr) peaks | **Early peak <8 yr**; late recurrence rare |
| **Targeted therapy** | **Tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors, CDK4/6 inhibitors** (palbociclib, abemaciclib, ribociclib) | **Anti-HER2 antibodies** (trastuzumab, pertuzumab), ADCs, TKIs | **Chemotherapy + immune checkpoints** (PD-L1/PD-1) |

### Luminal A vs Luminal B — The Key Distinction
| Feature | Luminal A | Luminal B |
|---|---|---|
| Proliferation | **Low** (Ki-67 low) | **High** (Ki-67 high) |
| PR status | **Positive** (high) | **Negative or low** |
| Grade | Usually 1–2 | Often 3 |
| Prognosis | **Best** of all subtypes | Intermediate |
| Chemo benefit | Minimal | Modest |

### Clinical Significance
- 🔴 **Luminal A** → antiestrogens alone; bone mets predominate; slow late relapse
- 🔴 **HER2+** → trastuzumab transformed prognosis (>50% complete remission); bimodal relapse
- 🔴 **TNBC** → most aggressive; relapse within 8 years; genomic instability → neoantigens → immunotherapy responsive; shares DNA-repair defects with serous ovarian cancer

### IHC Surrogate for Molecular Subtypes
| IHC Marker | Luminal A | Luminal B | HER2+ | TNBC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ER** | + (strong) | + (weak) | − | − |
| **PR** | + (>20%) | − or <20% | − | − |
| **HER2** | − | − | **+ (3+ or amplified)** | − |
| **Ki-67** | **<14%** | **≥14%** | Variable | Usually high |

> 🎯 "Luminal = ER+ → bone mets → tamoxifen; HER2 = amplified → targeted therapy → bimodal relapse; TNBC = basal → chemo + immunotherapy → early relapse."

# Part D — Prognostic Factors & Gynecomastia (Q7–8)

## Q7. What are the prognostic factors of breast cancer?

### Overview
🔴 **Prognostic factors** tell the likely outcome; **predictive factors** tell the likely response to therapy. The AJCC 8th edition combines anatomic staging with prognostic factors (grade, ER, PR, HER2) to create a **prognostic stage**.

### Prognostic Factors (Ranked by Importance)

| Factor | Key Facts |
|---|---|
| **1. Distant Metastases (M)** | 🔴 **Most important prognostic factor**; once present, cure is unlikely |
| **2. Lymph Node Status (N)** | 🔴 **Second most important**; 10-yr DFS: N0 = 70–80%; 1–3 nodes = 35–40%; >10 nodes = 10–15%. Sentinel node biopsy guides management. |
| **3. Tumor Size (T)** | Node-negative: <1 cm → 10-yr survival >90%; >2 cm → 77%. Size correlates with nodal metastasis risk. |
| **4. Histologic Grade** | Grade 3 (poorly differentiated) → worse survival than Grade 1 (well-differentiated) |
| **5. ER/PR Status** | ER+/PR+ → best prognosis; responds to antiestrogens. ER−/PR− → worse prognosis. |
| **6. HER2 Status** | HER2+ → historically poor prognosis; now transformed by targeted therapy (trastuzumab). Predictive, not just prognostic. |
| **7. Lymphovascular Invasion** | Present in ~50%; predicts nodal mets + local recurrence + poor survival (if node-negative) |
| **8. Special Histologic Types** | **Favorable:** tubular, mucinous, adenoid cystic. **Unfavorable:** metaplastic, inflammatory. |
| **9. Age** | <35 yr → worse prognosis (often TNBC); >70 yr → may have slower biology but comorbidities |
| **10. Molecular Subtype** | Luminal A = best; TNBC = worst (early relapse); HER2+ = intermediate (improved with targeted therapy) |
| **11. Response to Neoadjuvant Chemo** | Complete pathological response (pCR) strongly prognostic for **TNBC and HER2+**; not for most luminal cancers |
| **12. Gene Expression Profiling** | Oncotype DX, MammaPrint → identify low-risk patients who **don't need chemotherapy** |

### AJCC 8th Edition Staging

| Stage | T / N / M | 10-yr Survival |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | DCIS, N0, M0 | **97%** |
| **I** | Invasive ≤2 cm, N0 or micrometastases | **87%** |
| **II** | >2 cm with 0–3 positive LNs | **65%** |
| **III** | >5 cm ± LNs; ≥4 positive LNs; skin/chest wall/inflammatory | **40%** |
| **IV** | Any with distant mets | **5%** |

### Prognostic Stage (New in 8th Edition)
- Combines anatomic TNM + **grade + ER + PR + HER2**
- TNBC may be "up-staged" for aggressive behavior
- Gene expression assays may substitute for some parameters

> 🎯 "Distant mets > lymph node status > tumor size > grade > ER/PR/HER2. N0 = 70–80% 10-yr DFS; >10 nodes = 10–15%."

## Q8. Short note: Gynecomastia.

### Definition
🔴 **Gynecomastia = benign proliferation of male breast glandular tissue**, producing a palpable subareolar mass. It is **NOT** the same as pseudogynecomastia (fat deposition in obese men).

### Epidemiology
- **Most common cause of male breast enlargement**
- Bimodal age distribution: **neonatal** (maternal estrogen), **puberty** (transient, resolves in 6 months–2 years), and **adults >50 years** (most common pathological group)
- Up to **70% of pubertal boys** develop some degree of gynecomastia
- Unilateral or bilateral (bilateral in ~50%)

### Pathogenesis
🔴 **Mechanism = Estrogen/Androgen imbalance** — either ↑ estrogen, ↓ testosterone, or ↑ peripheral aromatization of androgens to estrogens.

| Mechanism | Causes |
|---|---|
| **↑ Estrogen** | Exogenous estrogen (topical creams), estrogen-producing tumors (testicular Leydig cell tumor, adrenal tumor, hepatocellular carcinoma), β-hCG-secreting tumors (germ cell tumors, lung cancer) |
| **↓ Testosterone** | Hypogonadism (Klinefelter 47,XXY — most common genetic cause), Kallmann syndrome, orchiectomy, orchitis (mumps), testicular torsion/trauma, 5α-reductase deficiency, anti-androgen therapy (spironolactone, finasteride, cyproterone) |
| **↑ Aromatase activity** | Obesity (adipose aromatase), aging, chronic liver disease (impaired estrogen metabolism), hyperthyroidism |
| **Drug-induced** | **Spironolactone**, **ketoconazole**, cimetidine, HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors, isoniazid, methyldopa, estrogens, digitalis, penicillamine |
| **Systemic disease** | Chronic liver disease/cirrhosis (↑ aromatization + ↓ sex hormone-binding globulin), chronic renal failure, malnutrition/refeeding, hyperthyroidism |

### Morphology
- **Glandular proliferation** — branching ducts lined by cuboidal/columnar epithelium with papillary projections, surrounded by **loose, edematous, periductal stroma**
- No lobule formation (male breast lacks lobules normally)
- **Periductal fibrosis** in long-standing cases
- Distinguished from carcinoma by presence of **two cell layers** (luminal + myoepithelial) and absence of nuclear atypia/invasion

### Clinical Features
- **Firm, rubbery, subareolar, mobile mass** (disc-like, concentric around nipple)
- Tender in early/acute phases; painless in chronic
- Bilateral in ~50% of pathological cases
- **"Girlie" breast** — fatty tissue + glandular tissue
- ⚠️ Any **unilateral, hard, eccentric** mass in a male → suspect **male breast cancer** until proven otherwise

### Investigation
- **History:** drugs, liver disease, alcohol, testicular examination, family history
- **Hormonal profile:** testosterone, estradiol, LH, FSH, β-hCG, prolactin
- **Imaging:** mammography (differentiate glandular from fatty tissue); ultrasound (assess mass)
- **Biopsy** if mass is suspicious or doesn't resolve after removing cause

### Treatment
- **Remove underlying cause** (stop offending drug, treat liver disease, etc.)
- **Observation** — spontaneous resolution in many cases (especially pubertal)
- **Tamoxifen** — first-line medical therapy; anti-estrogen effect
- **Surgery (mastectomy)** — for persistent or cosmetically bothersome gynecomastia

### When to Suspect Male Breast Cancer
- Age >60, unilateral hard mass, skin ulceration, nipple retraction, bloody discharge, axillary lymphadenopathy
- 1% of male breast enlargement is cancer
- >90% are luminal (ER+); BRCA2 is the key gene (~6% of male carriers)

> 🎯 "Gynecomastia = estrogen/androgen imbalance; rule out male breast cancer (1%); Klinefelter = most common genetic cause; tamoxifen = first-line treatment."

# Part E — Clinical Cases (Q9–10)

## Q9. A 25 yr old woman is presented with a firm, mobile nodule in the outer quadrant of the right breast. Write down your D/D. How will you proceed to investigate the case?

### Differential Diagnosis

| Diagnosis | Key Features Supporting | Against |
|---|---|---|
| **Fibroadenoma** 🔴🔴 | **Most likely in this age group**; firm, mobile, well-circumscribed; peak 20s–30s | Usually painless |
| **Fibrocystic changes** | Common; may present as lumpy breast; cyclical pain | Usually bilateral, multiple |
| **Intraductal papilloma** | Young woman; may present as mass or bloody nipple discharge | More common near areola |
| **Phyllodes tumor (benign)** | Can occur in young women; firm, well-circumscribed | Less common than fibroadenoma; usually larger |
| **Breast abscess** | Firm, tender mass; may have erythema/warmth | Usually painful, fluctuant, fever |
| **Fat necrosis** | History of trauma; may mimic carcinoma | Usually painful, may calcify |
| **Invasive carcinoma** (rare in this age) | Hard, irregular, fixed mass | Very rare at 25; but TNBC possible |
| **Granulomatous mastitis** | Firm mass; may mimic carcinoma | Young women; may have systemic symptoms |

### Investigation Protocol

#### Step 1: Detailed History
- Duration, pain, changes with menstrual cycle, nipple discharge
- Family history of breast/ovarian cancer
- Oral contraceptive use, pregnancy history
- Trauma history

#### Step 2: Clinical Breast Examination
- Characterize the lump (size, consistency, mobility, borders, skin changes)
- Assess both breasts and axillary lymph nodes
- Nipple examination (retraction, discharge)

#### Step 3: Imaging
- **Ultrasound** (first-line for age <30 — dense breast tissue makes mammography less sensitive)
  - Characterize as solid vs cystic
  - BI-RADS classification
- **Mammography** — may be done if clinical suspicion is high, but less sensitive in young dense breasts

#### Step 4: Tissue Diagnosis
- **Core needle biopsy** (preferred — preserves architecture, allows IHC)
  - FNAC as alternative if core not available
  - Send for: histopathology + IHC (ER, PR, HER2, Ki-67)

#### Step 5: If Malignancy Confirmed
- Staging workup: blood tests, chest X-ray, ultrasound abdomen, CT/MRI as indicated
- Molecular subtyping for treatment planning
- Genetic counseling if family history positive

> 🎯 "25 yr + firm mobile nodule = fibroadenoma until proven otherwise; ultrasound first → core biopsy if needed → IHC if malignant."

## Q10. A 65 yr old woman is found to have a 1.5 cm firm, fixed, irregular mass in the upper outer quadrant of the left breast. Write down your D/D.

### Differential Diagnosis

| Diagnosis | Key Features Supporting | Against |
|---|---|---|
| **Invasive Ductal Carcinoma (NST)** 🔴🔴 | **Most likely diagnosis**; 65 yr; firm, fixed, irregular; upper outer quadrant (most common site); postmenopausal | — |
| **Invasive Lobular Carcinoma** | Same age group; may present as ill-defined thickening; can be harder to palpate/image | Often subtle; may not form a discrete mass |
| **Phyllodes tumor (malignant)** | Can present as a firm fixed mass | Less common; usually larger; lower age than 65 |
| **Breast abscess / Mastitis** | Firm mass; may have warmth, erythema | Usually painful, fever, fluctuant; less likely to be fixed/irregular |
| **Fat necrosis** | May mimic carcinoma | Usually history of trauma; may calcify |
| **Granulomatous mastitis** | Firm mass; may mimic carcinoma | Younger women; may have systemic symptoms |
| **Lymphoma (primary breast)** | Firm mass; may be bilateral | Rare; usually younger; systemic symptoms |
| **Metastasis to breast** | Known primary elsewhere (melanoma, ovarian) | Rare; usually known history |

### Why This is Highly Suspicious for Malignancy
🔴 **The combination of age (65), firm/fixed consistency, irregular borders, and upper outer quadrant location strongly suggests invasive carcinoma until proven otherwise.** Upper outer quadrant contains the most glandular tissue and is the most common site for breast cancer (~50%).

### Investigation Protocol

#### Step 1: Clinical Assessment
- Detailed history (duration, skin changes, nipple retraction, bloody discharge, weight loss, bone pain)
- Full breast and axillary examination
- Assess for inflammatory signs (erythema, peau d'orange → inflammatory carcinoma)

#### Step 2: Triple Assessment
1. **Clinical** — suspicious mass (BI-RADS 4 or 5)
2. **Imaging:**
   - **Mammography** — spiculated mass, microcalcifications, architectural distortion
   - **Ultrasound** — solid, irregular, hypoechoic mass with posterior shadowing
   - **MRI** — assess extent of disease, chest wall invasion, contralateral breast
3. **Tissue Diagnosis:**
   - **Core needle biopsy** (mandatory) — histopathology + IHC (ER, PR, HER2, Ki-67)
   - If multicentric → stereotactic or MRI-guided biopsy

#### Step 3: Staging Workup (if malignancy confirmed)
- **Blood tests:** CBC, liver function, ALP, calcium, CA-15-3 (monitoring)
- **Chest X-ray** — lung metastases
- **Ultrasound abdomen** — liver metastases
- **Bone scan** — skeletal metastases (if symptomatic or elevated ALP)
- **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis** — for Stage III/IV
- **PET-CT** — if metastatic disease suspected
- **Sentinel lymph node biopsy** — for staging axilla

#### Step 4: Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) Discussion
- Surgery (lumpectomy vs mastectomy)
- Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (if large tumor or to downstage)
- Adjuvant therapy based on molecular subtype
- Radiation therapy
- Genetic counseling if indicated

> 🎯 "65 yr + firm/fixed/irregular mass = invasive carcinoma until proven otherwise. Triple assessment → core biopsy + IHC → staging → MDT."
