# Item 26 — Cytopathology Techniques: FNAC, Pap Smear, Cytological Staining & Fixatives

<summary><b>Q1. What are the types of cytopathology? Mention with examples.</b></summary>

### Definition
🔴 **Cytopathology (exfoliative + aspiration cytology) = the study of DISEASE at the level of individual CELLS.** Unlike histopathology (which studies intact tissue architecture), cytopathology examines **cells** that have been **exfoliated (shed), scraped, or aspirated** from the body — it answers "what cells are present and are they benign or malignant?" but loses the tissue architecture.

### Types of cytopathology
| Type | Method | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Exfoliative cytology** | Cells **shed/spontaneously desquamated** into a cavity or fluid | Sputum, urine, CSF, pleural/peritoneal fluid, nipple discharge |
| **2. Abrasive cytology** | Cells **scraped/brushed** off a surface | **Pap smear (cervix)**, esophageal/endobronchial **brushings**, sputum scrape, BAL |
| **3. Aspiration cytology (FNAC)** | Cells **aspirated** from a lesion through a thin needle | **Fine needle aspiration cytology** of thyroid, breast, lymph node, salivary gland, soft tissue |
| **4. Touch (imprint) cytology** | Fresh tissue gently **pressed** on a slide | Rapid confirmation during surgery (lymph nodes, margins) |
| **5. Fluid/serous cavity cytology** | Cells from aspirated body fluids | Pericardial, pleural, ascitic fluid |

> 🎯 "Exfoliative = cells fall off by themselves; abrasive = we scrape them; aspiration = we suck them out; imprint = we press tissue on glass."

> 📖 Orell & Sterrett, Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology, 5th ed.; Koss' Diagnostic Cytology — FNAC & exfoliative cytology chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499

<summary><b>Q2. Short note: Exfoliative cytology, Abrasive cytology.</b></summary>

### Exfoliative cytology
- Study of cells that **shed spontaneously** (exfoliate) from a surface into a lumen or cavity.
- The desquamated cells are collected from the **fluid/cavity** and examined for benign vs malignant changes.
- **Specimens:** **sputum** (lung), **urine** (bladder), **CSF**, **pleural/peritoneal/pericardial effusions**, nipple discharge, gastric washings.

### Abrasive cytology
- Cells are **actively scraped, brushed or abraded** off an epithelial surface to increase cell yield.
- **Specimens:** **cervical Pap smear**, **endobronchial brushings**, esophageal brushings, gastric brushings, bladder washings.

| Feature | Exfoliative | Abrasive |
|---|---|---|
| Cell acquisition | **Spontaneous shedding** | **Mechanical scraping/brushing** |
| Typical samples | Sputum, urine, effusion fluid | **Pap smear**, bronchial brushing, esophageal brushing |
| Cell preservation | May degenerate in the fluid | Better, fresher cells |

> 🎯 "Pap smear = the classic ABRASIVE (scraped) cytology; urine = the classic EXFOLIATIVE cytology."

> 📖 Orell & Sterrett, Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology, 5th ed.; Koss' Diagnostic Cytology — FNAC & exfoliative cytology chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499

<summary><b>Q3. What is FNAC. Mention the advantages of FNAC.</b></summary>

### Definition
🔴 **FNAC = Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology — a technique in which cells are aspirated from a palpable or image-detected lesion using a THIN needle (22–25 G), smeared onto slides, stained and examined microscopically.** It is a cytological (not histological) method — cells only, no tissue architecture.

### Advantages (over biopsy and other methods)
1. 🔴 **Minimally invasive** — thin needle, no incision, done in OPD.
2. 🔴 **Quick / rapid result** — often same day (or within hours) → early diagnosis & early treatment.
3. **Cheap & cost-effective** — no OT time, minimal equipment.
4. **OPD / bedside / portable** — can be done at the bedside or in clinics.
5. **No anaesthesia** needed (local anaesthesia usually unnecessary).
6. **Complications are rare & minor** (bruise, mild pain, very low infection risk).
7. **Can sample deep-seated organs** under image guidance (USG/CT) — e.g., lung, liver, kidney, pancreas.
8. **Repeatable** — safe to repeat for follow-up of lesions (e.g., goitre, benign lumps).
9. **Screening role** — rapid triage of a lump (e.g., breast lump → benign vs malignant → decides surgery).
10. **Multiple sites** can be sampled in one sitting; also useful in developing-country and rural settings.

### Disadvantages (for balance)
- **Cells only — no architecture**, so invasion/stroma cannot be assessed.
- **Sampling error** — a negative result does not exclude malignancy.
- Risk of **haemorrhage** in vascular/coagulopathic patients; small risk of **needle-tract seeding** (rare).

> 🎯 "FNAC = cells, cheap, fast, repeatable, safe, no anaesthesia — but no architecture, so biopsy decides final surgery."

> 📖 Orell & Sterrett, Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology, 5th ed.; Koss' Diagnostic Cytology — FNAC & exfoliative cytology chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499

<summary><b>Q4. Describe shortly the procedure of FNAC.</b></summary>

### Equipment
- 10–20 ml syringe (or **syringe holder/pistol** for one-handed control) with **22–25 G needle** (🔴 thin needle).
- Glass slides, fixative (95% ethanol / spray fixative), container for transport.
- Antiseptic, sterile gauze, gloves.

### Steps
1. **Prepare the patient & equipment** — explain the procedure, obtain consent; sit/position the patient.
2. **Localize the lesion** — by palpation (superficial) or **image guidance (USG/CT)** for deep lesions.
3. **Clean the skin** over the lesion with antiseptic.
4. **Fix the lump** between two fingers of the non-dominant hand (to immobilize it).
5. **Insert the needle** directly into the lesion (no local anaesthetic needed usually).
6. **Aspirate** — pull the plunger to create **negative pressure**, and make **multiple rapid to-and-fro movements** in different directions within the lesion (10–20 passes) to dislodge cells.
7. **Release negative pressure BEFORE withdrawing** the needle (prevents the sample from being sucked into the syringe hub).
8. **Withdraw the needle**; press the site with sterile gauze (to prevent haematoma).
9. **Smear** — a drop of aspirate is expelled onto a slide; make **thin smears** (spread between two slides — the "pull/push" or sandwich method) so cells form a single layer.
10. **Fix & stain** — **wet-fix in 95% ethanol or spray-fix** (for **Pap stain**) or **air-dry** (for **MGG/Giemsa**) → then examine microscopically.
11. **Report** — categorize as benign, suspicious, malignant, or inadequate/insufficient (needs repeat).

> 🎯 "Fix the lump → insert thin needle → to-and-fro with negative pressure → release pressure before pulling out → smear → fix/air-dry → stain → report."

> 📖 Orell & Sterrett, Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology, 5th ed.; Koss' Diagnostic Cytology — FNAC & exfoliative cytology chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499

<summary><b>Q5. What types of needle is used in FNAC? Mention the complications of FNAC.</b></summary>

### Needles
| Needle gauge (G) | Use |
|---|---|
| **22–25 G** (🔴 standard "fine" needle) | **Routine superficial FNAC** — breast, thyroid, lymph node, salivary glands |
| **25–27 G** (very thin) | Very vascular / small lesions, children |
| **21 G** | Slightly larger when thicker material expected (rare) |
| **Core biopsy needles (14–18 G)** | NOT FNAC — these take tissue (histology) |

🔴 "Fine needle" = outer diameter **0.6–0.9 mm (≤ 22 G)**. The thinness is what makes FNAC safe and painless.

### Complications (rare & minor)
1. **Pain / mild discomfort** at the puncture site.
2. **Local haematoma / bruising** (usually self-limiting).
3. **Bleeding** — significant haemorrhage is rare (screen for coagulopathy; caution in vascular lesions e.g. carotid-body tumour).
4. **Infection** — very low; risk if asepsis is broken or necrotic/infected cysts are punctured.
5. **Pneumothorax** — for deep thoracic lesions (lung FNAC under image guidance).
6. **Bowel perforation** — abdominal deep lesions (rare).
7. **Vasovagal reaction** / fainting in anxious patients.
8. 🔴 **Needle-tract seeding** — implantation of malignant cells along the needle track — **very rare** with fine needles; one reason FNAC is preferred over large-bore/core needles in e.g. thyroid.
9. **False negative/positive** — a "complication" of interpretation (sampling error → repeat/biopsy).

> 🎯 "FNAC is SAFE (thin needle) — main risks: bruising, haematoma; rare: infection, pneumothorax, needle-tract seeding (very rare with fine needles)."

> 📖 Orell & Sterrett, Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology, 5th ed.; Koss' Diagnostic Cytology — FNAC & exfoliative cytology chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499

<summary><b>Q6. How the tumors in deep seated organs (Liver, lung) can be sampled for cytopathology?</b></summary>

Deep-seated organs (lung, liver, pancreas, kidney, thyroid mediastinum, retroperitoneum) cannot be palpated — they are sampled under **IMAGE GUIDANCE**:

1. **Ultrasound (USG)-guided FNAC** — thyroid, liver, kidney, pancreas, breast deep lesions, abdominal masses — real-time, cheap, no radiation.
2. **CT-guided FNAC** — lung nodules, mediastinal/retroperitoneal masses, pancreas — precise targeting of small deep lesions.
3. **Fluoroscopy-guided** — some lung/diaphragm lesions.
4. **Endoscopic routes (endosonography):**
   - **EBUS-TBNA (endobronchial ultrasound + transbronchial needle aspiration)** — mediastinal nodes, peribronchial lesions.
   - **EUS-FNA (endoscopic ultrasound)** — pancreas, subepithelial GI lesions, mediastinum.
   - **ERCP brushing** — biliary/pancreatic ducts.
5. **Image-guided fine needle** (22–25 G) gives cells; sometimes a **core needle** (for architecture) is preferred for deep lesions.
6. **Route & safety:** approach planned to avoid vessels/bowel; after sampling, imaging may be repeated to check for **haemorrhage/pneumothorax**; USG/CT guidance → allows aspiration of only a few millilitres from cysts/fluid collections (fluid sent for cytology + biochemistry/microbiology).

> 🎯 "Deep organs: USG/CT-guided FNAC; mediastinum/bronchi: EBUS-TBNA; pancreas: EUS-FNA."

> 📖 Orell & Sterrett, Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology, 5th ed.; Koss' Diagnostic Cytology — FNAC & exfoliative cytology chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499

<summary><b>Q7. Mention the names cytological staining methods. Which one is used commonly?</b></summary>

| Stain | Type / cells shown | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Papanicolaou (Pap) stain** | 🔴 Nuclear & cytoplasmic detail; polychromatic (blue + pink + orange) | **Gynaecological smears** (cervical Pap), fluids, urine, sputum, FNAC smears — best for nuclear morphology |
| **May–Grünwald–Giemsa (MGG) / Romanowsky (Leishman, Wright, Diff-Quik)** | 🔴 Cytoplasmic granules, haematological cells, mucin vacuoles | **Lymph nodes, bone marrow, body fluids, FNAC** — best for cytoplasm; must be **air-dried** |
| **Hematoxylin–Eosin (H&E)** | Routine nuclear/cytoplasmic contrast | FNAC smears fixed in alcohol |
| **Alcian blue / PAS** | Mucin, glycogen, fungi | Distinguishing mucin-producing tumours |
| **Immunocytochemistry (ICC)** | Specific antigens (CK, CD markers, TTF-1, HER2) | Tumour classification & lineage |
| **Congo red** | Amyloid | Amyloid on cytology (rarely) |
| **Oil Red O / Sudan** | Fat | Lipid vacuoles in cells |

### Key principle
- 🔴 **Pap stain is used commonly / most routinely** — for gynaecological (cervical Pap), fluid, urine and sputum cytology.
- 🔴 **MGG/Giemsa is equally common in FNAC practice** (lymph nodes, bone marrow, fluids) — best for cytoplasmic detail.
- **Pap stain** → **wet-fixed** smears (alcohol/spray) → nuclear detail.
- **Giemsa (MGG)** → **air-dried** smears → cytoplasmic detail.
- 🔴 **Two stains are complementary** — labs often do BOTH on FNAC (one wet-fixed for Pap, one air-dried for MGG).

> 🎯 "Pap = wet-fixed, nuclei; MGG/Giemsa = air-dried, cytoplasm. Use both in FNAC."

> 📖 Orell & Sterrett, Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology, 5th ed.; Koss' Diagnostic Cytology — FNAC & exfoliative cytology chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499

<summary><b>Q8. Name the fixatives used in cytopathology. Which one is most commonly used? What is coating fixative?</b></summary>

| Fixative | Use |
|---|---|
| **95% Ethanol (alcohol)** | 🔴 **Routine wet fixation** of cytology smears (Pap stain) — precipitates protein, preserves nuclear detail |
| **Commercial spray fixative (e.g., Carbowax / polyethylene glycol)** | **Coating/refixation fixative** — covers smears with a polymer film that keeps cells moist & preserves them during transport; removed before staining |
| **Methanol** | Fixative for **Romanowsky/Giemsa stains** (MGG) |
| **Cytolyt / PreservCyt (methanol-based) & ThinPrep®** | **Liquid-based cytology (LBC)** — cells suspended in fixative, processed into a monolayer |
| **Acetone** | Some immunocytochemistry |
| **Formalin (10%)** | Cell blocks — the cellular sediment is processed like tissue for histology + IHC |
| **Bouin's** | Rare; cytogenetic/specific work |
| **No fixative → air-drying** | For Giemsa (air-dried smears are the fix method) |

### Key principles
- 🔴 **Most commonly used fixative = 95% ethanol (alcohol)** — the routine wet fixative for cytology smears (Pap stain).
- 🔴 **Coating fixative = a spray containing Carbowax (polyethylene glycol) in alcohol** — sprayed onto a fresh smear it forms a **thin plastic-like polymer film** that seals the cells, **prevents air-drying artefacts** and preserves them during **transport** when a jar of ethanol is unavailable; the film is dissolved off before staining.
- **Wet fixation (alcohol/spray)** → preserves **nuclear detail** → needed for **Pap stain**.
- **Air-drying** → preserves **cytoplasm** → needed for **Giemsa/MGG**.
- 🔴 **Spray (coating) fixatives** are the practical alternative when 95% ethanol is unavailable — they are also ideal for **transporting** smears from peripheral centres.

> 🎯 "Ethanol/spray = wet-fix for Pap; air-dry = for Giemsa; Cytolyt/LBC = liquid-based; formalin = cell blocks."

> 📖 Orell & Sterrett, Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology, 5th ed.; Koss' Diagnostic Cytology — FNAC & exfoliative cytology chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499

<summary><b>Q9. Between Biopsy & FNAC; Which in one is better for diagnosis & Why?</b></summary>

| Feature | **FNAC (cytology)** | **Biopsy (histology)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Sample** | **Cells** only | **Tissue** — intact architecture |
| **Needle** | Thin (**22–25 G**) | **Core needle (14–18 G)** or surgical excision/incision |
| **Procedure** | OPD, no anaesthesia | OT/invasive, may need anaesthesia |
| **Time to result** | **Rapid (hours/same day)** | Days (fixation + processing) |
| **Diagnostic scope** | Cell type/benign–malignant (screening/triage) | 🔴 **Tumour type, grade, invasion, margins, architecture** |
| **Special tests** | Limited (some ICC, cell block) | 🔴 **IHC, special stains, molecular tests, receptors** |
| **Invasiveness/risk** | Minimal; bruise/haematoma; very rare seeding | Bleeding, infection, anaesthetic risk, more tissue damage |
| **Cost** | Low | Higher |
| **Gold standard** | Screening / rapid triage | 🔴 **Definitive (gold standard) diagnosis** |
| **False negatives** | More (sampling error) | Fewer |
| **Interpretation** | Cytologist (pathologist) | Histopathologist |

### Bottom line
> 🔴 **FNAC = cheap, quick, safe, repeatable — a "screening/triage" tool; Biopsy = provides tissue architecture, grading, invasion and IHC — the GOLD STANDARD for final diagnosis and treatment planning.** They are complementary: FNAC first → if malignant or suspicious → biopsy for confirmation and subtyping.

> 🎯 "FNAC screens (fast, cheap, cells); biopsy confirms (architecture, grade, invasion, IHC) — FNAC asks the question, biopsy gives the final answer."

> 📖 Orell & Sterrett, Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology, 5th ed.; Koss' Diagnostic Cytology — FNAC & exfoliative cytology chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499
