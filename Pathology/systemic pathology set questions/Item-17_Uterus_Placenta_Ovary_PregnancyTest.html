Q1. Name the tumors of uterus.

Endometrial (epithelial)

  • Endometrial polyp (benign, exophytic, estrogen-responsive)
  • Endometrial hyperplasia (typical / atypical — EIN)
  • Endometrial carcinoma — 🔴 Type I endometrioid (80–85%) vs Type II serous (~15%); also clear cell, mucinous
  • Endometrial stromal tumours — stromal nodule (benign), low/high-grade endometrial stromal sarcoma
  • Adenosarcoma (benign glands + malignant stroma)
  • Carcinosarcoma / malignant mixed müllerian tumour (MMMT) — carcinoma + sarcoma components

Myometrial (mesenchymal)

  • 🔴 Leiomyoma (fibroid) — the MOST COMMON TUMOR IN WOMEN (benign smooth muscle; MED12 mutations ~70%)
  • Leiomyosarcoma — rare, arises de novo, mitoses/atypia/necrosis
  • Intravenous leiomyomatosis, disseminated peritoneal leiomyomatosis (benign variants)
🎯 "Most common uterine tumour = leiomyoma; most common invasive cancer of the female genital tract = endometrial (endometrioid) carcinoma."
📌 Mam's slide (Dr. Sharmeen Jahan) — Endometrial carcinoma (see Endometrial Pathology PDF): most common invasive cancer of the female genital tract in developed countries; usually after menopause (peak 55–65 yr). Type I (endometrioid, ~80%) — excess estrogen, endometrial hyperplasia, PTEN/PIK3CA/KRAS mutations, microsatellite instability; generally favorable prognosis. Type II (serous) — not estrogen related, arises in atrophic endometrium, TP53 mutation; more aggressive.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291
Q2. What do you mean by endometriosis? Name the common sites of endometriosis.

Definition

🔴 Endometriosis = the presence of endometrial glands + stroma OUTSIDE the uterus (stroma-only variants exist). ~10% of women; peak 3rd–4th decades. Clinical: infertility, dysmenorrhea, pelvic pain.

Common sites (descending frequency)

  1. 🔴 Ovaries (most common — "chocolate cysts"/endometriomas)
  2. Uterine ligaments
  3. Rectovaginal septum
  4. Cul-de-sac (pouch of Douglas)
  5. Pelvic peritoneum
  6. Serosa of bowel/appendix
  7. Mucosa of cervix/vagina/tubes
  8. Laparotomy scars (and rarely lung/brain — benign metastasis)

Pathogenesis theories

Regurgitation (retrograde menstruation — explains most peritoneal cases) · benign metastasis (vascular/lymphatic) · metaplastic (coelomic epithelium) · extrauterine stem cells. Implants make aromatase (absent in normal endometrium) → local estrogen; ↑ 3-fold risk of ovarian endometrioid + clear cell carcinoma.

🎯 "Endometriosis = glands + stroma outside the uterus — ovary #1, chocolate cyst. Adenomyosis = endometrium INSIDE the myometrium."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291
Q3. What do you mean by adenomyosis? Mention the complications of adenomyosis.

Definition

🔴 Adenomyosis = endometrial glands ± stroma WITHIN the myometrium, remaining in continuity with the endometrium (down-growth of basal endometrium into the muscle). Found in up to 20% of uteri.

Complications / clinical features

  • Menometrorrhagia (heavy, prolonged bleeding)
  • Colicky dysmenorrhea (worse pain)
  • Dyspareunia, premenstrual pelvic pain
  • Enlarged, boggy uterus (diffuse thickening); may coexist with endometriosis
  • Less commonly: subfertility
🎯 "Endometriosis = outside the uterus; adenomyosis = inside the myometrium."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291
Q4. What is DUB? What are the causes of abnormal uterine bleeding?

Definition

DUB (dysfunctional uterine bleeding) = abnormal uterine bleeding WITHOUT a structural abnormality (no tumour, polyp, pregnancy, or infection). It is the most common overall cause of abnormal bleeding, and most often results from anovulation — estrogen unopposed by progesterone. Most common at the two ends of reproductive life (menarche and perimenopause).

Causes of abnormal uterine bleeding (by age)

Age groupCauses
PrepubertalPrecocious puberty
AdolescentAnovulatory cycles + coagulation disorders
Reproductive agePregnancy complications (abortion, trophoblastic disease, ectopic) + anatomic lesions (leiomyoma, adenomyosis, polyps, hyperplasia, carcinoma)
PerimenopausalDUB + anatomic (above)
PostmenopausalAtrophy + anatomic (carcinoma, hyperplasia, polyps)
🎯 "DUB = bleeding without a structural lesion — usually anovulatory (unopposed estrogen)."
📌 Mam's slide (Dr. Sharmeen Jahan): AUB = abnormal uterine bleeding: excessive, irregular, prolonged bleeding, or bleeding occurring outside the normal menstrual cycle. Common causes by age: - Adolescents: most common = anovulatory cycles (immature hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis; no ovulation → no progesterone → continuous estrogen stimulation); other causes = coagulation disorders (von Willebrand disease, platelet disorders), endocrine abnormalities. - Reproductive age: pregnancy-related (spontaneous abortion, ectopic pregnancy, gestational trophoblastic disease); structural (leiomyoma, endometrial polyps, adenomyosis); infections (endometritis, PID); endocrine (PCOS, thyroid disease, hyperprolactinemia); functional (anovulatory cycles). - Perimenopausal: anovulatory cycles, endometrial hyperplasia, leiomyomas, endometrial polyps (irregular ovarian function, unopposed estrogen). - Postmenopausal: endometrial atrophy (most common), endometrial polyps, endometrial hyperplasia, endometrial carcinoma. Any postmenopausal bleeding should be considered endometrial carcinoma until proven otherwise.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291
Q5. Short note: Leiomyomas/ Fibroids.

Overview

🔴 Leiomyoma = the MOST COMMON TUMOR IN WOMEN — benign smooth-muscle tumour of the myometrium, single or (usually) multiple. Hormonally responsive (estrogen-dependent; grow in reproductive years, shrink after menopause). MED12 mutations in ~70% (causative); ~40% have a chromosomal abnormality (12q14/HMGIC, 6p/HMGIY).

Locations

Intramural · Submucosal (→ abnormal bleeding, bulge into cavity) · Subserosal.

Gross

Sharp circumscription + whorled cut surface; variable size (tiny to huge).

Microscopy

Uniform spindle cells with oval nuclei, slender bipolar (eosinophilic) cytoplasm, arranged in whorls/fascicles; 🔴 scarce mitoses (key vs sarcoma).

Clinical

  • Bleeding (menorrhagia), urinary frequency/pressure, sudden pain from infarction of a large/pedunculated tumour.
  • Impaired fertility; in pregnancy → spontaneous abortion, malpresentation, uterine inertia, PPH.
  • 🔴 Malignant transformation is extremely rare — leiomyosarcoma arises DE NOVO, not from a fibroid.

Variants (all still benign)

Bizarre nuclei, cellular, intravenous leiomyomatosis (extends into vessels → vena cava/right atrium), disseminated peritoneal leiomyomatosis.

🎯 "Leiomyoma = MED12, whorled, scarce mitoses, benign. Leiomyosarcoma = de novo, ≥10 mitoses/10 HPF + atypia + necrosis."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291

Q7. Classify germ cell tumor of ovary. What is dermoid cyst?

Classification of ovarian germ cell tumours

  • Teratomas (the "differentiated" group):
  • Mature (benign) cystic teratoma — DERMOID CYST (most common benign)
  • Immature (malignant) teratoma — primitive neuroepithelium, grade I–III
  • Monodermal/specialized: struma ovarii (pure thyroid), carcinoid, strumal carcinoid
  • Malignant non-teratomatous:
  • Dysgerminoma (ovarian seminoma) — most common malignant GCT
  • Yolk sac (endodermal sinus) tumour — 2nd most common malignant; AFP+
  • Choriocarcinoma (nongestational — chemo-resistant)
  • Embryonal carcinoma, polyembryoma
  • Mixed germ cell tumours

Dermoid cyst (mature cystic teratoma)

🔴 The most common benign ovarian germ cell tumour, in young women. A unilocular cyst filled with hair + sebaceous material; wall shows stratified squamous epithelium + skin adnexa, with all germ layers (cartilage, bone, thyroid, neural tissue). Bilateral 10–15%. Karyotype 46,XX (arises from an ovum after the first meiotic division). ~1% undergo malignant transformation — most often SCC. Paraneoplastic limbic encephalitis possible.

🎯 "Dermoid = mature cystic teratoma: hair + sebum + all 3 germ layers; 1% → SCC."
📌 Mam's slide (Dr. Sharmeen Jahan): Dermoid cyst (mature cystic teratoma): most common germ cell tumor; usually benign; contains mature tissues from all three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm); gross — hair, teeth, sebaceous material; complications — ovarian torsion, rare malignant transformation. Malignant GCTs: dysgerminoma (most common malignant GCT; ovarian counterpart of seminoma; large uniform cells with clear cytoplasm + fibrous septa infiltrated by lymphocytes; highly radiosensitive, excellent prognosis), yolk sac tumor (AFP, Schiller-Duval bodies), immature teratoma (malignant potential depends on grade), embryonal carcinoma, non-gestational choriocarcinoma, mixed GCT.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291
Q8. What do you mean by Krukenberg tumor?

🔴 Krukenberg tumour = a BILATERAL metastatic ovarian tumour containing mucin-filled SIGNET-RING cells, usually from gastric carcinoma (also colon, breast, biliary, pancreas). The ovaries are typically enlarged bilaterally, firm, and often retain an ovarian shape. It is a metastatic (secondary) tumour, NOT a primary ovarian neoplasm.

🎯 "Krukenberg = bilateral ovarian mets with signet-ring cells → think stomach."
📌 Mam's slide (Dr. Sharmeen Jahan): Krukenberg tumor = metastatic tumor of the ovary (usually from gastric carcinoma); other primaries — colon, breast, endometrium, pancreas.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291
Q9. What is Meigs syndrome?

🔴 Meigs syndrome = the triad of an OVARIAN FIBROMA (>6 cm) + ASCITES + RIGHT-SIDED HYDROTHORAX. The ascites and pleural effusion disappear after removal of the fibroma. Association with basal cell nevus (Gorlin) syndrome.

🎯 "Fibroma + ascites + right hydrothorax = Meigs syndrome — cured by surgery."
📌 Mam's slide (Dr. Sharmeen Jahan): Meigs syndrome = ovarian fibroma + ascites + pleural effusion.

Q10. Short note: Dysgerminoma.

Definition

🔴 Dysgerminoma = the ovarian counterpart of testicular SEMINOMA. 2% of ovarian cancers but ~50% of malignant ovarian germ cell tumours; 75% occur in the 2nd–3rd decades; associated with gonadal dysgenesis.

Histology

Sheets of large uniform vesicular cells with clear cytoplasm separated by fibrous septa containing lymphocytes; ± noncaseating granulomas; syncytiotrophoblastic giant cells → mild hCG.

IHC / markers

Stem-cell markers OCT3, OCT4, NANOG; activating KIT mutations in ~⅓ (therapeutic target); hCG mild if syncytiotrophoblasts.

Clinical

80–90% unilateral; all malignant but only ~⅓ aggressive → confined unilateral = up to 96% cure; overall survival >80%; chemo-responsive; radiosensitive (like seminoma).

🎯 "Dysgerminoma = ovarian seminoma: uniform clear cells + lymphocytic septa; OCT3/4+, KIT; 96% cure if confined."
📌 Mam's slide (Dr. Sharmeen Jahan): Dysgerminoma — most common malignant germ cell tumor; ovarian counterpart of seminoma of the testis. Microscopy: large uniform cells with clear cytoplasm; fibrous septa infiltrated by lymphocytes. Prognosis: highly radiosensitive; excellent prognosis.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291
Q11. Enumerates the functions of placenta.
  1. Materno-fetal exchange — oxygen, CO₂, nutrients (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids), electrolytes, waste (urea, bilirubin) across the placental membrane.
  2. Hormone production — 🔴 hCG (from syncytiotrophoblast), hPL (human placental lactogen), estrogen, progesterone, relaxin.
  3. Immunologic barrier/protection — selective passage of maternal IgG; local immune modulation preventing rejection of the fetus.
  4. Metabolic function — glycogen storage, synthesis of hormones/steroids, enzyme production.
  5. Protective barrier — restricts many drugs, toxins and organisms (though not all: alcohol, HIV, rubella, CMV, toxoplasma cross).
🎯 "Placenta = materno-fetal exchange + hormones (hCG, hPL, estrogen, progesterone) + immunologic barrier + metabolism."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Pregnancy Test, p. 493
Q12. Enumerates the indications of pregnancy test.
  1. Diagnosis of pregnancy — the most common indication.
  2. Evaluation of abnormal pregnancy — threatened/missed abortion, ectopic pregnancy (serial quantitative hCG), molar pregnancy (markedly elevated hCG).
  3. Monitoring — hydatidiform mole and gestational trophoblastic disease (follow-up hCG for 6–12 months); choriocarcinoma (hCG monitoring).
  4. Assessment of fetal well-being in early pregnancy (serial β-hCG).
  5. Pre-operative / pre-radiological safety check — before procedures that could harm an undiagnosed pregnancy.
  6. Screening in assisted reproduction — confirmation after IVF.
🎯 "Pregnancy test indications: diagnose pregnancy, evaluate abnormal pregnancy (ectopic/mole), monitor trophoblastic disease."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Pregnancy Test, p. 493
Q13. Mention the methods employed in pregnancy test?
  • Urine tests (qualitative, home/immunochromatographic): Monoclonal antibody-based immunoassays (dipstick/cassette) detecting β-hCG — rapid, 5–10 min.
  • Serum tests:
  • Qualitative β-hCG — presence/absence.
  • Quantitative β-hCG (radioimmunoassay / ELISA / chemiluminescence) — measures the level; essential for monitoring (mole, ectopic).
  • Principle (all): Antigen–antibody reaction — anti–β-hCG antibody captures the hormone; a labelled second antibody produces a colour signal.
  • Old methods: bioassays (Friedman, Aschheim-Zondek) — historical, using ovulation in rabbits/mice; no longer used.
🎯 "Pregnancy tests = immunoassays for β-hCG (urine qualitative; serum quantitative)."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Pregnancy Test, p. 493
Q14. Give the principle of pregnancy tests. How pregnancy is diagnosed immunologically?

Principle

🔴 All pregnancy tests detect HUMAN CHORIONIC GONADOTROPIN (hCG) — a glycoprotein hormone secreted by the syncytiotrophoblast of the placenta, present in maternal blood and urine from ~8–10 days after conception. Detection is by antigen–antibody (immunologic) reaction using monoclonal antibodies specific for the β-subunit of hCG (α-subunit cross-reacts with LH/FSH/TSH — so β-specificity is essential).

Immunologic diagnosis (steps)

  1. Urine or serum sample containing hCG is applied to the test.
  2. A monoclonal anti-β-hCG antibody (capture) binds the hormone; a second labelled antibody reacts with the bound hCG.
  3. A visible colour change / band / fluorescence indicates hCG → positive pregnancy test.
  4. Quantitative (serum) immunoassay gives a numerical value: doubling every ~48 h = healthy early pregnancy; abnormally high = molar pregnancy; slow rise/fall = ectopic or failing pregnancy.
🎯 "β-hCG + anti-β-hCG antibody → colour signal = pregnant. β-specificity avoids LH cross-reaction."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Pregnancy Test, p. 493
Q15. Short note: Pregnancy test.
  • What: A laboratory/point-of-care test detecting β-hCG in urine or serum — the earliest and most reliable marker of pregnancy (positive by ~8–10 days after conception).
  • Methods: Urine qualitative immunochromatography (home kits) · serum quantitative immunoassay (ELISA/CLIA/RIA) — the gold standard for monitoring.
  • Uses: Confirm pregnancy; serial values diagnose ectopic, missed/threatened abortion, and monitor hydatidiform mole / choriocarcinoma.
  • Limitations: False negative — early pregnancy, dilute urine; false positive — rare (hCG-producing tumours, menopause, recent abortion/mole). β-hCG also made by some GCTs (testicular/ovarian choriocarcinoma, dysgerminoma with syncytiotrophoblasts).
🎯 "Pregnancy test = β-hCG immunoassay; quantitative serum testing is the gold standard for monitoring."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Pregnancy Test, p. 493

## SAQ 4. Enumerate the types of serous ovarian tumors. Benign serous cystadenoma (→ cystadenofibroma) · Borderline (atypical proliferative) · Serous carcinoma (low-grade = micropapillary; high-grade).

📌 Mam's slide (Dr. Sharmeen Jahan): Serous tumors are the most common ovarian epithelial tumors (resemble fallopian tube epithelium). Benign serous cystadenoma: usually unilateral, thin-walled cyst with clear serous fluid, lined by ciliated columnar epithelium. Borderline: increased epithelial proliferation, no stromal invasion. Serous carcinoma: most common malignant ovarian tumor, frequently bilateral; high-grade serous carcinoma is the most common form — often originates from STIC (serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma) in the fimbrial end of the fallopian tube; TP53 mutations (>95%), BRCA1/BRCA2. Low-grade serous carcinoma: develops from borderline tumors; KRAS/BRAF mutations. Morphology: complex papillary structures, solid and cystic areas, psammoma bodies may be present. Spread: peritoneal dissemination common; omental involvement → "omental cake".
🎯 "Serous lineage: cystadenoma → borderline → carcinoma (low-grade vs high-grade)."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291

## SAQ 5. Enumerate the types of mucinous ovarian tumors. Benign mucinous cystadenoma · borderline · mucinous carcinoma. (The trichotomy benign → borderline → malignant per lineage.)

Pseudomyxoma peritonei = accumulation of mucin in the peritoneal cavity — usually originates from appendiceal mucinous tumors rather than ovarian tumors.

# PART C — Ovarian Tumor Cases (1–5)

## Case 1: Mature Cystic Teratoma

A 24-year-old woman presents with lower abdominal pain. Ultrasonography reveals a unilateral ovarian cyst containing calcified material, hair, and sebaceous material.

1. What is the most likely diagnosis? To which group of ovarian tumors does it belong? 🔴 Mature cystic teratoma (dermoid cyst). Group: germ cell tumors (teratomas).

2. Is it benign or malignant? Benign (mature). Malignant only if immature elements or transformation (~1%, usually SCC).

3. Mention two complications of this tumor. Torsion (the most common — pedunculated, mobile cyst), rupture (chemical peritonitis from sebum/hair), and rare malignant transformation (SCC).

4. Name the most common complication. 🔴 Ovarian torsion (twisting of the pedicle → infarction/acute pain).

📌 Mam's slide (Dr. Sharmeen Jahan): Mature cystic teratoma — most common germ cell tumor, usually benign; contains mature tissues from all three germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm); gross: hair, teeth, sebaceous material; complications: ovarian torsion, rare malignant transformation.
🎯 "Ovarian cyst with hair and sebum = dermoid cyst; chief danger = torsion."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291

## Case 2: Dysgerminoma

A 19-year-old woman presents with abdominal distension and pelvic mass. Histology shows sheets of large uniform cells with clear cytoplasm separated by fibrous septa containing lymphocytes.

1. What is the most likely diagnosis? 🔴 Dysgerminoma.

2. Classify this tumor. Malignant germ cell tumor (ovarian counterpart of seminoma).

3. Mention one important tumor marker. OCT3/OCT4/NANOG (stem-cell markers); also LDH; mild hCG if syncytiotrophoblasts; KIT mutations in ~⅓.

4. What is the testicular counterpart of this tumor? 🔴 Seminoma (CNS midline = germinoma).

5. Comment on its prognosis. Excellent — all malignant but only ~⅓ aggressive; confined unilateral → up to 96% cure; overall survival >80%; radiosensitive + chemosensitive.

📌 Mam's slide (Dr. Sharmeen Jahan): Dysgerminoma — most common malignant germ cell tumor; ovarian counterpart of seminoma of the testis; microscopy: large uniform cells with clear cytoplasm, fibrous septa infiltrated by lymphocytes; highly radiosensitive with excellent prognosis.
🎯 "Clear cells + lymphocytic septa = dysgerminoma — seminoma's twin, highly curable when confined."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291

## Case 3: Sertoli-Leydig Cell Tumor

A 28-year-old woman develops hirsutism, deepening of voice, amenorrhea, and clitoromegaly. Imaging reveals a unilateral ovarian mass.

1. What is the most likely diagnosis? 🔴 Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor.

2. Which hormone is likely secreted by this tumor? Androgens (testosterone) → virilization.

3. To which group of ovarian tumors does it belong? Sex cord–stromal tumors.

4. Mention two clinical features of virilization. Hirsutism + clitoromegaly (also deepening of voice, breast atrophy, amenorrhea — "defeminization").

5. Differentiate it from granulosa cell tumor regarding hormonal effect. Sertoli-Leydig = androgen → virilization/masculinization; granulosa cell = estrogen → feminizing effects (endometrial hyperplasia, precocious puberty, proliferative breast disease).

📌 Mam's slide (Dr. Sharmeen Jahan): Sertoli-Leydig cell tumor produces androgens → clinical manifestations: hirsutism, amenorrhea, virilization.
🎯 "Virilizing ovarian mass = Sertoli-Leydig cell tumour (androgen, sex cord–stromal)."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291

## Case 4: Clear Cell Carcinoma

A 45-year-old woman with long-standing endometriosis develops an ovarian mass. Histology shows malignant cells with clear cytoplasm.

1. What is the most likely diagnosis? 🔴 Clear cell carcinoma of the ovary.

2. Which category of ovarian tumor is this? Surface epithelial–stromal tumor (endometrioid-related family).

3. Mention its important association. 🔴 Endometriosis (strongly linked; arises from atypical endometriosis; ~3-fold endometrioid + clear cell risk).

4. Mention two common molecular abnormalities. PIK3CA, ARID1A, KRAS, PTEN, TP53 mutations (share the endometrioid/endometriosis pathway) — quote any two.

5. Comment on its response to chemotherapy. Poor relative to other epithelial tumors — advanced-stage clear cell morphology = poor; but confined to the ovary → 90% 5-yr survival.

📌 Mam's slide (Dr. Sharmeen Jahan): Clear cell carcinoma — composed of clear glycogen-rich cells; strongly associated with endometriosis; molecular changes: ARID1A, PIK3CA mutations; prognosis generally aggressive, often resistant to chemotherapy.
🎯 "Endometriosis → clear cell carcinoma; ARID1A/PIK3CA; relatively chemo-resistant."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291

## Case 5: Krukenberg Tumor

A 50-year-old woman presents with bilateral ovarian enlargement. Histology shows mucin-filled signet-ring cells in the ovarian stroma. Further investigation reveals gastric carcinoma.

1. What is the diagnosis? 🔴 Krukenberg tumor.

2. Is it a primary or secondary ovarian tumor? Secondary (metastatic) ovarian tumor.

3. What is the common primary site? 🔴 Stomach (gastric adenocarcinoma).

4. Why are the ovaries commonly bilateral in this condition? Bilateral involvement is characteristic of hematogenous/transcoelomic seeding of a circulating tumor that implants in both ovaries (symmetric desmoplastic response); ~both ovaries enlarge.

5. Mention two other organs that can give metastasis to the ovary. Breast and colon (also biliary tract, pancreas; müllerian primaries — endometrium, tube).

📌 Mam's slide (Dr. Sharmeen Jahan): Krukenberg tumor = metastatic ovarian tumor (usually from gastric carcinoma); other metastatic primaries: colon, breast, endometrium, pancreas.
🎯 "Bilateral signet-ring ovarian masses = Krukenberg tumour from gastric carcinoma."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 22 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1036 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Female Genital System, pp. 264–291
