Q1. Classify carcinoma breast according to morphology. Name the stromal tumors of the breast.

Morphological Classification of Carcinoma of the Breast

🔴 Almost all breast malignancies are adenocarcinomas. All arise from the terminal duct lobular unit (TDLU). By convention, "lobular" = biologically related to LCIS; "ductal" = cannot be classified as a special type.

I. Carcinoma In Situ (Non-invasive)

TypeKey Features
Ductal Carcinoma In Situ (DCIS)Clonal epithelial proliferation confined within ducts by basement membrane; myoepithelial cells preserved; detected by mammography (calcifications). Patterns: comedo (high-grade + central necrosis), cribriform (cookie-cutter spaces), micropapillary (no fibrovascular cores), papillary (true fibrovascular cores). Untreated ~1%/yr progress to invasive. Mastectomy cures >95%.
Lobular Carcinoma In Situ (LCIS)Dyscohesive cells from E-cadherin/CDH1 loss; incidental finding; no calcifications; bilateral 20–40%; risk marker (not obligate precursor); ER+/PR+, HER2−; no Paget disease.

II. Invasive Carcinoma

A. Invasive Carcinoma of No Special Type (NST) — ~70–80%

  • 🔴 Most common type; presents as hard, irregular mass with desmoplastic stromal reaction (grating sound like cutting water chestnut).
  • Nottingham Histologic Grade: tubule formation + nuclear pleomorphism + mitotic rate → Grade 1 (well-differentiated) / Grade 2 (moderately) / Grade 3 (poorly differentiated).

B. Special Histologic Types

SubgroupTypesKey Features
Luminal (ER+/HER2−)Invasive lobular, mucinous (colloid), tubular, papillaryLobular: CDH1 loss, single-file "Indian filing," minimal desmoplasia; mets to peritoneum/leptomeninges/GI/ovaries. Mucinous: cells floating in mucin lakes. Tubular: pure tubules, excellent prognosis.
HER2-proneApocrine, micropapillaryApocrine: sweat-gland appearance. Micropapillary: hollow balls of cells in fluid spaces.
TNBCMedullary pattern, metaplasticMedullary: soft pushing-border mass, dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate, BRCA1-associated, better prognosis. Metaplastic: spindle-cell and matrix-producing.
Favorable typesTubular, adenoid cystic, secretory, mucinousAll carry excellent prognosis regardless of grade.

C. Inflammatory Carcinoma

  • 🔴 Dermal lymphovascular plugging by carcinoma → erythema, swelling, peau d'orange. "Inflammatory" is a misnomer — usually no inflammation. No discrete mass. 3-yr survival 3–10%.

D. Paget Disease of the Nipple

  • 1–4% of breast cancers; malignant Paget cells from DCIS track via lactiferous sinuses into nipple skin; unilateral pruritic scaly eruption (eczema mimic). 50–60% have palpable mass → invasive carcinoma (usually ER−/HER2+).

Stromal Tumors of the Breast

🔴 Two stromas, two families:

TumorOriginKey Features
FibroadenomaIntralobular stromaMost common benign breast tumor; MED12 mutations (~2/3); 20s–30s; well-circumscribed rubbery nodule with slit-like spaces; pericanalicular/intracanalicular patterns; hormonally responsive.
Phyllodes tumorIntralobular stromaLeaf-like protrusions; 6th decade; higher cellularity, mitoses, stromal overgrowth; MED12 + TERT mutations; low-grade = local recurrence; high-grade = hematogenous stromal mets only (axillary dissection contraindicated).
MyofibroblastomaInterlobular stromaOnly breast tumor equally common in males.
FibromatosisInterlobular stromaClonal fibroblasts; locally aggressive, never metastasizes; associated with FAP/Gardner syndrome.
AngiosarcomaInterlobular stroma<0.05% of breast malignancies; sporadic (young) or post-radiation (older, 5–10 yr after RT).
LipomaInterlobular stromaBenign fat-containing tumor.

Pathogenesis of Sporadic Breast Cancer

🔴 Estrogenic stimulation + age are the most important risk factors. Estrogen drives proliferation → more cells "at risk" for DNA damage. Proof: tamoxifen reduces luminal cancers; postmenopausal HRT increases them.

3 Molecular Pathways:

  1. ER-positive (50–65%): Flat epithelial atypia → ADH/ALH → DCIS → invasive luminal cancer; PIK3CA mutations, 1q gain, 16q loss.
  2. HER2+ (~20%): HER2 amplification on 17q; no definite precursor.
  3. TNBC (~15%): BRCA1 pathway (germline or epigenetic); TP53 mutated in majority.

Diagnosis of Carcinoma Cervix

Clinical Presentation

  • Abnormal vaginal bleeding (postcoital, intermenstrual, postmenopausal)
  • Vaginal discharge (watery, bloody, foul-smelling)
  • Pelvic pain (advanced disease)
  • Contact bleeding

Breast Abscess — Detailed Discussion

Definition

🔴 Breast abscess = a localized collection of pus within the breast parenchyma, most commonly a complication of acute puerperal mastitis (lactational) or non-puerperal mastitis (non-lactational, central subareolar).

Etiology / Predisposing Factors

  • Lactational: cracked nipples → bacterial entry → S. aureus (most common organism); also Streptococcus, E. coli.
  • Non-lactational: smoking (damages ducts), retroareolar duct ectasia, periductal mastitis → mixed anaerobes + aerobes; recurrent abscesses common.

Pathogenesis

Bacterial entry (cracked nipple / duct obstruction)
   → Acute suppurative inflammation
   → Neutrophilic infiltration + tissue necrosis
   → Walled-off pus collection = ABSCESS
   →if untreated → chronic abscess, fistula formation

Clinical Features

  • Pain, swelling, redness, warmth in the breast
  • Fluctuant, tender mass with surrounding erythema
  • Fever, malaise, leukocytosis
  • Nipple discharge (purulent in lactational)
  • axillary lymphadenopathy (reactive)

Diagnosis

  • Clinical examination — fluctuant, tender mass
  • Ultrasound — confirms fluid collection (hypoechoic/anechoic collection)
  • Aspiration — diagnostic and therapeutic; send pus for culture and sensitivity
  • Mammography — may mimic carcinoma; needed to rule out underlying malignancy (especially in non-lactational)
  • Culture — identifies organism and guides antibiotic therapy

Management

  1. Antibiotics — empirical (flucloxacillin or cephalosporin) → targeted based on culture
  2. Incision and drainage (I&D) — the mainstay of treatment for established abscess
  3. Aspiration (needle) — alternative for small abscesses; may need repeat
  4. Continue breastfeeding (lactational) — to prevent milk stasis
  5. Stop smoking (non-lactational) — reduces recurrence
  6. Underlying pathology — rule out carcinoma (especially non-lactational, non-puerperal); biopsy if persistent mass

Complications

  • Chronic abscess / recurrent abscess
  • Fistula formation (especially non-lactational)
  • Mammary duct fistula
  • Cosmetic deformity
  • Rarely: sepsis, spread to chest wall
🎯 "Breast abscess = I&D + antibiotics + culture; non-lactational → think smoking + rule out underlying carcinoma."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 23 (The Breast), pp. 1037–1064 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I The Breast, pp. 296–309

Triple Assessment

🔴 Triple assessment = the gold standard clinical approach to evaluate any breast lump — combining three independent modalities to achieve near-100% diagnostic accuracy.

ComponentMethodPurpose
1. Clinical AssessmentHistory (age, family history, symptoms, duration, changes) + Clinical breast examination (inspection, palpation of lump and axillary lymph nodes)Characterize lump (size, location, consistency, mobility, skin changes, nipple retraction, axillary nodes)
2. ImagingMammography (≥40 yr or high-risk) + Ultrasound (all ages, first-line for <40 yr) ± MRI (high-risk screening, preoperative assessment)Detect mass, calcifications, architectural distortion; distinguish solid vs cystic; BI-RADS classification
3. Tissue DiagnosisFNAC (fine-needle aspiration cytology) or Core needle biopsy (preferred — preserves architecture, allows IHC) or Excisional biopsyHistopathological examination for definitive diagnosis; IHC (ER, PR, HER2, Ki-67) for molecular subtyping

Why Triple Assessment Works

  • Clinical + imaging alone have significant false-positive and false-negative rates
  • Adding tissue diagnosis raises accuracy to >99%
  • If all three agree benign → safe observation
  • If any component is suspicious/malignant → proceed to histological diagnosis and staging

# Part C — Fibroadenoma & Molecular Subtypes (Q5–6)

Q5. Mention the morphological features of fibroadenoma of breast.

Definition

🔴 Fibroadenoma = the most common benign tumor of the female breast, a biphasic neoplasm composed of proliferating intralobular stroma and epithelium. Driven by somatic MED12 mutations (~2/3 of cases).

Epidemiology

  • Peak incidence: 20s–30s years
  • Most common breast mass in women <35 years
  • Frequently multiple and bilateral (25–50%)
  • Hormonally responsive — grow during pregnancy, regress after menopause

Gross Morphology

  • 🔴 Well-circumscribed, rubbery, grayish-white, bulging nodule
  • Size: variable (0.5–15 cm; average 2–3 cm)
  • Cut surface: bulging, white-gray, glistening, with slit-like spaces lined by epithelium
  • In older women → hyalinized stroma + atrophic epithelium
  • May have cystic change, calcification (popcorn calcification on mammography)

Microscopic Morphology

FeatureDescription
Biphasic patternProliferating intralobular stroma + compressed epithelium-lined ducts
Pericanalicular patternStroma proliferates around ducts → open, oval/elongated spaces
Intracanalicular patternStroma proliferates within and compresses ducts → branching, slit-like, elongated spaces (more common)
EpitheliumTwo cell layers — inner luminal columnar + outer myoepithelial; may show usual ductal hyperplasia, apocrine metaplasia, sclerosing adenosis
StromaMyxoid in young patients → hyalinized with age; low cellularity; no atypia; rare mitoses
Stromal overgrowthRarely — if stroma predominates, consider phyllodes tumor

"Complex" Fibroadenoma Features

  • Cysts >0.3 cm, sclerosing adenosis, epithelial calcifications, papillary apocrine change
  • Slightly increased cancer risk (higher with complex features)

Clinical Features

  • Firm, mobile, painless, well-defined mass ("breast mouse" — rolls under fingers)
  • Usually discovered incidentally or during self-examination
  • Rapid growth during pregnancy can mimic carcinoma
  • Cyclosporin A (post-renal transplant) → multiple bilateral fibroadenomas

Treatment

  • Observation if small and asymptomatic
  • Surgical excision or vacuum-assisted biopsy if large, symptomatic, or growing
  • Recurrence is uncommon after complete excision
🎯 "Fibroadenoma = rubbery, mobile, circumscribed, slit-like spaces; MED12; pericanalicular/intracanalicular; breast mouse."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 23 (The Breast), pp. 1037–1064 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I The Breast, pp. 296–309

# Part D — Prognostic Factors & Gynecomastia (Q7–8)

Q7. What are the prognostic factors of breast cancer?

Overview

🔴 Prognostic factors tell the likely outcome; predictive factors tell the likely response to therapy. The AJCC 8th edition combines anatomic staging with prognostic factors (grade, ER, PR, HER2) to create a prognostic stage.

Prognostic Factors (Ranked by Importance)

FactorKey Facts
1. Distant Metastases (M)🔴 Most important prognostic factor; once present, cure is unlikely
2. Lymph Node Status (N)🔴 Second most important; 10-yr DFS: N0 = 70–80%; 1–3 nodes = 35–40%; >10 nodes = 10–15%. Sentinel node biopsy guides management.
3. Tumor Size (T)Node-negative: <1 cm → 10-yr survival >90%; >2 cm → 77%. Size correlates with nodal metastasis risk.
4. Histologic GradeGrade 3 (poorly differentiated) → worse survival than Grade 1 (well-differentiated)
5. ER/PR StatusER+/PR+ → best prognosis; responds to antiestrogens. ER−/PR− → worse prognosis.
6. HER2 StatusHER2+ → historically poor prognosis; now transformed by targeted therapy (trastuzumab). Predictive, not just prognostic.
7. Lymphovascular InvasionPresent in ~50%; predicts nodal mets + local recurrence + poor survival (if node-negative)
8. Special Histologic TypesFavorable: tubular, mucinous, adenoid cystic. Unfavorable: metaplastic, inflammatory.
9. Age<35 yr → worse prognosis (often TNBC); >70 yr → may have slower biology but comorbidities
10. Molecular SubtypeLuminal A = best; TNBC = worst (early relapse); HER2+ = intermediate (improved with targeted therapy)
11. Response to Neoadjuvant ChemoComplete pathological response (pCR) strongly prognostic for TNBC and HER2+; not for most luminal cancers
12. Gene Expression ProfilingOncotype DX, MammaPrint → identify low-risk patients who don't need chemotherapy

AJCC 8th Edition Staging

StageT / N / M10-yr Survival
0DCIS, N0, M097%
IInvasive ≤2 cm, N0 or micrometastases87%
II>2 cm with 0–3 positive LNs65%
III>5 cm ± LNs; ≥4 positive LNs; skin/chest wall/inflammatory40%

Prognostic Stage (New in 8th Edition)

  • Combines anatomic TNM + grade + ER + PR + HER2
  • TNBC may be "up-staged" for aggressive behavior
  • Gene expression assays may substitute for some parameters
🎯 "Distant mets > lymph node status > tumor size > grade > ER/PR/HER2. N0 = 70–80% 10-yr DFS; >10 nodes = 10–15%."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 23 (The Breast), pp. 1037–1064 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I The Breast, pp. 296–309
Q8. Short note: Gynecomastia.

Definition

🔴 Gynecomastia = benign proliferation of male breast glandular tissue, producing a palpable subareolar mass. It is NOT the same as pseudogynecomastia (fat deposition in obese men).

Epidemiology

  • Most common cause of male breast enlargement
  • Bimodal age distribution: neonatal (maternal estrogen), puberty (transient, resolves in 6 months–2 years), and adults >50 years (most common pathological group)
  • Up to 70% of pubertal boys develop some degree of gynecomastia
  • Unilateral or bilateral (bilateral in ~50%)

Pathogenesis

🔴 Mechanism = Estrogen/Androgen imbalance — either ↑ estrogen, ↓ testosterone, or ↑ peripheral aromatization of androgens to estrogens.

MechanismCauses
↑ EstrogenExogenous estrogen (topical creams), estrogen-producing tumors (testicular Leydig cell tumor, adrenal tumor, hepatocellular carcinoma), β-hCG-secreting tumors (germ cell tumors, lung cancer)
↓ TestosteroneHypogonadism (Klinefelter 47,XXY — most common genetic cause), Kallmann syndrome, orchiectomy, orchitis (mumps), testicular torsion/trauma, 5α-reductase deficiency, anti-androgen therapy (spironolactone, finasteride, cyproterone)
↑ Aromatase activityObesity (adipose aromatase), aging, chronic liver disease (impaired estrogen metabolism), hyperthyroidism
Drug-inducedSpironolactone, ketoconazole, cimetidine, HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors, isoniazid, methyldopa, estrogens, digitalis, penicillamine
Systemic diseaseChronic liver disease/cirrhosis (↑ aromatization + ↓ sex hormone-binding globulin), chronic renal failure, malnutrition/refeeding, hyperthyroidism

Morphology

  • Glandular proliferation — branching ducts lined by cuboidal/columnar epithelium with papillary projections, surrounded by loose, edematous, periductal stroma
  • No lobule formation (male breast lacks lobules normally)
  • Periductal fibrosis in long-standing cases
  • Distinguished from carcinoma by presence of two cell layers (luminal + myoepithelial) and absence of nuclear atypia/invasion

Clinical Features

  • Firm, rubbery, subareolar, mobile mass (disc-like, concentric around nipple)
  • Tender in early/acute phases; painless in chronic
  • Bilateral in ~50% of pathological cases
  • "Girlie" breast — fatty tissue + glandular tissue
  • ⚠️ Any unilateral, hard, eccentric mass in a male → suspect male breast cancer until proven otherwise

Investigation

  • History: drugs, liver disease, alcohol, testicular examination, family history
  • Hormonal profile: testosterone, estradiol, LH, FSH, β-hCG, prolactin
  • Imaging: mammography (differentiate glandular from fatty tissue); ultrasound (assess mass)
  • Biopsy if mass is suspicious or doesn't resolve after removing cause

Treatment

  • Remove underlying cause (stop offending drug, treat liver disease, etc.)
  • Observation — spontaneous resolution in many cases (especially pubertal)
  • Tamoxifen — first-line medical therapy; anti-estrogen effect
  • Surgery (mastectomy) — for persistent or cosmetically bothersome gynecomastia

When to Suspect Male Breast Cancer

  • Age >60, unilateral hard mass, skin ulceration, nipple retraction, bloody discharge, axillary lymphadenopathy
  • 1% of male breast enlargement is cancer
  • >90% are luminal (ER+); BRCA2 is the key gene (~6% of male carriers)
🎯 "Gynecomastia = estrogen/androgen imbalance; rule out male breast cancer (1%); Klinefelter = most common genetic cause; tamoxifen = first-line treatment."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 23 (The Breast), pp. 1037–1064 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I The Breast, pp. 296–309

Investigation Protocol

  • Trauma history

Step 2: Clinical Breast Examination

  • Characterize the lump (size, consistency, mobility, borders, skin changes)
  • Assess both breasts and axillary lymph nodes
  • Nipple examination (retraction, discharge)

Step 3: Imaging

  • Ultrasound (first-line for age <30 — dense breast tissue makes mammography less sensitive)
  • Characterize as solid vs cystic
  • BI-RADS classification

  • Send for: histopathology + IHC (ER, PR, HER2, Ki-67)

  • Staging workup: blood tests, chest X-ray, ultrasound abdomen, CT/MRI as indicated
  • Molecular subtyping for treatment planning
  • Genetic counseling if family history positive

Why This is Highly Suspicious for Malignancy

🔴 The combination of age (65), firm/fixed consistency, irregular borders, and upper outer quadrant location strongly suggests invasive carcinoma until proven otherwise. Upper outer quadrant contains the most glandular tissue and is the most common site for breast cancer (~50%).

Investigation Protocol

Step 1: Clinical Assessment

  • Detailed history (duration, skin changes, nipple retraction, bloody discharge, weight loss, bone pain)
  • Full breast and axillary examination
  • Assess for inflammatory signs (erythema, peau d'orange → inflammatory carcinoma)

Step 2: Triple Assessment

  1. Clinical — suspicious mass (BI-RADS 4 or 5)
  2. Imaging:
  • Mammography — spiculated mass, microcalcifications, architectural distortion
  • Ultrasound — solid, irregular, hypoechoic mass with posterior shadowing
  • MRI — assess extent of disease, chest wall invasion, contralateral breast
  1. Tissue Diagnosis:
  • Core needle biopsy (mandatory) — histopathology + IHC (ER, PR, HER2, Ki-67)
  • If multicentric → stereotactic or MRI-guided biopsy

Step 3: Staging Workup (if malignancy confirmed)

  • Blood tests: CBC, liver function, ALP, calcium, CA-15-3 (monitoring)
  • Chest X-ray — lung metastases
  • Ultrasound abdomen — liver metastases
  • Bone scan — skeletal metastases (if symptomatic or elevated ALP)
  • CT chest/abdomen/pelvis — for Stage III/IV
  • PET-CT — if metastatic disease suspected
  • Sentinel lymph node biopsy — for staging axilla

Step 4: Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) Discussion

  • Surgery (lumpectomy vs mastectomy)
  • Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (if large tumor or to downstage)
  • Adjuvant therapy based on molecular subtype
  • Radiation therapy
  • Genetic counseling if indicated
🎯 "65 yr + firm/fixed/irregular mass = invasive carcinoma until proven otherwise. Triple assessment → core biopsy + IHC → staging → MDT."
