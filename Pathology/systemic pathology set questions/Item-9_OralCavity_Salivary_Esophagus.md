## Q1. What are the diseases of oral cavity?

### Overview
📌 The oral cavity extends from the lips to the palatoglossal arch; its diseases fall into **6 major groups** — developmental/tooth-related, inflammatory/reactive, infections, premalignant, malignant, and odontogenic lesions.

| Group | Diseases |
|---|---|
| **Teeth & supporting structures** | **Dental caries** (demineralization of enamel/dentin by bacterial acids — leading cause of tooth loss before age 35) · **Gingivitis** (plaque/calculus; reversible) · **Periodontitis** (*Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Prevotella intermedia*; destroys periodontal ligament + alveolar bone → tooth loss) |
| **Reactive/inflammatory lesions** | **Aphthous ulcer** (canker sore) · **Irritation (traumatic) fibroma** (buccal bite line) · **Pyogenic granuloma** ("pregnancy tumour", gingiva) · Peripheral ossifying fibroma · Peripheral giant-cell granuloma |
| **Infections** | **HSV-1** (herpetic gingivostomatitis, herpes labialis) · **Candidiasis/thrush** (scrapable pseudomembrane) · **Hairy leukoplakia** (EBV, lateral tongue, HIV) · Deep fungal (mucormycosis, histoplasmosis) · Diphtheria, scarlet fever (strawberry tongue), syphilis |
| **Premalignant lesions** | **Leukoplakia** (white patch not scrapable; 5–25% premalignant) · **Erythroplakia** (red velvety patch; ~90% severe dysplasia/CIS) |
| **Malignant tumours** | **Squamous cell carcinoma (95%)** — oral cavity type (tobacco + alcohol, keratinizing) vs HPV-16 oropharyngeal type · Minor salivary gland malignancies |
| **Odontogenic cysts & tumours** | Dentigerous cyst · Keratocystic odontogenic tumour (Gorlin syndrome, PTCH) · Radicular (periapical) cyst · Odontoma (most common odontogenic tumour) · Ameloblastoma |

> 🎯 "Caries–gingivitis–periodontitis → aphthous/fibroma/granuloma → HSV/Candida/EBV → leuko/erythroplakia → SCC → odontogenic."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 16 (Head and Neck), pp. 731–751 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Head, Neck and Oral Cavity, pp. 136–143

## Q2. What are common infections of oral cavity?

### Common infections

| Infection | Agent | Key features | Diagnosis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Herpetic gingivostomatitis** | **HSV-1** | Children; vesicles → **red-rimmed shallow ulcers** on tongue/oral mucosa; adults → herpes pharyngitis | **Tzanck smear: multinucleate polykaryons + eosinophilic intranuclear (Cowdry A) inclusions**; latency in **trigeminal ganglion** → reactivation = herpes labialis (cold sore) |
| **Oral candidiasis (thrush)** | *Candida albicans* | **Most common oral fungal infection**; gray-white adherent **pseudomembrane that CAN be scraped off** leaving erythematous base; predisposed by immunosuppression, neutropenia, diabetes, broad-spectrum antibiotics, dentures | Scraping + KOH/culture shows budding yeasts + pseudohyphae |
| **Hairy leukoplakia** | **Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)** | White fluffy **non-scrapable** hyperkeratotic patches on **lateral border of tongue**; marker of immunodeficiency (**HIV/AIDS**) | Hyperparakeratosis + acanthosis + **balloon cells**; EBV DNA/RNA (ISH) in lesional cells; often superinfected with Candida |
| **Deep fungal infections** | Histoplasma, Blastomyces, Coccidioides, Cryptococcus, **Mucor/Zygomycetes**, Aspergillus | Ulcers/necrotic lesions in the **immunocompromised**; mucormycosis rapidly destructive | Biopsy with fungal stains (PAS/GMS) |
| **Bacterial/pharyngeal syndromes** | Streptococcus pyogenes, Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Treponema pallidum, Borrelia vincentii | Scarlet fever → **strawberry tongue**; diphtheria → **dirty-white tough membrane** over tonsils; primary syphilis → chancre; Vincent angina → necrotizing gingivitis | Culture / serology |
| **Systemic disease markers** | — | Koplik spots (measles, buccal mucosa near Stensen duct); gingival enlargement (monocytic leukemia, phenytoin); Ludwig angina from agranulocytosis spread | Clinical context |

🔴 **Scrape test:** thrush scrapes off; **hairy leukoplakia does NOT** — the classic viva discriminator.

> 🎯 "**Thrush scrapes, EBV hair doesn't** · HSV = Tzanck polykaryons + trigeminal latency."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 16 (Head and Neck), pp. 731–751 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Head, Neck and Oral Cavity, pp. 136–143

## Q3. What are tumours and pre-cancerous lesions of oral cavity?

### Pre-cancerous (potentially malignant) lesions

| Feature | **Leukoplakia** | **Erythroplakia** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition (WHO) | **White patch/plaque that cannot be scraped off** (not attributable to any other disease) | **Red, velvety, possibly eroded** patch |
| Frequency | ~3% of world population; peak 40–70 yr, M:F ≈ 2:1 | Much rarer |
| Favourite sites | Buccal mucosa, **floor of mouth, ventral tongue**, palate, gingiva | Floor of mouth, tongue, soft palate |
| Cause | Tobacco (all forms), alcohol, chronic friction | Same, but less defined |
| 🔴 Premalignant risk | **5–25% show dysplasia/carcinoma in situ or transform** | **~90% already severe dysplasia / CIS / invasive carcinoma** |
| Prognosis | Moderate danger | **Far more ominous** |

- Intermediate speckled form = **speckled leukoerythroplakia**.
- 📌 **Rule: "All leukoplakia is precancerous until proven otherwise" — biopsy every lesion.**

### Tumours

| Type | Lesions |
|---|---|
| **Benign (soft-tissue)** | Irritation fibroma · Pyogenic granuloma · Squamous papilloma (HPV 6/11) |
| **Odontogenic** | Odontoma (**most common**, hamartoma of enamel+dentin) · **Ameloblastoma** (cystic, slow-growing, **locally invasive**, wide resection needed) |
| **Malignant** | 🔴 **Squamous cell carcinoma = >95%** of oral cancers · Variants: verrucous, basaloid, spindle-cell · Minor salivary gland tumours (mucoepidermoid, adenoid cystic) · Melanoma, lymphoma (rare) |

### Squamous cell carcinoma — essentials
- 🔴 **Risk factors:** smoked tobacco + alcohol (synergistic, oral cavity); **betel-quid/paan chewing (India/Asia)**; HPV-16 (oropharynx — tonsil, base of tongue); actinic radiation + pipe smoking (lower lip).
- **Sites:** ventral/lateral tongue, floor of mouth, lower lip, soft palate, gingiva.
- **Histology:** invasive nests/sheets of **keratinizing squamous cells** with intercellular bridges, keratin pearls, dysplasia at margins; preceded by leukoplakia/erythroplakia.
- **Spread:** local infiltration → lymphatics to **submandibular, jugulodigastric, cervical nodes**; distant mets late (lungs, liver, bone).
- **Molecular:** **TP53, CDKN2A, PIK3CA, NOTCH1** mutations; **field cancerization** → second primaries 3–7%/yr.
- **Prognosis:** 5-yr survival ~80% (early) vs ~20% (late); HPV-positive oropharyngeal SCC behaves better.

> 🎯 "**Erythroplakia > leukoplakia in danger (90% vs 5–25%)** · SCC = 95% of oral cancer · tobacco+alcohol (oral) vs HPV-16 (oropharynx)."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 16 (Head and Neck), pp. 731–751 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Head, Neck and Oral Cavity, pp. 136–143

## Q4. Classify tumours of salivary gland / What are the malignant neoplasms of salivary gland?

### General rules (say these first)
- Salivary tumours = **<2% of all human tumours**; **65–80% arise in the PAROTID**; only ~5% occur before age 16.
- 🔴 **Malignant potential is INVERSELY proportional to gland size:** parotid ~15–20% malignant → submandibular ~40% → minor glands ~50% → **sublingual 70–90% malignant**.

### Classification

| Behaviour | Tumours |
|---|---|
| **Benign** | **Pleomorphic adenoma** (**most common overall**, ~60% of parotid tumours) · **Warthin tumour** (2nd most common; parotid; smokers) · Oncocytoma · Basal cell adenoma · Canalicular adenoma · Myoepithelioma |
| **Malignant** | **Mucoepidermoid carcinoma** (**most common malignant**) · **Adenoid cystic carcinoma** (perineural invasion) · **Acinic cell carcinoma** · **Carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma** · Adenocarcinoma NOS · Squamous cell carcinoma · Lymphoepithelial carcinoma · Rare: small-cell, clear-cell carcinoma |

### Malignant salivary neoplasms — key points

| Tumour | Site | Hallmarks |
|---|---|---|
| **Mucoepidermoid carcinoma** | 60–70% parotid | **t(11;19)(q21;p13) → CRTC1–MAML2 fusion**; mixture of **mucous + squamous + intermediate** cells ± cysts; graded low/intermediate/high (low grade: >90% 5-yr survival; high grade ~50%) |
| **Adenoid cystic carcinoma** | **50% in MINOR glands** (palate) | Cribriform "**Swiss-cheese**" pattern — small dark cells around hyaline basement-membrane material; **MYB–NFIB fusion**; 🔴 **perineural invasion** → pain/facial palsy; **late recurrence/metastasis** (bone, liver, brain) — survival 60–70% at 5 yr but only **~15% at 15 yr** |
| **Acinic cell carcinoma** | Parotid | Cells resemble serous acini with **purple zymogen granules**; may be bilateral/multicentric; ~90% 5-yr survival |
| **Carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma** | Parotid (long-standing PA) | Malignant transformation of pre-existing PA: **~2% if present <5 yr → ~10% if >15 yr**; aggressive (30–50% mortality) |
| **Adenocarcinoma NOS / SCC** | Any gland | Diagnosis of exclusion; poor prognosis |

> 🎯 "**PA most common overall, Mucoepidermoid most common malignant, Warthin 2nd benign** · 'Smaller gland ⇒ more malignant' · Adenoid cystic = Swiss cheese + perineural."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 16 (Head and Neck), pp. 731–751 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Head, Neck and Oral Cavity, pp. 136–143

## Q5. Give the morphology of pleomorphic adenoma / Give the histological features of pleomorphic adenoma / Short note on pleomorphic adenoma.

### Definition
**Pleomorphic adenoma (mixed tumour) = the most common benign salivary gland neoplasm**, composed of a **mixture of ductal (epithelial), myoepithelial and mesenchymal-like (chondromyxoid) elements** — hence "mixed tumour." Accounts for ~60% of parotid tumours; also submandibular/minor glands (palate). Molecular driver: **PLAG1 overexpression** (chromosomal rearrangement); **HMGA2** in PLAG1-negative cases.

### Clinical features
- Painless, slow-growing, **mobile**, firm swelling (parotid/preauricular region or palate); face of a middle-aged woman/man; 30–50 yr typical.
- Facial nerve **not involved** (unlike carcinoma) — nerve sparing is a hallmark.

### Gross morphology
- Rounded, well-demarcated, encapsulated-feeling mass, **4–6 cm**; 🔴 **capsule is INCOMPLETE/thin** (especially in palate) — tumour tongues extend beyond it.
- Cut surface: **gray-white, glistening, myxoid/chondroid (translucent blue-grey)**, firm areas, occasional cysts, haemorrhage.

### Histology (microscopic)
- **Ductal epithelial cells** forming ducts/acini lined by low cuboidal-to-columnar cells, often with an outer layer of modified **myoepithelial cells**.
- **Myoepithelial cells** — plasmacytoid, spindled, stellate or clear — in sheets, cords and islands.
- 🔴 **Mesenchymal-like stroma:** myxoid, chondroid (cartilage-like), mucinous, hyalinized, even osseous — **produced by the modified myoepithelial cells**, giving the "pleomorphic" appearance.
- No anaplasia/mitoses; intact basement membranes around ducts.

### Behaviour & complications
| Point | Data |
|---|---|
| Recurrence | **~25% after simple enucleation** vs only **~4% after superficial parotidectomy** (because capsule incomplete → satellite nodules left behind); recurrences often multinodular |
| Malignant transformation | **Carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma:** ~2% (<5 yr duration) → **~10% (>15 yr)**; heralded by rapid growth, fixation, facial palsy; extremely aggressive (30–50% 5-yr mortality) |
| Treatment | Superficial (conservative) parotidectomy with facial-nerve preservation |

> 🎯 "**Mixed = epithelial + myoepithelial + chondromyxoid** · incomplete capsule → recur (25% enucleation) · long-standing PA → carcinoma ex PA."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 16 (Head and Neck), pp. 731–751 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Head, Neck and Oral Cavity, pp. 136–143

## Q6. Why is it called pleomorphic adenoma?

- 🔴 The term **"pleomorphic" describes the ARCHITECTURAL diversity, not cellular pleomorphism (anaplasia)** — individual cells are actually bland and benign-looking.
- One tumour shows **multiple patterns simultaneously**: ducts, tubules, acini, solid sheets, cords, islands and cysts formed by epithelial + myoepithelial cells.
- In addition, the myoepithelial cells elaborate a **mesenchymal-like stroma** (myxoid, chondroid, osseous) — so the same tumour mimics both an epithelial tumour AND a connective-tissue tumour → old name **"mixed tumour."**
- Hence: **epithelium + mesenchyme-like matrix in varying proportions = pleomorphic appearance** (Willis: "structural polymorphism").
- 📌 Contrast: true nuclear pleomorphism/anaplasia would suggest **carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma**, which arises in long-standing cases.

```
Modified myoepithelial cell
   → secretes basement-membrane-like ground substance
   → myxoid / chondroid / osseous stroma
   + proliferating ductal epithelium (ducts, acini, sheets)
   = structurally "mixed"/pleomorphic tumour
```

> 🎯 "Pleomorphic = **many architectural patterns** (ducts + sheets + cartilage-like stroma), NOT many abnormal nuclei — cells stay benign."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 16 (Head and Neck), pp. 731–751 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Head, Neck and Oral Cavity, pp. 136–143

## Q8. What is achalasia? What are the causes?

### Definition
**Achalasia = a disorder of esophageal motility characterized by the triad of (1) incomplete relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES), (2) increased LES resting tone, and (3) aperistalsis of the esophageal body.**

### Pathogenesis
```
Loss/degeneration of NO-producing inhibitory neurons
of the myenteric (Auerbach) plexus
   → LES cannot relax + lost peristaltic wave (aperistalsis)
   → functional obstruction → food retention
   → progressive esophageal dilation + wall hypertrophy
   → "bird-beak" tapering on barium swallow
```

### Causes

| Type | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Primary achalasia** | Degeneration of **nitric oxide–producing (nitrergic) inhibitory neurons** of LES; rare; slight ↑ esophageal SCC risk (insufficient for routine surveillance) |
| **Secondary (pseudo-) achalasia** | 🔴 **Chagas disease** (*Trypanosoma cruzi* destroys myenteric plexus — also duodenum/colon/ureters) · Diabetic autonomic neuropathy · Amyloidosis · Sarcoidosis · Systemic sclerosis · Infiltrating gastric cardia carcinoma (tumour pseudoachalasia) · Polio · **Allgrove (triple-A) syndrome**: achalasia + alacrima + ACTH-resistant adrenal insufficiency |

### Clinical features
- Progressive **dysphagia to BOTH solids and liquids**, regurgitation of undigested food, chest pain, weight loss, difficulty belching.
- Barium swallow: **dilated esophagus with smooth bird-beak tapering at GEJ**; manometry confirms the triad.
- Treatment: laparoscopic **Heller myotomy**, pneumatic balloon dilation, botulinum toxin.

> 🎯 "**Triad: no relaxation + ↑ tone + aperistalsis** · primary = nitrergic neuron loss · secondary = **Chagas** · bird-beak."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 17 (Gastrointestinal Tract), pp. 753–822 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Gastrointestinal System, pp. 144–191

## Q9. What is Barrett's esophagus?

### Definition
**Barrett esophagus = intestinal metaplasia of the distal esophageal squamous mucosa caused by chronic gastroesophageal reflux (GERD), in which the normal stratified squamous epithelium is replaced by columnar epithelium containing goblet cells.**

### Key facts
| Aspect | Details |
|---|---|
| Epidemiology | Up to **10% of symptomatic GERD**, ~2% of general population; **white males 40–60 yr**; incidence rising |
| 🔴 Diagnostic requirement | Presence of **GOBLET cells** (pale-blue mucin vacuoles, wine-goblet shaped) in columnar-lined esophagus **above the GEJ**; non-goblet cardiac/foveolar metaplasia alone is controversial |
| Extent | Long segment **≥3 cm** vs short segment **<3 cm** (short segment → lower dysplasia/cancer risk) |
| Endoscopy | **Salmon-pink tongue-like mucosa** extending up from GEJ against pale squamous background |
| Histology | Goblet cells + columnar absorptive/foveolar cells; chronic inflammation; **low-grade/high-grade dysplasia** (hyperchromatic stratified nuclei, failure of surface maturation; cribriform glands in high-grade); lamina propria invasion = **intramucosal adenocarcinoma** |
| Molecular progression | Early **TP53 loss + CDKN2A (p16) loss/hypermethylation** → later amplification of **EGFR, ERBB2, MET, cyclin D1/E** |
| Significance | 🔴 **Strongly associated with esophageal ADENOCARCINOMA** (30–125× increased risk) → surveillance endoscopy; multifocal high-grade dysplasia treated by resection/endoscopic ablation (radiofrequency, photodynamic) |
| Outcome | Most patients never develop cancer; surveillance protocols remain debated |

> 🎯 "**Chronic GERD → goblet cells ABOVE the GEJ = Barrett** → dysplasia (TP53/CDKN2A) → adenocarcinoma."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 17 (Gastrointestinal Tract), pp. 753–822 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Gastrointestinal System, pp. 144–191

## Q10. What are esophageal varices?

### Definition
**Esophageal varices = abnormally dilated, tortuous subepithelial veins of the distal esophagus (and proximal stomach) that develop as portosystemic collateral channels when portal hypertension forces blood to shunt from the portal system into the systemic circulation.**

### Pathogenesis
```
Cirrhosis / hepatic schistosomiasis / other portal HTN
   → portal venous pressure ↑ (impaired flow through liver)
   → reversal of flow through LEFT GASTRIC (coronary) vein
   → engorgement of lower esophageal + proximal gastric veins
   → tortuous bulging submucosal varices
   → mucosal erosion / ↑ venous pressure → RUPTURE → hematemesis
```

### Key facts
- 🔴 **Cause: portal hypertension** — most common = **cirrhosis** (esp. alcoholic liver disease); worldwide #2 = **hepatic schistosomiasis**.
- Present in **30% of compensated and ~60% of decompensated cirrhosis**.
- Rupture risk factors: large/tortuous varices, raised hepatic venous pressure gradient, previous bleed, advanced liver disease (red wale marks, cherry-red spots).
- 🔴 **Each bleed carries 15–20% mortality; >50% rebleed within 1 year.**
- Morphology: longitudinal columns of dilated bluish submucosal veins just above the GEJ, thin-walled, eroded epithelium, thrombi possible.
- Treatment: prophylaxis **β-blockers** (↓ portal flow) + **endoscopic variceal ligation**; active bleed — sclerotherapy, balloon tamponade, TIPS.

> 🎯 "**Portal HTN → left gastric vein shunting → varices** · cirrhosis (US) vs schistosomiasis (worldwide) · 15–20% mortality per bleed, 50% rebleed."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 17 (Gastrointestinal Tract), pp. 753–822 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Gastrointestinal System, pp. 144–191

## Q11. Mention the sites of esophageal cancer.

### Anatomic sites

| Region | Predominant malignancy | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Upper third (cervical)** | Squamous cell carcinoma | Spreads to **cervical nodes** |
| 🔴 **Middle third** | 🔴 **Squamous cell carcinoma (~50% occur here)** | Strictures common; spreads to mediastinal, paratracheal, tracheobronchial nodes |
| 🔴 **Lower (distal) third** | 🔴 **Adenocarcinoma** (arising in Barrett esophagus; may invade gastric cardia) | Spreads to **gastric + celiac nodes** |

### Additional points
- Overall, **SCC remains the most common esophageal cancer worldwide**; **adenocarcinoma is now >half of US cases** (rising).
- 🔴 **Most common BENIGN esophageal tumour = leiomyoma** (mesenchymal).
- Clinical correlate: progressive dysphagia (solids → liquids), odynophagia, weight loss; **tracheoesophageal fistula** when mid-esophageal SCC invades the trachea.

> 🎯 "**SCC = upper & middle thirds; Adenocarcinoma (Barrett) = lower/distal third** · node map follows the third · benign = leiomyoma."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 17 (Gastrointestinal Tract), pp. 753–822 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Gastrointestinal System, pp. 144–191

## Q13. Barrett's esophagus is a premalignant condition - explain.

### Explanation (step-wise)
Barrett esophagus is premalignant because the metaplastic goblet-cell epithelium **accumulates sequential genetic alterations and passes through recognizable histologic stages ending in adenocarcinoma**:

```
Chronic GERD (acid + bile reflux)
   → squamous injury → repair
   → INTESTINAL METAPLASIA (goblet cells) = Barrett
   → LOW-GRADE dysplasia
   → HIGH-GRADE dysplasia (budding, cribriform glands)
   → intramucosal ADENOCARCINOMA (lamina propria invasion)
   → invasive adenocarcinoma of distal esophagus
```

### Molecular evidence (why the sequence is real)
| Stage | Molecular events |
|---|---|
| Barrett without dysplasia | **Early: TP53 loss/mutation + CDKN2A (p16) silencing by deletion or promoter hypermethylation** — drivers detectable even BEFORE histologic dysplasia |
| Progression | Amplification of **EGFR, ERBB2 (HER2), MET, cyclin D1, cyclin E** |

### Epidemiologic evidence
- Barrett carries **30–125× increased relative risk of adenocarcinoma**; adenocarcinoma arises **only within Barrett mucosa** (distal third).
- Incidence of Barrett-associated adenocarcinoma has risen in parallel with GERD/obesity epidemics.

### Practical implications (what makes it clinically "premalignant")
- Requires **surveillance endoscopy with biopsies** (4-quadrant protocol) to detect dysplasia.
- **High-grade dysplasia / multifocal lesions** → endoscopic mucosal resection, radiofrequency ablation, photodynamic therapy or esophagectomy.
- 📌 Caveat: **most patients with Barrett NEVER develop cancer** — risk is real but modest per year; surveillance benefit debated in trials.

> 🎯 "**Metaplasia → LGD → HGD → adenoCa** with TP53 + CDKN2A first, growth-factor amplification later — hence Barrett = premalignant (surveillance!)."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 17 (Gastrointestinal Tract), pp. 753–822 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Gastrointestinal System, pp. 144–191

## Q14. What are the malignancies of esophagus?

### Malignant tumours
1. 🔴 **Squamous cell carcinoma** — most common worldwide; middle third; keratinizing/nonkeratinizing nests recapitulating squamous epithelium; variants: verrucous, basaloid, spindle-cell.
2. 🔴 **Adenocarcinoma** — distal third; arises in **Barrett esophagus**; mucin-producing intestinal-type glands; signet-ring/small-cell variants possible; now >50% of US esophageal cancers.
3. Rare primary: small-cell (neuroendocrine) carcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, mucoepidermoid carcinoma, melanoma, lymphoma.
4. Secondary (metastatic) tumours — direct invasion from gastric cardia; distant metastases uncommon to esophagus.

### Adenocarcinoma vs SCC (comparison)

| Feature | Adenocarcinoma | Squamous cell carcinoma |
|---|---|---|
| Location | **Distal third** (from Barrett; may invade cardia) | **Middle third (~50%)** |
| Trend | Rising (now >half of US cases) | Declining relatively; still commonest worldwide |
| Risk factors | GERD/Barrett, obesity, tobacco, radiation | **Alcohol + tobacco**, caustic injury, achalasia, Plummer-Vinson, hot beverages |
| Molecular | TP53/CDKN2A early → EGFR/ERBB2/MET amplification | **SOX2 amplification**, cyclin D1, TP53, NOTCH1 loss, CDH1 |
| Morphology | Gland formation + mucin | Keratin pearls, intercellular bridges |
| Node spread | Gastric/celiac | Upper → cervical; middle → mediastinal/tracheobronchial; lower → gastric/celiac |
| Survival | 5-yr <25%; ~80% if confined to mucosa/submucosa | 5-yr ~75% superficial; <20% overall |

### Clinical course (both)
Progressive **dysphagia (solids → liquids) → odynophagia → obstruction**, weight loss/cachexia, hemorrhage, sepsis from ulceration, aspiration via **tracheoesophageal fistula**; diagnosis by **endoscopy + biopsy** (staging CT/PET-EUS).

> 🎯 "**Two big ones: SCC (middle, alcohol+tobacco) and Adenocarcinoma (distal, Barrett)** — everything else is rare."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 17 (Gastrointestinal Tract), pp. 753–822 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Gastrointestinal System, pp. 144–191

## Q15. Short note: Aphthous Ulceration, Leukoplakia.

### (a) Aphthous ulceration (canker sore)
**Definition: a recurrent, painful, shallow round-to-oval ulceration of the nonkeratinized oral mucosa with an erythematous halo, occurring singly or in crops.**
- **Most common oral mucosal lesion**; up to **40% of the population**; begins in the **first two decades**, recurs periodically, heals spontaneously in **7–10 days**.
- **Site:** lips, tongue, buccal mucosa, floor of mouth (movable, nonkeratinized mucosa).
- **Microscopy:** superficial ulcer covered by fibrinopurulent membrane over granulation tissue; mononuclear infiltrate at the base.
- 🔴 **Associations (systemic): celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn/UC), Behçet disease**; also stress, hormonal changes, trauma, nutritional deficiency.
- Treatment: topical steroids/anaesthetics; self-limiting.

### (b) Leukoplakia
**Definition (WHO): a predominantly white lesion/plaque of the oral mucosa that CANNOT be characterised clinically or pathologically as any other disease — i.e., a persistent, NON-SCRAPABLE white patch.**
- Frequency ~3% of adults; peak **40–70 yr**, male predominance; **tobacco is the common antecedent** (also alcohol, chronic friction, HPV).
- **Sites:** buccal mucosa, **floor of mouth, ventral tongue**, palate, gingiva.
- **Microscopy spectrum:** hyperkeratosis/parakeratosis + acanthosis → **mild/moderate/severe epithelial dysplasia → carcinoma in situ → invasive SCC**.
- 🔴 **Premalignant: 5–25% show dysplasia or progress to carcinoma** — therefore **biopsy every lesion**; excision of dysplastic lesions.
- Related lesion: **erythroplakia** (red velvety) — far more ominous (~90% severe dysplasia/CIS).

> 🎯 "**Aphthous = painful recurrent shallow ulcer + celiac/IBD/Behçet** · **Leukoplakia = non-scrapable white patch, 5–25% precancer, always biopsy.**"

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 16 (Head and Neck), pp. 731–751 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Head, Neck and Oral Cavity, pp. 136–143
