## Q1. Classify the diseases of Kidney. Name the cystic kidney diseases.

### Classification of diseases of kidney

| Category | Diseases |
|---|---|
| **1. Congenital / developmental anomalies** | Renal agenesis (bilateral/unilateral), hypoplasia, ectopic kidney, horseshoe kidney, multicystic dysplasia |
| **2. Glomerular diseases** | **Nephritic syndrome** (acute/post-streptococcal GN, IgA nephropathy), **nephrotic syndrome** (minimal change disease, FSGS, membranous nephropathy, MPGN), **RPGN (crescentic GN)**, chronic glomerulonephritis; systemic diseases involving glomeruli (SLE, diabetes mellitus, amyloidosis) |
| **3. Tubulointerstitial diseases** | **Acute tubular injury/necrosis (#1 cause of AKI)**, acute & chronic **pyelonephritis**, drug-induced (hypersensitivity) interstitial nephritis, analgesic nephropathy, urate/gouty nephropathy, light-chain (myeloma) cast nephropathy |
| **4. Vascular diseases** | Benign & malignant **nephrosclerosis**, renal artery stenosis, thrombotic microangiopathies (**HUS/TTP**), atheroembolic disease, sickle cell nephropathy, renal infarction, diffuse cortical necrosis |
| **5. Obstructive uropathy & urolithiasis** | Hydronephrosis, renal calculi, reflux nephropathy |
| **6. Cystic diseases** | See list below |
| **7. Neoplasms** | Benign — renal papillary adenoma, oncocytoma, angiomyolipoma; malignant — **renal cell carcinoma**, urothelial (transitional cell) carcinoma of renal pelvis, **Wilms tumour** (children) |

### Cystic kidney diseases

| Disease | Inheritance / setting | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| **ADPKD** (adult polycystic kidney disease) | AD (**PKD1 16p13, PKD2 4q21**) | Bilateral huge cystic kidneys + **liver cysts + berry aneurysms**; renal failure 40–60 yr |
| **ARPKD** (childhood polycystic kidney disease) | AR (**PKHD1 → fibrocystin**) | Enlarged spongy kidneys + **congenital hepatic fibrosis** |
| **Medullary sponge kidney** | None (sporadic) | Medullary collecting-duct cysts; **recurrent stones**; benign |
| **Familial juvenile nephronophthisis** | AR | Corticomedullary cysts; salt wasting, polyuria; **most common genetic cause of childhood ESRD** |
| **Multicystic renal dysplasia** | Sporadic | Irregular cysts + **cartilage islands**; mimics tumour |
| **Acquired cystic kidney disease** | Long-term **dialysis** | Multiple cysts; hemorrhage; ↑ risk of **RCC** |
| **Simple (solitary) cortical cysts** | Ageing | Single/few cysts; benign, incidental |

> 🎯 **Remember:** "CG-TVCON" — Congenital, Glomerular, Tubulointerstitial, Vascular, Cystic, Obstructive, Neoplastic · Cysts: "**Adult Dominant (PKD1/2 + berry aneurysm), Child Recessive (fibrocystin + liver fibrosis)**."

## Q2. Mention the developmental anomalies of kidney. Classify Glomerular diseases.

### Developmental anomalies of kidney

| Anomaly | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Renal agenesis** | **Bilateral** = incompatible with life (Potter sequence, stillbirth, hypoplastic lungs); **unilateral** = compatible — remaining kidney undergoes compensatory hypertrophy, later risk of glomerulosclerosis |
| **Renal hypoplasia** | Small kidney with **no scars**, reduced number of lobes/pyramids (**≤6**); bilateral → childhood renal failure |
| **Ectopic kidney** | Located in pelvis/above pelvic brim; ureteral kinking → obstruction → infection/stones |
| **Horseshoe kidney** | **Fusion of lower poles (90%)** across midline anterior to great vessels; incidence **1 in 500–1000**; prone to obstruction, stones, Wilms tumour association |
| **Renal dysplasia** | Abnormal differentiation with undifferentiated mesenchyme ± cartilage; multicystic form mimics tumour |

📌 ~**10% of people** have some congenital anomaly of the urinary system.

### Classification of glomerular diseases

**A. By aetiology:**

| Group | Diseases |
|---|---|
| **Primary (idiopathic) glomerular diseases** | Minimal change disease, FSGS, membranous nephropathy, MPGN (type I + dense deposit disease), post-infectious (post-streptococcal) GN, **IgA nephropathy**, hereditary nephritis (Alport), anti-GBM disease, crescentic (RPGN) GN, chronic GN |
| **Secondary / systemic** | **Diabetes mellitus**, **SLE (lupus nephritis)**, amyloidosis, Henoch-Schönlein purpura, Goodpasture syndrome, ANCA vasculitides, thrombotic microangiopathies (HUS/TTP), infections (HBV, HCV, HIV, malaria), drugs (NSAIDs, penicillamine, gold) |

**B. By clinical syndrome:** nephritic · nephrotic · rapidly progressive (crescentic) · chronic renal failure · asymptomatic isolated haematuria/proteinuria.

> 🎯 **Remember:** "Children → Minimal Change; Adults → FSGS/Membranous; Systemic big three → **Diabetes, Lupus, Amyloid**."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q3. What are the clinical manifestations of renal disease? Why anaemia occurs in CKD patient?

### Clinical manifestations of renal disease

| Manifestation | Description |
|---|---|
| **Azotemia** | Elevated blood urea nitrogen (BUN) & creatinine due to ↓ GFR |
| **Uremia** | Azotemia + clinical signs/symptoms (pericarditis, encephalopathy, gastritis) of advanced renal failure |
| **Nephritic syndrome** | Haematuria, RBC casts, oliguria, hypertension, azotemia, mild proteinuria |
| **Nephrotic syndrome** | Heavy proteinuria ≥3.5 g/day, hypoalbuminemia, oedema, hyperlipidemia, lipiduria |
| **Acute kidney injury (AKI)** | Rapid ↓ GFR over hours–days with rising urea/creatinine ± oliguria |
| **Chronic kidney disease (CKD)** | Slowly progressive, irreversible loss of functioning nephrons over years |
| **Renal tubular defects** | Polyuria, nocturia, salt wasting, renal tubular acidosis (impaired concentration/acidification) |
| **Urinary tract infection** | Cystitis, pyelonephritis |
| **Urolithiasis** | Calculi → renal colic, hematuria |
| **Others** | Hydronephrosis, cystic diseases, renal tumours (hematuria, loin pain, mass) |

### Why anaemia occurs in CKD

1. **↓ Erythropoietin (EPO) production** 🔴 — damaged peritubular interstitial cells fail to secrete EPO → **hypoproliferative normocytic normochromic anaemia** (the principal mechanism).
2. **Uremic toxins suppress the bone marrow** (direct myelosuppression).
3. **Shortened RBC lifespan** — circulating red cells damaged by the uremic milieu.
4. **Iron & folate deficiency** — dietary restriction, malabsorption, chronic blood loss (platelet dysfunction → GI bleeding, haemodialysis circuit losses).
5. **Associated inflammation** (↑ hepcidin) → functional iron blockade.

> 🎯 **Remember:** "CKD anaemia = **no EPO + poisoned marrow + dying RBCs + lost iron** — treat with recombinant EPO + iron."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q4. What is azotemia and uremia?

### Definitions

🔴 **Azotemia = elevated blood levels of urea (and other nitrogenous waste products such as creatinine) resulting from a reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR).** It is a **biochemical** abnormality.

🔴 **Uremia = the clinical syndrome in which azotemia progresses to symptomatic systemic illness** — it denotes the biochemical **plus** clinical signs and symptoms of advanced/established renal failure affecting virtually every organ system.

### Types/causes of azotemia

| Type | Site of problem | Common causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-renal** | Before the kidney (↓ perfusion) | Hypovolemia (haemorrhage, dehydration, burns), cardiac failure, shock, cirrhosis, renal artery stenosis |
| **Renal (intrinsic)** | Within the kidney | **Acute tubular injury (#1)**, glomerulonephritis, acute interstitial nephritis, vasculitis, HUS/TTP, chronic kidney disease |
| **Post-renal** | Below the kidney (obstruction) | Calculi, BPH, tumours (bladder/cervix/prostate), posterior urethral valves, retroperitoneal fibrosis, neurogenic bladder |

### Clinical manifestations of uremia

- Pericarditis (fibrinous), pleuritis · **Encephalopathy** (confusion → asterixis → coma) · neuropathy
- **Gastroenteritis/gastritis**, nausea, anorexia, halitosis (ammoniacal odour)
- Bleeding tendency (platelet dysfunction), anaemia, pruritus, skin pallor/"uremic frost"
- Metabolic: hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, hypocalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism

> 🎯 **Remember:** "Azotemia = **waste in blood**; Uremia = azotemia + **whole-body sickness**. Pre-Renal → fix the plumbing pressure; Renal → parenchyma dead; Post-renal → pipe blocked."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q5. What are the different mechanisms involved in glomerular injury?

### Immunologic mechanisms (principal)

| Pattern | Mechanism | Immunofluorescence (IF) pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Immune complex deposition** | **Circulating** immune complexes lodge in glomeruli, OR **in situ** complex formation against planted (exogenous microbial) or intrinsic antigens (e.g., **PLA2R** in membranous nephropathy; SpeB in post-strep GN) | **Granular** deposits of Ig + complement |
| **2. Anti-GBM antibody mediated** | Autoantibodies against the **α3 chain of collagen IV (NC1 domain)** shared by GBM & alveolar BM → Goodpasture syndrome | **Linear** ribbon-like IgG along GBM |
| **3. Pauci-immune (ANCA-mediated)** | **ANCA** (anti-MPO/p-ANCA, anti-PR3/c-ANCA) activates primed neutrophils → necrotizing GN with **no antibody/complement deposits** | Negative (pauci-immune) |
| **4. Cell-mediated immunity** | Sensitized **T cells/macrophages** delay-type hypersensitivity injury (contributes in crescent formation, minimal change disease) | — |
| **5. Alternative complement pathway activation** | Dysregulation (C3 nephritic factor, factor H mutation) → **C3 glomerulopathies** (dense deposit disease) | Isolated C3 deposits |

### Mediators & amplification of injury

- **Complement — C5b-C9 membrane attack complex**: lysis + stimulates mesangial cells → oxidants & proteases → capillary wall damage.
- **Cytokines**: IL-1, TNF, chemokines (MCP-1) → leukocyte recruitment.
- **Growth factors**: **PDGF** → mesangial proliferation; **TGF-β** → ECM accumulation → **glomerulosclerosis**; VEGF maintains endothelium.
- **Coagulation cascade**: fibrin leakage into Bowman space drives **crescent formation**.
- **Non-immunologic progression:** once GFR falls to 30–50% of normal, **hyperfiltration & intraglomerular hypertension** injure surviving glomeruli → secondary focal segmental sclerosis + tubulointerstitial fibrosis (final common pathway to ESRD regardless of initial cause).

```text
Antigen exposure (microbial / self e.g. PLA2R / collagen IV)
        ↓
Immune complex formation (circulating or in situ)  OR  Anti-GBM autoantibody  OR  ANCA-primed neutrophils
        ↓
Complement activation (classical → C3a/C5a chemotaxis; C5b-C9 MAC)
        ↓
Neutrophils + monocytes + mesangial proliferation → endothelial/podocyte injury
        ↓
GBM damage → proteinuria + hematuria ; fibrin leaks into Bowman space → crescents
        ↓
PDGF/TGF-β → ECM accumulation → glomerulosclerosis + tubulointerstitial fibrosis → CKD/ESRD
```

> 🎯 **Remember:** "**Granular = immune complex, Linear = anti-GBM, Nothing = ANCA**; TGF-β scars, PDGF multiplies, fibrin makes crescents."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q6. Name the primary glomerular diseases. What is the role of immunofluorescence microscopy in diagnosis of glomerular diseases?

### Primary (idiopathic) glomerular diseases

1. **Minimal change disease**
2. **Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)**
3. **Membranous nephropathy**
4. **Membranoproliferative GN (MPGN)** — type I & dense deposit disease
5. **Acute post-infectious (post-streptococcal) GN**
6. **IgA nephropathy (Berger disease)**
7. **Hereditary nephritis — Alport syndrome**
8. **Anti-GBM antibody (linear) glomerulonephritis**
9. **Crescentic (rapidly progressive) GN**
10. **Chronic glomerulonephritis**

### Role of immunofluorescence (IF) microscopy in diagnosis

📌 IF is performed on **frozen renal biopsy tissue** using fluorescein-tagged antibodies against human IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q, fibrin — it localizes and characterizes immune deposits, which is often **the decisive diagnostic step**.

| IF finding | Diagnosis suggested |
|---|---|
| **Granular** IgG + C3 along GBM (subepithelial) | Membranous nephropathy (anti-**PLA2R** positivity), post-streptococcal GN ("starry sky") |
| **Granular IgA + C3 confined to mesangium** (C1q/C4 absent) | **IgA nephropathy / Henoch-Schönlein purpura** |
| **Linear** IgG (+C3) along GBM | **Anti-GBM disease / Goodpasture syndrome** |
| **No deposits** (pauci-immune) | **ANCA-associated crescentic GN**, minimal change disease (IF negative) |
| Granular "full house" (IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q) | **Lupus nephritis** |
| IgM + C3 in sclerotic segments only | FSGS |
| Granular IgG + C3 + C1q subendothelial, tram-track | MPGN type I |
| C3 only (no immunoglobulin/C1q) | Dense deposit disease (C3 glomerulopathy) |

**Roles summarised:** (1) confirms presence/absence of immune deposits; (2) **identifies pattern (granular vs linear vs none)** → separates immune-complex, anti-GBM and pauci-immune disease; (3) identifies the immunoglobulin class (IgA vs IgG) and complement components; (4) subclasses disease (e.g., IgG4 in primary membranous); (5) guides classification of lupus nephritis classes I–VI.

> 🎯 **Remember:** "**L**inear = anti-GBM · **Gr**anular = complexes · **No stain = ANCA or MCD** · Mesangial IgA = Berger."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q7. Name the Glomerular syndromes with their manifestations.

| Syndrome | Manifestations |
|---|---|
| **1. Nephritic syndrome** | **Haematuria (dysmorphic RBCs + RBC casts)**, mild-to-moderate proteinuria (<3.5 g/day), **oliguria**, **hypertension**, periorbital oedema, **azotemia** (↓GFR); smoky/cola-coloured urine |
| **2. Nephrotic syndrome** | **Heavy proteinuria ≥3.5 g/day/1.73 m²**, **hypoalbuminemia (<3 g/dL)**, **generalised pitting oedema**, **hyperlipidemia + lipiduria** (oval fat bodies); increased susceptibility to infection & thromboembolism (lost antithrombin III) |
| **3. Rapidly progressive (crescentic) GN — RPGN** | Acute nephritic picture with **rapid loss of renal function over weeks–months**; severe oliguria/anuria; **crescents** on biopsy |
| **4. Chronic renal failure (chronic GN syndrome)** | Insidious onset, slowly progressive azotemia developing over years; polyuria/nocturia, hypertension, anaemia, bilateral small kidneys; ends in **ESRD** |
| **5. Asymptomatic urinary abnormalities** | Isolated (sub-nephrotic) proteinuria or isolated microscopic hematuria picked up on routine urinalysis (thin basement membrane disease, IgA nephropathy) |

> 🎯 **Remember:** "**Nephritic = Blood + BP; Nephrotic = Frothy + Puffy; RPGN = nephritic on fast-forward; Chronic = silent killer; Asymptomatic = biopsy surprise.**"

## Q8. Define nephritic syndrome. Mention the common causes of nephritic syndrome.

### Definition

🔴 **Nephritic syndrome is a clinical syndrome caused by inflammatory (proliferative) injury of glomeruli, characterized by haematuria with RBC casts, oliguria, hypertension, azotemia and mild-to-moderate proteinuria (usually <3.5 g/day).**

### Common causes of nephritic syndrome

| Category | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Infectious (post-infectious)** 🔴 | **Acute post-streptococcal GN** (most common in children), infective endocarditis, shunt nephritis, visceral abscess |
| **Primary GN** | **IgA nephropathy** (most common GN worldwide), mesangioproliferative GN |
| **Rapidly progressive GN** | Type I anti-GBM (Goodpasture), type II immune complex, type III pauci-immune (ANCA — GPA, microscopic polyangiitis) |
| **Systemic diseases** | **SLE (diffuse proliferative lupus nephritis class IV)**, **Henoch-Schönlein purpura**, cryoglobulinemia |
| **MPGN** | Type I & dense deposit disease |
| **Hereditary** | **Alport syndrome** |

> 🎯 **Remember:** "Bloody child 1–2 weeks after sore throat = **post-strep**; bloody young adult with synpharyngitic episodes = **IgA**; bloody smoker with hemoptysis = **Goodpasture**."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q9. Clinical Presentation of AGN & Nephrotic syndrome.

| Feature | **Acute glomerulonephritis (AGN)** | **Nephrotic syndrome (NS)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Typical patient** | Child 6–10 yr, 1–4 weeks after streptococcal pharyngitis/impetigo | Child 2–6 yr (MCD) or adult |
| **Onset** | Abrupt | Insidious (except post-infectious) |
| **Urine** | **Smoky/cola-coloured (gross hematuria)**, oliguria, RBC casts, mild proteinuria | **Frothy urine** — massive proteinuria ≥3.5 g/day, no RBC casts |
| **Edema** | Mild, **periorbital/facial** (morning puffiness) | **Generalised pitting oedema** — periorbital, legs, ascites, pleural effusion, anasarca |
| **Blood pressure** | **Hypertension** (fluid retention + renin) | Usually normal |
| **Renal function** | ↓ GFR → azotemia | Preserved early (MCD) |
| **Other features** | Malaise, fever, nausea, flank pain | Susceptibility to **pneumococcal/staphylococcal infection**, **renal vein thrombosis/thromboembolism** (lost antithrombin III), growth faltering |
| **Laboratory** | ↑ ASO/anti-DNase B titre, **↓ serum C3**, ↑ urea/creatinine | **Serum albumin <3 g/dL**, hyperlipidemia (cholesterol ↑, LDL/VLDL ↑), 24-h urine protein ≥3.5 g, oval fat bodies, lipiduria |

> 🎯 **Remember:** "AGN child = **bloody smoky urine + puffy eyes + high BP** · NS child = **frothy urine + puffy everywhere + normal BP + high cholesterol**."

## Q10. Mention the pathogenesis of acute glomerulonephritis / Post Streptococcal GN.

### Pathogenesis of post-streptococcal (post-infectious) GN

📌 **Trigger:** nephritogenic strains of **group A β-hemolytic streptococcus** (M protein types **12, 4, 1**) following pharyngitis (1–2 weeks latency) or impetigo/skin infection (3–6 weeks latency).

📌 **Principal antigen = streptococcal pyogenic exotoxin B (SpeB)** — deposited in glomeruli; **immune-complex mediated (type III hypersensitivity)** injury; complement activated → **low serum C3**; anti-streptococcal antibody titres (ASO, anti-DNase B) rise.

```text
Group A β-hemolytic streptococcus (nephritogenic strain M12/M4/M1) infection
(pharynx or skin) → latent period 1–4 weeks
        ↓
Streptococcal antigens (SpeB) plant in glomeruli (initially subendothelial)
        ↓
Host antibodies (IgG) bind antigen → immune complexes formed IN SITU
(circulating complexes also deposit)
        ↓
Complexes dissociate, traverse GBM, reform SUBEPITHELIALLY as discrete "HUMPS"
        ↓
Classical complement pathway activation → C3a/C5a → neutrophil + monocyte recruitment;
C5b-C9 (MAC) injures capillary wall
        ↓
Endothelial + mesangial proliferation → HYPERCELLULAR glomeruli;
GBM damage → hematuria, RBC casts, proteinuria; ↓ GFR → oliguria, azotemia
        ↓
Salt & water retention → periorbital edema + hypertension
```

📌 **Evidence for immune-complex mechanism:** granular IgG + C3 on IF; subepithelial electron-dense **"humps"** on EM; hypocomplementemia; latent period between infection and nephritis.

📌 Same lesion follows other infections — staphylococcal endocarditis, pneumococcal pneumonia, hepatitis B/C, varicella, malaria (infection-associated GN).

> 🎯 **Remember:** "**Strep sore → 2 weeks wait → humps appear, C3 drops down** — speckled (granular) glomeruli, cola-coloured urine."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q12. Give the morphology of AGN. Mention the fates and complications of AGN.

### Morphology of acute (post-streptococcal) GN

- **Gross:** kidneys symmetrically enlarged, pale cortex with **petechial haemorrhages** ("flea-bitten" appearance).
- **Light microscopy:** 🔴 **Diffuse, global enlargement & hypercellularity of all glomeruli** — proliferation of **endothelial + mesangial cells** with **neutrophils and monocytes** within capillary lumina; capillary spaces compressed; occasional **crescents** in severe cases. Tubules contain **red blood cell casts**; interstitial oedema.
- **Immunofluorescence:** **granular deposits of IgG + C3** in mesangium and along GBM ("starry-sky" pattern).
- **Electron microscopy:** 🔴 discrete, amorphous **electron-dense subepithelial deposits — "humps"** — the diagnostic hallmark.

### Fates of AGN

| Outcome | Frequency |
|---|---|
| **Complete recovery** (children) | **>95%** recover within weeks with supportive therapy |
| **Persistent/progressive disease** | Adults fare worse — only ~60% of sporadic adult cases recover promptly; some progress to chronic GN |
| **RPGN** | **<1%** (more in adults/severe crescentic cases) |
| **Death** | Rare today (uraemia, complications) |

### Complications of AGN

1. **Rapidly progressive (crescentic) GN** → acute renal failure
2. **Hypertensive emergencies** — hypertensive encephalopathy, intracranial haemorrhage
3. **Acute pulmonary oedema / heart failure** (fluid overload)
4. **Uremia** — severe oliguria/anuria
5. **Chronic glomerulonephritis** with progressive CKD (mainly adults with persistent heavy proteinuria)

> 🎯 **Remember:** Morphology = "**Big busy glomeruli stuffed with neutrophils; humps outside (EM), starry sky (IF)**"; Fate = "**95 kids cured, 1 goes crescent, adults linger.**"

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q13. Define Nephrotic Syndrome. List the Causes of Nephrotic Syndrome.

### Definition

🔴 **Nephrotic syndrome is a clinical syndrome caused by increased permeability of the glomerular capillary wall (podocyte/GBM injury), characterised by heavy proteinuria (≥3.5 g/day/1.73 m²), hypoalbuminemia (<3 g/dL), generalized oedema, hyperlipidemia and lipiduria.**

```text
Podocyte / slit-diaphragm (nephrin-podocin) or GBM injury
        ↓
Loss of size & charge barrier → MASSIVE PROTEINURIA ≥3.5 g/day
        ↓
Hypoalbuminemia (<3 g/dL) → ↓ plasma oncotic pressure
        ↓
Generalized oedema (+ Na/water retention via RAAS)
        ↓
Liver responds: ↑ lipoprotein synthesis (hyperlipidemia) → lipiduria (oval fat bodies);
loss of Ig → infections; loss of antithrombin III → thromboembolism
```

### Causes of nephrotic syndrome

**A. Primary (idiopathic) glomerular diseases:**

| Cause | Children (%) | Adults (%) |
|---|---|---|
| **Minimal change disease** 🔴 | **75** (most common) | 8 |
| **Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis** 🔴 | 10 | **35** (most common overall) |
| **Membranous nephropathy** 🔴 | 3 | **30** (most common in older adults) |
| **MPGN / dense deposit disease** | 10 | 10 |
| Other proliferative GN | 2 | 17 |

**B. Secondary (systemic) causes:**

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Metabolic** 🔴 | **Diabetes mellitus** (leading systemic cause) |
| **Immunologic** 🔴 | **SLE (membranous class V / proliferative)** |
| **Deposition** 🔴 | **Amyloidosis** (AL/AA), light-chain deposition disease |
| **Infections** | Hepatitis B & C, HIV, malaria, syphilis |
| **Drugs/toxins** | NSAIDs, penicillamine, gold, heroin, captopril |
| **Malignancy** | Hodgkin lymphoma (MCD), solid tumours (lung, colon — membranous) |
| **Vascular/allergic** | Renal vein thrombosis (usually consequence), bee-sting allergy |

📌 **Rule of thumb:** children → think MCD first (steroid-responsive); older adults → membranous; always rule out **diabetes, lupus, amyloid**.

> 🎯 **Remember:** Pentad = "**Proteinuria, Plasma-albumin-down, Puffiness, Plasma-lipids-up, Pee-fat (lipiduria)**"; causes by age = "**Children Choose MCD, Adults Adopt FSGS/Membranous.**"

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q14. Short Note: Minimal Change disease, RPGN (crescentic glomerulonephritis), IgA nephropathy, Lupus nephritis, Diabetic nephropathy. (separate mini-sections)

### (a) Minimal change disease (MCD — lipoid nephrosis)

🔴 **Definition:** the most frequent cause of nephrotic syndrome in **children (peak 2–6 yr)**, characterized by **normal glomeruli on light microscopy** and **diffuse effacement of podocyte foot processes visible only on electron microscopy**; tubules show lipid-laden cells (old name *lipoid nephrosis*).
- **Pathogenesis:** immune-mediated circulating factor(s) (associated with respiratory infection, immunization, atopy, **Hodgkin lymphoma**; NSAIDs) injure podocytes → **loss of glomerular polyanions (charge barrier)** → highly **selective albumin-selective proteinuria**.
- **Morphology:** LM — normal (only tubular lipid); IF — **negative**; EM — uniform **foot process effacement**, no deposits.
- **Clinical/lab:** nephrotic syndrome with preserved renal function, no hematuria/HTN; **>90% respond dramatically to corticosteroids**; relapses common but resolves at puberty; excellent prognosis.
> 🎯 "Normal light, sick electrons, happy steroids."

### (b) RPGN — rapidly progressive (crescentic) glomerulonephritis

🔴 **Definition:** a clinical syndrome (not a single disease) of rapid loss of renal function (over weeks–months) with severe oliguria and nephritic features; histological hallmark = **crescents** (proliferating parietal epithelial cells + macrophages filling Bowman space, driven by fibrin leakage through ruptured GBM) in most glomeruli.
- **Three immunological types:** **Type I anti-GBM** (linear IgG; Goodpasture with pulmonary haemorrhage) · **Type II immune complex** (granular; postinfectious, lupus, IgA, HSP) · **Type III pauci-immune** (**ANCA** — GPA/Wegener c-ANCA/PR3, microscopic polyangiitis p-ANCA/MPO; commonest).
- **Lab:** anti-GBM antibody, ANCA, ↓complement according to type; RBC casts.
- **Outcome:** untreated → death in weeks–months; early plasmapheresis (type I) / steroids + cyclophosphamide (type III).
> 🎯 "Crescents crush the tuft; Linear–Granular–Nothing tells you who attacked."

### (c) IgA nephropathy (Berger disease)

🔴 **Definition:** the **most common glomerulonephritis worldwide**, defined by dominant **mesangial IgA deposits** presenting as **recurrent synpharyngitic (within days of URI) macroscopic haematuria**, mostly in children/young adults.
- **Pathogenesis ("multi-hit"):** ↑ aberrantly glycosylated **polymeric IgA1** (defective galactosylation of hinge-region O-glycans) → anti-IgA1 IgG autoantibodies → mesangial immune complexes → **alternative complement pathway** activation → mesangial proliferation.
- **Morphology:** variable mesangial widening/focal proliferative GN; IF = **mesangial IgA + C3 (C1q/C4 absent)**; EM = mesangial dense deposits.
- **Course:** episodic haematuria recurs every few months; **15–40% progress to chronic renal failure over 20 years**; no curative therapy (ACEi/ARB for proteinuria).
> 🎯 "Sore throat today, bloody urine tomorrow — mesangial IgA is the culprit."

### (d) Lupus nephritis

🔴 **Definition:** renal involvement in **SLE** producing the widest variety of glomerular lesions by **immune-complex (full-house) deposition**; classified (ISN/RPS) as:
| Class | Lesion |
|---|---|
| **I** | Minimal mesangial |
| **II** | Mesangial proliferative |
| **III** | Focal proliferative |
| **IV** 🔴 | **Diffuse proliferative (worst prognosis — classic nephritic presentation)** |
| **V** | Membranous (nephrotic syndrome) |
| **VI** | Advanced sclerotic (end-stage) |

- **Morphology:** "**full-house**" granular deposits (**IgG + IgA + IgM + C3 + C1q**) in mesangium/subendothelial/subepithelial sites; wire-loop capillary thickening (massive subendothelial deposits); hematoxylin bodies; ± crescents.
- **Clinical:** hematuria, proteinuria (nephritic ± nephrotic), progressive renal failure in active classes III–V; monitored by urine protein & repeat biopsy; treated with steroids + mycophenolate/cyclophosphamide.
> 🎯 "Class IV = worst; full-house staining + wire loops = lupus."

### (e) Diabetic nephropathy

🔴 **Definition:** the **single leading cause of chronic kidney disease/ESRD worldwide**; develops in up to **40% of type 1 & type 2 diabetics** after 10–20 years of hyperglycemia.
- **Lesions:** **diffuse glomerulosclerosis** (uniform mesangial matrix expansion + GBM thickening), **nodular glomerulosclerosis — Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules** (PAS-positive laminated nodules), capsular drop & fibrin-cap lesions, **hyaline arteriolosclerosis (efferent > afferent arteriole — characteristic)**, "capsular drop" lesions; tubular atrophy + interstitial fibrosis.
- **Pathogenesis:** nonenzymatic glycation (AGEs), PKC & polyol pathway activation, TGF-β-driven mesangial matrix expansion, podocyte loss → proteinuria.
- **Clinical/lab:** microalbuminuria (earliest marker) → overt proteinuria (often nephrotic) → progressive CKD with hypertension; control of glycemia/BP (ACEi/ARB, SGLT2 inhibitors) slows progression.
> 🎯 "**KW nodules + thick GBM + efferent-first arteriolosclerosis = diabetic kidney** — start counting from microalbuminuria."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q15. Define AKI. List the Causes of AKI. Give the short pathogenesis of AKI in renal ischemia.

### Definition

🔴 **Acute kidney injury (AKI, formerly acute renal failure) is a sudden deterioration of renal function occurring over hours to days, with rapid rise of serum urea & creatinine (± reduction in urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hr), reversible if the cause is corrected in time.**

### Causes of AKI

| Category | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Pre-renal (≈60%)** — ↓ renal perfusion, parenchyma intact | Hypovolemia (haemorrhage, diarrhoea, burns), cardiac failure, sepsis/shock, cirrhosis, NSAIDs/ACE inhibitors, renal artery stenosis/embolism |
| **Renal (intrinsic)** — parenchymal damage | 🔴 **Acute tubular injury/necrosis (ATI — ~50% of hospital AKI)**: ischemic (shock, surgery) or nephrotoxic (aminoglycosides, radiocontrast, heavy metals, myoglobin/haemoglobin casts); acute glomerulonephritis; **acute pyelonephritis/acute interstitial nephritis** (drugs); vasculitis; HUS/TTP; malignant hypertension; myeloma cast nephropathy; uric acid nephropathy (tumour lysis) |
| **Post-renal** — obstruction | Calculi, BPH/prostate cancer, bladder/pelvic tumours, posterior urethral valves, strictures, neurogenic bladder, retroperitoneal fibrosis |

### Short pathogenesis of AKI in renal ischemia (ischemic ATI)

```text
Reduced renal perfusion (shock/hypotension)
        ↓
TUBULAR INJURY : proximal tubule most vulnerable (high O2 demand)
   • Ischemia → ATP depletion → cell swelling, loss of brush border & polarity
   • Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase redistributes to luminal membrane → impaired Na reabsorption
        ↓
Detached viable/necrotic cells + casts → LUMINAL OBSTRUCTION (↑ intratubular pressure → ↓GFR)
        ↓
BACKLEAK of glomerular filtrate through denuded tubular basement membrane → interstitial oedema
        ↓
DISTURBED HAEMODYNAMICS : intrarenal VASOCONSTRICTION
   (renin-angiotensin II + endothelin ↑ , nitric oxide & prostacyclin ↓)
   → outer-medullary hypoxia + tubuloglomerular feedback further lowers GFR
        ↓
OLIGURIA + AZOTEMIA  →  (repair: tubular regeneration by surviving cells → recovery phase with polyuria)
```

📌 **Phases:** initiation (~36 h) → maintenance (oliguria 40–400 mL/day, uremia, hyperkalemia, acidosis) → recovery (polyuria up to 3 L/day with **hypokalemia**). Most patients recover if the precipitating insult is survived.

> 🎯 **Remember:** "Ischemic ATN = **plug (casts) + leak (backleak) + squeeze (vasoconstriction)**."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q16. Define Oliguria, anuria, glycosuria, proteinuria and hematuria.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Oliguria** 🔴 | Urine output **<400 mL/day (<0.5 mL/kg/hr)** in adults — seen in dehydration, AKI, shock, obstruction |
| **Anuria** 🔴 | Urine output **<100 mL/day** — complete bilateral obstruction, acute cortical necrosis, severe AGN, shock |
| **Glycosuria** 🔴 | Presence of **glucose in urine** — appears when plasma glucose exceeds the renal threshold (~**180 mg/dL**), e.g., diabetes mellitus; or with normal blood sugar in **renal glycosuria** (defective proximal tubular reabsorption, SGLT2 defect) |
| **Proteinuria** 🔴 | Excretion of protein **>150 mg/day** in urine (persistent dipstick positivity) — indicates glomerular/tubular/overflow pathology |
| **Hematuria** 🔴 | Presence of **≥3 red blood cells per high-power field** of centrifuged urine sediment (or frank blood in urine) — glomerular (dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts) or urological origin |

> 🎯 **Remember:** "400 oligo, 100 anu, 180 threshold, 150 protein, 3 RBC/HPF = blood."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q17. Define CKD. Enumerate the cause of CKD. What are the stages of CKD?

### Definition

🔴 **Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is the presence of kidney damage (structural or functional abnormality — albuminuria, urinary sediment abnormality, imaging or histological change) and/or a GFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m² persisting for ≥3 months.** It is progressive and irreversible, ending in end-stage renal disease (ESRD).

### Causes of CKD

| Category | Causes |
|---|---|
| **Glomerular** 🔴 | **Diabetic nephropathy (most common)**, chronic glomerulonephritis (IgA, membranous, FSGS, MPGN), lupus nephritis |
| **Vascular** | Benign nephrosclerosis (hypertension), renal artery stenosis |
| **Tubulointerstitial** | Chronic pyelonephritis/reflux nephropathy, analgesic nephropathy, drug-induced interstitial nephritis, myeloma kidney, gouty nephropathy |
| **Obstructive** | Stones, BPH, tumours, neurogenic bladder |
| **Hereditary/cystic** | **ADPKD**, Alport syndrome, medullary cystic disease |
| **Miscellaneous** | Chronic AKI sequelae, tuberculosis of kidney, HIV nephropathy |

### Stages of CKD (KDIGO — based on GFR)

| Stage | GFR (mL/min/1.73 m²) | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **G1** | ≥90 (with kidney damage) | Normal or high GFR |
| **G2** | 60–89 | Mildly decreased |
| **G3a** | 45–59 | Mildly-to-moderately decreased |
| **G3b** | 30–44 | Moderately-to-severely decreased |
| **G4** | 15–29 | Severely decreased |
| **G5** | **<15** 🔴 | **Kidney failure (ESRD) — dialysis/transplant** |

📌 Once GFR falls to **30–50% of normal**, progression continues at a steady rate independent of the original disease — driven by **hyperfiltration → glomerulosclerosis + tubulointerstitial fibrosis**.

> 🎯 **Remember:** "CKD = damage or GFR <60 for **3 months**; stages 90-60-45-30-15 (**G1→G5**); Diabetes tops the list."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q18. What are the common causes of chronic glomerulonephritis. How diabetes causes chronic kidney disease?

### Common causes of chronic glomerulonephritis

1. **Progression of primary GN** — IgA nephropathy, membranous nephropathy, FSGS, MPGN (≈⅓–½ reach chronicity)
2. **Unresolved/recurrent acute post-streptococcal GN** (mainly adults)
3. **RPGN** healing with scarring (crescents → segmental sclerosis)
4. **Systemic diseases** — **diabetic nephropathy (leading cause)**, lupus nephritis, Henoch-Schönlein purpura, amyloidosis
5. **Hereditary** — Alport syndrome
6. Secondary FSGS — renal ablation/hyperfiltration, obesity, reflux nephropathy
7. Idiopathic — many cases of "chronic GN" begin insidiously with no antecedent history

### How diabetes causes chronic kidney disease

```text
Chronic hyperglycemia (poor glycemic control)
        ↓
Non-enzymatic glycation → ADVANCED GLYCATION END PRODUCTS (AGEs) + receptor (RAGE) activation
+ Polyol pathway flux + PKC activation + oxidative stress + TGF-β ↑
        ↓
GBM COLLAGEN IV THICKENING (diffuse) + MESANGIAL MATRIX EXPANSION
+ Podocyte injury/loss + hyaline arteriolosclerosis (efferent > afferent) → glomerular ischemia
        ↓
DIFFUSE & NODULAR (Kimmelstiel-Wilson) GLOMERULOSCLEROSIS
        ↓
MICROALBUMINURIA (earliest marker) → overt proteinuria → nephrotic syndrome + hypertension
        ↓
Progressive glomerulosclerosis + tubulointerstitial fibrosis → CHRONIC KIDNEY DISEASE → ESRD
```

> 🎯 **Remember:** "Sugar sticks (AGEs) → basement membrane fattens, mesangium nods (KW), efferent artery hardens → leak → scar → dialysis."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q19. What is proteinuria? Mention its cause.

### Definition

🔴 **Proteinuria is the excretion of protein in urine exceeding the normal limit of 150 mg/24 hours (adults)**, detected by persistent dipstick positivity, sulfosalicylic acid turbidity or quantitative 24-hour urinary total protein.

### Causes of proteinuria

| Type | Mechanism | Causes |
|---|---|---|
| **Glomerular** 🔴 | ↑ permeability of GBM/podocyte barrier | Nephrotic syndrome diseases — MCD, FSGS, membranous, MPGN; **diabetic nephropathy**, lupus nephritis, amyloidosis, AGN, IgA nephropathy, preeclampsia |
| **Tubular** 🔴 | Failure of proximal tubule to reabsorb low-molecular-weight proteins | **Acute tubular injury**, Fanconi syndrome, interstitial nephritis, heavy metals (cadmium/lead), myeloma kidney |
| **Overflow** | Overproduction of small proteins exceeding reabsorptive capacity | **Bence-Jones proteinuria (multiple myeloma)**, myoglobinuria (rhabdomyolysis), hemoglobinuria (intravascular hemolysis), lysozymuria (leukemia) |
| **Post-renal (secretory)** | Addition of protein distal to the kidney | UTI, cystitis, urothelial tumours, prostatic secretions, vaginal contamination |
| **Functional/benign (transient)** | Haemodynamic, reversible | Exercise, fever, cold, emotional stress, **orthostatic (postural) proteinuria**, congestive heart failure |

> 🎯 **Remember:** "Leaky filter (glomerular), tired recycler (tubular), flood from above (overflow), sprinkled downstream (post-renal), temporary surge (functional)."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q21. What do you mean by non selective, selective, highly selective, orthostatic proteinuria? Enumerate urinary findings of Nephrotic Syndrome.

### Selectivity of proteinuria

| Term | Meaning | Example/significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Selective proteinuria** | Loss restricted to proteins of **low molecular weight/size** (mainly albumin); high-molecular-weight proteins (IgG) retained | Indicates intact size-barrier with charge-barrier loss |
| **Highly selective proteinuria** 🔴 | Only **albumin (and transferrin)** excreted — selectivity index (clearance of IgG ÷ clearance of albumin) **<0.1** | 🔴 **Minimal change disease** — predicts good steroid response in children |
| **Non-selective proteinuria** 🔴 | All plasma proteins lost including **large molecules (IgG, α2-macroglobulin)** — selectivity index >0.2 | FSGS, membranous nephropathy, diabetic nephropathy — poorer steroid response |
| **Orthostatic (postural) proteinuria** | Proteinuria appearing **only on standing/upright posture**; disappears on recumbency (first morning sample negative); benign, common in thin adolescents; 24-hour protein usually <1 g/day | No renal disease — reassure |

### Urinary findings of Nephrotic Syndrome

1. 🔴 **Massive proteinuria ≥3.5 g/day/1.73 m²** (qualitative: +++/++++; frothy urine)
2. 🔴 **Lipiduria — oval fat bodies** (desquamated tubular cells laden with lipoprotein), **fatty casts**, free fat droplets showing **Maltese-cross birefringence** under polarized light
3. **Hyaline casts** (Tamm-Horsfall protein)
4. Granular casts (with hypoproteinaemic states/ATN)
5. **Absence of RBC casts & significant hematuria** (pure nephrotic state)
6. Low urine sodium with oedema; urine may be foamy due to surfactant effect of protein

> 🎯 **Remember:** "**Highly selective = Minimal Change (albumin only); non-selective = FSGS/Membranous (everything leaks); orthostatic = stand up, leak, lie down, stop.**"

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q22. What do you mean by massive proteinuria? Tell some cause of massive proteinuria.

### Definition

🔴 **Massive (nephrotic-range) proteinuria is the urinary protein excretion of ≥3.5 g/day per 1.73 m² body surface area (equivalent to ≥40 mg/m²/hr in children)** — sufficient to exceed hepatic albumin synthetic capacity and produce hypoalbuminemia and oedema.

### Causes of massive proteinuria

| Primary glomerular diseases | Secondary/systemic diseases |
|---|---|
| **Minimal change disease** | **Diabetic nephropathy** (most common systemic cause) |
| **Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis** | **SLE** (class V membranous / class IV proliferative) |
| **Membranous nephropathy** | **Amyloidosis (AL/AA)** |
| **MPGN / dense deposit disease** | Preeclampsia/eclampsia |
| Mesangiocapillary/other proliferative GN | Renal vein thrombosis; infections (HBV, HCV, HIV, malaria, syphilis); drugs (gold, penicillamine, NSAIDs, heroin); malignancy-associated (Hodgkin, solid tumours) |

> 🎯 **Remember:** "≥3.5 g/day = door to nephrotic syndrome — think **MCD-FSGS-Membranous + Diabetes-Lupus-Amyloid**."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

## Q23. A 4 year old boy presents with severe proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, generalized edema & Hyperlipidemia. The patient improves on an empiric trial of corticosteroids with complete resolution of proteinuria. Which is most likely Diagnosis? Mention the diagnostic modalities of primary glomerular disease.

### Most likely diagnosis

🔴 **Minimal change disease (MCD — "steroid-responsive nephrotic syndrome of childhood").**

**Supporting points:**
- Age 4 years = peak age group (2–6 yr) — **75% of childhood nephrotic syndrome is MCD**.
- Full nephrotic tetrad: massive proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia (<3 g/dL), generalized pitting oedema, hyperlipidemia.
- **Complete resolution of proteinuria on corticosteroids (>90% of children respond dramatically)** — the therapeutic signature of MCD.
- Expected biopsy (if done): normal light microscopy, negative IF, diffuse **foot process effacement on EM**.

### Diagnostic modalities of primary glomerular disease

| Modality | Findings/Role |
|---|---|
| **1. Urinalysis (routine + microscopy)** 🔴 | Proteinuria (quantify: dipstick, sulfosalicylic acid, **24-hour urinary total protein**), hematuria/dysmorphic RBCs, **casts** (RBC casts = nephritic; fatty casts/oval fat bodies = nephrotic), lipiduria |
| **2. Blood investigations** 🔴 | Serum albumin, total protein, cholesterol/triglycerides (nephrotic profile); **urea & creatinine/eGFR**; **serum C3/C4** (low in post-strep GN, MPGN, lupus); ASO/anti-DNase B titre; ANA/anti-dsDNA (lupus), ANCA (pauci-immune), anti-GBM antibody; hepatitis/HIV serology; immunoglobulins |
| **3. Renal biopsy + histopathology** 🔴 — the definitive modality | **Light microscopy** (architecture, hypercellularity, sclerosis, crescents), **immunofluorescence microscopy** (granular/linear/pauci-immune pattern; IgA/IgG/complement identification), **electron microscopy** (foot process effacement, subepithelial humps, subendothelial deposits, tram-tracks) |
| **4. Imaging & adjuncts** | Ultrasonography (kidney size, echogenicity, cysts, obstruction); selective proteinuria index; genetic testing (Alport, steroid-resistant NS genes) in selected cases |

📌 **Indication rule:** typical childhood steroid-responsive nephrotic syndrome (as here) is treated **without biopsy**; biopsy reserved for atypical age (>8 yr), hematuria/HTN/renal failure, non-response to steroids, or relapse.

> 🎯 **Remember:** "Child + full nephrotic + steroids cure = **MCD**; diagnose glomerular disease with **Urine → Blood → Biopsy (LM + IF + EM)**."

> 📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249
