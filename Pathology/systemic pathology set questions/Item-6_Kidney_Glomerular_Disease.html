Why anaemia occurs in CKD

  1. ↓ Erythropoietin (EPO) production 🔴 — damaged peritubular interstitial cells fail to secrete EPO → hypoproliferative normocytic normochromic anaemia (the principal mechanism).
  2. Uremic toxins suppress the bone marrow (direct myelosuppression).
  3. Shortened RBC lifespan — circulating red cells damaged by the uremic milieu.
  4. Iron & folate deficiency — dietary restriction, malabsorption, chronic blood loss (platelet dysfunction → GI bleeding, haemodialysis circuit losses).
  5. Associated inflammation (↑ hepcidin) → functional iron blockade.
🎯 Remember: "CKD anaemia = no EPO + poisoned marrow + dying RBCs + lost iron — treat with recombinant EPO + iron."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249
Q4. What is azotemia and uremia?

Definitions

🔴 Azotemia = elevated blood levels of urea (and other nitrogenous waste products such as creatinine) resulting from a reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR). It is a biochemical abnormality.

🔴 Uremia = the clinical syndrome in which azotemia progresses to symptomatic systemic illness — it denotes the biochemical plus clinical signs and symptoms of advanced/established renal failure affecting virtually every organ system.

Types/causes of azotemia

TypeSite of problemCommon causes
Pre-renalBefore the kidney (↓ perfusion)Hypovolemia (haemorrhage, dehydration, burns), cardiac failure, shock, cirrhosis, renal artery stenosis
Renal (intrinsic)Within the kidneyAcute tubular injury (#1), glomerulonephritis, acute interstitial nephritis, vasculitis, HUS/TTP, chronic kidney disease
Post-renalBelow the kidney (obstruction)Calculi, BPH, tumours (bladder/cervix/prostate), posterior urethral valves, retroperitoneal fibrosis, neurogenic bladder

Clinical manifestations of uremia

  • Pericarditis (fibrinous), pleuritis · Encephalopathy (confusion → asterixis → coma) · neuropathy
  • Gastroenteritis/gastritis, nausea, anorexia, halitosis (ammoniacal odour)
  • Bleeding tendency (platelet dysfunction), anaemia, pruritus, skin pallor/"uremic frost"
  • Metabolic: hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, hypocalcemia, hyperphosphatemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism
🎯 Remember: "Azotemia = waste in blood; Uremia = azotemia + whole-body sickness. Pre-Renal → fix the plumbing pressure; Renal → parenchyma dead; Post-renal → pipe blocked."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249

Role of immunofluorescence (IF) microscopy in diagnosis

📌 IF is performed on frozen renal biopsy tissue using fluorescein-tagged antibodies against human IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q, fibrin — it localizes and characterizes immune deposits, which is often the decisive diagnostic step.

IF findingDiagnosis suggested
Granular IgG + C3 along GBM (subepithelial)Membranous nephropathy (anti-PLA2R positivity), post-streptococcal GN ("starry sky")
Granular IgA + C3 confined to mesangium (C1q/C4 absent)IgA nephropathy / Henoch-Schönlein purpura
Linear IgG (+C3) along GBMAnti-GBM disease / Goodpasture syndrome
No deposits (pauci-immune)ANCA-associated crescentic GN, minimal change disease (IF negative)
Granular "full house" (IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, C1q)Lupus nephritis
IgM + C3 in sclerotic segments onlyFSGS
Granular IgG + C3 + C1q subendothelial, tram-trackMPGN type I
C3 only (no immunoglobulin/C1q)Dense deposit disease (C3 glomerulopathy)

Roles summarised: (1) confirms presence/absence of immune deposits; (2) identifies pattern (granular vs linear vs none) → separates immune-complex, anti-GBM and pauci-immune disease; (3) identifies the immunoglobulin class (IgA vs IgG) and complement components; (4) subclasses disease (e.g., IgG4 in primary membranous); (5) guides classification of lupus nephritis classes I–VI.

Q10. Mention the pathogenesis of acute glomerulonephritis / Post Streptococcal GN.

Pathogenesis of post-streptococcal (post-infectious) GN

📌 Trigger: nephritogenic strains of group A β-hemolytic streptococcus (M protein types 12, 4, 1) following pharyngitis (1–2 weeks latency) or impetigo/skin infection (3–6 weeks latency).

📌 Principal antigen = streptococcal pyogenic exotoxin B (SpeB) — deposited in glomeruli; immune-complex mediated (type III hypersensitivity) injury; complement activated → low serum C3; anti-streptococcal antibody titres (ASO, anti-DNase B) rise.

📌 Same lesion follows other infections — staphylococcal endocarditis, pneumococcal pneumonia, hepatitis B/C, varicella, malaria (infection-associated GN).

Q12. Give the morphology of AGN. Mention the fates and complications of AGN.

Morphology of acute (post-streptococcal) GN

  • Gross: kidneys symmetrically enlarged, pale cortex with petechial haemorrhages ("flea-bitten" appearance).
  • Light microscopy: 🔴 Diffuse, global enlargement & hypercellularity of all glomeruli — proliferation of endothelial + mesangial cells with neutrophils and monocytes within capillary lumina; capillary spaces compressed; occasional crescents in severe cases. Tubules contain red blood cell casts; interstitial oedema.
  • Immunofluorescence: granular deposits of IgG + C3 in mesangium and along GBM ("starry-sky" pattern).
  • Electron microscopy: 🔴 discrete, amorphous electron-dense subepithelial deposits — "humps" — the diagnostic hallmark.

Fates of AGN

OutcomeFrequency
Complete recovery (children)>95% recover within weeks with supportive therapy
Persistent/progressive diseaseAdults fare worse — only ~60% of sporadic adult cases recover promptly; some progress to chronic GN
RPGN<1% (more in adults/severe crescentic cases)
DeathRare today (uraemia, complications)

Complications of AGN

  1. Rapidly progressive (crescentic) GN → acute renal failure
  2. Hypertensive emergencies — hypertensive encephalopathy, intracranial haemorrhage
  3. Acute pulmonary oedema / heart failure (fluid overload)
  4. Uremia — severe oliguria/anuria
  5. Chronic glomerulonephritis with progressive CKD (mainly adults with persistent heavy proteinuria)

Q14. Short Note: Minimal Change disease, RPGN (crescentic glomerulonephritis), IgA nephropathy, Lupus nephritis, Diabetic nephropathy. (separate mini-sections)

(a) Minimal change disease (MCD — lipoid nephrosis)

🔴 Definition: the most frequent cause of nephrotic syndrome in children (peak 2–6 yr), characterized by normal glomeruli on light microscopy and diffuse effacement of podocyte foot processes visible only on electron microscopy; tubules show lipid-laden cells (old name lipoid nephrosis).

  • Pathogenesis: immune-mediated circulating factor(s) (associated with respiratory infection, immunization, atopy, Hodgkin lymphoma; NSAIDs) injure podocytes → loss of glomerular polyanions (charge barrier) → highly selective albumin-selective proteinuria.
  • Morphology: LM — normal (only tubular lipid); IF — negative; EM — uniform foot process effacement, no deposits.
  • Clinical/lab: nephrotic syndrome with preserved renal function, no hematuria/HTN; >90% respond dramatically to corticosteroids; relapses common but resolves at puberty; excellent prognosis.
🎯 "Normal light, sick electrons, happy steroids."

(b) RPGN — rapidly progressive (crescentic) glomerulonephritis

🔴 Definition: a clinical syndrome (not a single disease) of rapid loss of renal function (over weeks–months) with severe oliguria and nephritic features; histological hallmark = crescents (proliferating parietal epithelial cells + macrophages filling Bowman space, driven by fibrin leakage through ruptured GBM) in most glomeruli.

  • Three immunological types: Type I anti-GBM (linear IgG; Goodpasture with pulmonary haemorrhage) · Type II immune complex (granular; postinfectious, lupus, IgA, HSP) · Type III pauci-immune (ANCA — GPA/Wegener c-ANCA/PR3, microscopic polyangiitis p-ANCA/MPO; commonest).
  • Lab: anti-GBM antibody, ANCA, ↓complement according to type; RBC casts.
  • Outcome: untreated → death in weeks–months; early plasmapheresis (type I) / steroids + cyclophosphamide (type III).
🎯 "Crescents crush the tuft; Linear–Granular–Nothing tells you who attacked."

(c) IgA nephropathy (Berger disease)

🔴 Definition: the most common glomerulonephritis worldwide, defined by dominant mesangial IgA deposits presenting as recurrent synpharyngitic (within days of URI) macroscopic haematuria, mostly in children/young adults.

  • Pathogenesis ("multi-hit"): ↑ aberrantly glycosylated polymeric IgA1 (defective galactosylation of hinge-region O-glycans) → anti-IgA1 IgG autoantibodies → mesangial immune complexes → alternative complement pathway activation → mesangial proliferation.
  • Morphology: variable mesangial widening/focal proliferative GN; IF = mesangial IgA + C3 (C1q/C4 absent); EM = mesangial dense deposits.
  • Course: episodic haematuria recurs every few months; 15–40% progress to chronic renal failure over 20 years; no curative therapy (ACEi/ARB for proteinuria).
🎯 "Sore throat today, bloody urine tomorrow — mesangial IgA is the culprit."

(d) Lupus nephritis

🔴 Definition: renal involvement in SLE producing the widest variety of glomerular lesions by immune-complex (full-house) deposition; classified (ISN/RPS) as:

ClassLesion
IMinimal mesangial
IIMesangial proliferative
IIIFocal proliferative
IV 🔴Diffuse proliferative (worst prognosis — classic nephritic presentation)
VMembranous (nephrotic syndrome)
VIAdvanced sclerotic (end-stage)
  • Morphology: "full-house" granular deposits (IgG + IgA + IgM + C3 + C1q) in mesangium/subendothelial/subepithelial sites; wire-loop capillary thickening (massive subendothelial deposits); hematoxylin bodies; ± crescents.
  • Clinical: hematuria, proteinuria (nephritic ± nephrotic), progressive renal failure in active classes III–V; monitored by urine protein & repeat biopsy; treated with steroids + mycophenolate/cyclophosphamide.
🎯 "Class IV = worst; full-house staining + wire loops = lupus."

(e) Diabetic nephropathy

🔴 Definition: the single leading cause of chronic kidney disease/ESRD worldwide; develops in up to 40% of type 1 & type 2 diabetics after 10–20 years of hyperglycemia.

  • Lesions: diffuse glomerulosclerosis (uniform mesangial matrix expansion + GBM thickening), nodular glomerulosclerosis — Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules (PAS-positive laminated nodules), capsular drop & fibrin-cap lesions, hyaline arteriolosclerosis (efferent > afferent arteriole — characteristic), "capsular drop" lesions; tubular atrophy + interstitial fibrosis.
  • Pathogenesis: nonenzymatic glycation (AGEs), PKC & polyol pathway activation, TGF-β-driven mesangial matrix expansion, podocyte loss → proteinuria.
  • Clinical/lab: microalbuminuria (earliest marker) → overt proteinuria (often nephrotic) → progressive CKD with hypertension; control of glycemia/BP (ACEi/ARB, SGLT2 inhibitors) slows progression.
🎯 "KW nodules + thick GBM + efferent-first arteriolosclerosis = diabetic kidney — start counting from microalbuminuria."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249
Q15. Define AKI. List the Causes of AKI. Give the short pathogenesis of AKI in renal ischemia.

Definition

🔴 Acute kidney injury (AKI, formerly acute renal failure) is a sudden deterioration of renal function occurring over hours to days, with rapid rise of serum urea & creatinine (± reduction in urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hr), reversible if the cause is corrected in time.

Causes of AKI

CategoryCauses
Pre-renal (≈60%) — ↓ renal perfusion, parenchyma intactHypovolemia (haemorrhage, diarrhoea, burns), cardiac failure, sepsis/shock, cirrhosis, NSAIDs/ACE inhibitors, renal artery stenosis/embolism
Renal (intrinsic) — parenchymal damage🔴 Acute tubular injury/necrosis (ATI — ~50% of hospital AKI): ischemic (shock, surgery) or nephrotoxic (aminoglycosides, radiocontrast, heavy metals, myoglobin/haemoglobin casts); acute glomerulonephritis; acute pyelonephritis/acute interstitial nephritis (drugs); vasculitis; HUS/TTP; malignant hypertension; myeloma cast nephropathy; uric acid nephropathy (tumour lysis)
Post-renal — obstructionCalculi, BPH/prostate cancer, bladder/pelvic tumours, posterior urethral valves, strictures, neurogenic bladder, retroperitoneal fibrosis

Short pathogenesis of AKI in renal ischemia (ischemic ATI)

Reduced renal perfusion (shock/hypotension)
        ↓
TUBULAR INJURY : proximal tubule most vulnerable (high O2 demand)
   • Ischemia → ATP depletion → cell swelling, loss of brush border & polarity
   • Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase redistributes to luminal membrane → impaired Na reabsorption
        ↓
Detached viable/necrotic cells + casts → LUMINAL OBSTRUCTION (↑ intratubular pressure → ↓GFR)
        ↓
BACKLEAK of glomerular filtrate through denuded tubular basement membrane → interstitial oedema
        ↓
DISTURBED HAEMODYNAMICS : intrarenal VASOCONSTRICTION
   (renin-angiotensin II + endothelin ↑ , nitric oxide & prostacyclin ↓)
   → outer-medullary hypoxia + tubuloglomerular feedback further lowers GFR
        ↓
OLIGURIA + AZOTEMIA  →  (repair: tubular regeneration by surviving cells → recovery phase with polyuria)

📌 Phases: initiation (~36 h) → maintenance (oliguria 40–400 mL/day, uremia, hyperkalemia, acidosis) → recovery (polyuria up to 3 L/day with hypokalemia). Most patients recover if the precipitating insult is survived.

🎯 Remember: "Ischemic ATN = plug (casts) + leak (backleak) + squeeze (vasoconstriction)."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249
Q16. Define Oliguria, anuria, glycosuria, proteinuria and hematuria.
TermDefinition
Oliguria 🔴Urine output <400 mL/day (<0.5 mL/kg/hr) in adults — seen in dehydration, AKI, shock, obstruction
Anuria 🔴Urine output <100 mL/day — complete bilateral obstruction, acute cortical necrosis, severe AGN, shock
Glycosuria 🔴Presence of glucose in urine — appears when plasma glucose exceeds the renal threshold (~180 mg/dL), e.g., diabetes mellitus; or with normal blood sugar in renal glycosuria (defective proximal tubular reabsorption, SGLT2 defect)
Proteinuria 🔴Excretion of protein >150 mg/day in urine (persistent dipstick positivity) — indicates glomerular/tubular/overflow pathology
Hematuria 🔴Presence of ≥3 red blood cells per high-power field of centrifuged urine sediment (or frank blood in urine) — glomerular (dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts) or urological origin
🎯 Remember: "400 oligo, 100 anu, 180 threshold, 150 protein, 3 RBC/HPF = blood."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249
Q17. Define CKD. Enumerate the cause of CKD. What are the stages of CKD?

Definition

🔴 Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is the presence of kidney damage (structural or functional abnormality — albuminuria, urinary sediment abnormality, imaging or histological change) and/or a GFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m² persisting for ≥3 months. It is progressive and irreversible, ending in end-stage renal disease (ESRD).

Causes of CKD

CategoryCauses
Glomerular 🔴Diabetic nephropathy (most common), chronic glomerulonephritis (IgA, membranous, FSGS, MPGN), lupus nephritis
VascularBenign nephrosclerosis (hypertension), renal artery stenosis
TubulointerstitialChronic pyelonephritis/reflux nephropathy, analgesic nephropathy, drug-induced interstitial nephritis, myeloma kidney, gouty nephropathy
ObstructiveStones, BPH, tumours, neurogenic bladder
Hereditary/cysticADPKD, Alport syndrome, medullary cystic disease
MiscellaneousChronic AKI sequelae, tuberculosis of kidney, HIV nephropathy

Stages of CKD (KDIGO — based on GFR)

StageGFR (mL/min/1.73 m²)Description
G1≥90 (with kidney damage)Normal or high GFR
G260–89Mildly decreased
G3a45–59Mildly-to-moderately decreased
G3b30–44Moderately-to-severely decreased
G415–29Severely decreased
G5<15 🔴Kidney failure (ESRD) — dialysis/transplant

📌 Once GFR falls to 30–50% of normal, progression continues at a steady rate independent of the original disease — driven by hyperfiltration → glomerulosclerosis + tubulointerstitial fibrosis.

🎯 Remember: "CKD = damage or GFR <60 for 3 months; stages 90-60-45-30-15 (G1→G5); Diabetes tops the list."

Q18. What are the common causes of chronic glomerulonephritis. How diabetes causes chronic kidney disease?

Common causes of chronic glomerulonephritis

  1. Progression of primary GN — IgA nephropathy, membranous nephropathy, FSGS, MPGN (≈⅓–½ reach chronicity)
  2. Unresolved/recurrent acute post-streptococcal GN (mainly adults)
  3. RPGN healing with scarring (crescents → segmental sclerosis)
  4. Systemic diseases — diabetic nephropathy (leading cause), lupus nephritis, Henoch-Schönlein purpura, amyloidosis
  5. Hereditary — Alport syndrome
  6. Secondary FSGS — renal ablation/hyperfiltration, obesity, reflux nephropathy
  7. Idiopathic — many cases of "chronic GN" begin insidiously with no antecedent history

How diabetes causes chronic kidney disease

Chronic hyperglycemia (poor glycemic control)
        ↓
Non-enzymatic glycation → ADVANCED GLYCATION END PRODUCTS (AGEs) + receptor (RAGE) activation
+ Polyol pathway flux + PKC activation + oxidative stress + TGF-β ↑
        ↓
GBM COLLAGEN IV THICKENING (diffuse) + MESANGIAL MATRIX EXPANSION
+ Podocyte injury/loss + hyaline arteriolosclerosis (efferent > afferent) → glomerular ischemia
        ↓
DIFFUSE & NODULAR (Kimmelstiel-Wilson) GLOMERULOSCLEROSIS
        ↓
MICROALBUMINURIA (earliest marker) → overt proteinuria → nephrotic syndrome + hypertension
        ↓
Progressive glomerulosclerosis + tubulointerstitial fibrosis → CHRONIC KIDNEY DISEASE → ESRD
🎯 Remember: "Sugar sticks (AGEs) → basement membrane fattens, mesangium nods (KW), efferent artery hardens → leak → scar → dialysis."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249
Q19. What is proteinuria? Mention its cause.

Definition

🔴 Proteinuria is the excretion of protein in urine exceeding the normal limit of 150 mg/24 hours (adults), detected by persistent dipstick positivity, sulfosalicylic acid turbidity or quantitative 24-hour urinary total protein.

Causes of proteinuria

TypeMechanismCauses
Glomerular 🔴↑ permeability of GBM/podocyte barrierNephrotic syndrome diseases — MCD, FSGS, membranous, MPGN; diabetic nephropathy, lupus nephritis, amyloidosis, AGN, IgA nephropathy, preeclampsia
Tubular 🔴Failure of proximal tubule to reabsorb low-molecular-weight proteinsAcute tubular injury, Fanconi syndrome, interstitial nephritis, heavy metals (cadmium/lead), myeloma kidney

Post-renal (secretory)Addition of protein distal to the kidneyUTI, cystitis, urothelial tumours, prostatic secretions, vaginal contamination
Functional/benign (transient)Haemodynamic, reversibleExercise, fever, cold, emotional stress, orthostatic (postural) proteinuria, congestive heart failure

Q21. What do you mean by non selective, selective, highly selective, orthostatic proteinuria? Enumerate urinary findings of Nephrotic Syndrome.

Selectivity of proteinuria

TermMeaningExample/significance
Selective proteinuriaLoss restricted to proteins of low molecular weight/size (mainly albumin); high-molecular-weight proteins (IgG) retainedIndicates intact size-barrier with charge-barrier loss
Highly selective proteinuria 🔴Only albumin (and transferrin) excreted — selectivity index (clearance of IgG ÷ clearance of albumin) <0.1🔴 Minimal change disease — predicts good steroid response in children
Non-selective proteinuria 🔴All plasma proteins lost including large molecules (IgG, α2-macroglobulin) — selectivity index >0.2FSGS, membranous nephropathy, diabetic nephropathy — poorer steroid response
Orthostatic (postural) proteinuriaProteinuria appearing only on standing/upright posture; disappears on recumbency (first morning sample negative); benign, common in thin adolescents; 24-hour protein usually <1 g/dayNo renal disease — reassure

Urinary findings of Nephrotic Syndrome

  1. 🔴 Massive proteinuria ≥3.5 g/day/1.73 m² (qualitative: +++/++++; frothy urine)
  2. 🔴 Lipiduria — oval fat bodies (desquamated tubular cells laden with lipoprotein), fatty casts, free fat droplets showing Maltese-cross birefringence under polarized light
  3. Hyaline casts (Tamm-Horsfall protein)
  4. Granular casts (with hypoproteinaemic states/ATN)
  5. Absence of RBC casts & significant hematuria (pure nephrotic state)
  6. Low urine sodium with oedema; urine may be foamy due to surfactant effect of protein
🎯 Remember: "Highly selective = Minimal Change (albumin only); non-selective = FSGS/Membranous (everything leaks); orthostatic = stand up, leak, lie down, stop."

Q22. What do you mean by massive proteinuria? Tell some cause of massive proteinuria.

Definition

🔴 Massive (nephrotic-range) proteinuria is the urinary protein excretion of ≥3.5 g/day per 1.73 m² body surface area (equivalent to ≥40 mg/m²/hr in children) — sufficient to exceed hepatic albumin synthetic capacity and produce hypoalbuminemia and oedema.

Q23. A 4 year old boy presents with severe proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, generalized edema & Hyperlipidemia. The patient improves on an empiric trial of corticosteroids with complete resolution of proteinuria. Which is most likely Diagnosis? Mention the diagnostic modalities of primary glomerular disease.

Most likely diagnosis

🔴 Minimal change disease (MCD — "steroid-responsive nephrotic syndrome of childhood").

Supporting points:

  • Age 4 years = peak age group (2–6 yr) — 75% of childhood nephrotic syndrome is MCD.
  • Full nephrotic tetrad: massive proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia (<3 g/dL), generalized pitting oedema, hyperlipidemia.
  • Complete resolution of proteinuria on corticosteroids (>90% of children respond dramatically) — the therapeutic signature of MCD.
  • Expected biopsy (if done): normal light microscopy, negative IF, diffuse foot process effacement on EM.

Diagnostic modalities of primary glomerular disease

ModalityFindings/Role
1. Urinalysis (routine + microscopy) 🔴Proteinuria (quantify: dipstick, sulfosalicylic acid, 24-hour urinary total protein), hematuria/dysmorphic RBCs, casts (RBC casts = nephritic; fatty casts/oval fat bodies = nephrotic), lipiduria
2. Blood investigations 🔴Serum albumin, total protein, cholesterol/triglycerides (nephrotic profile); urea & creatinine/eGFR; serum C3/C4 (low in post-strep GN, MPGN, lupus); ASO/anti-DNase B titre; ANA/anti-dsDNA (lupus), ANCA (pauci-immune), anti-GBM antibody; hepatitis/HIV serology; immunoglobulins
3. Renal biopsy + histopathology 🔴 — the definitive modalityLight microscopy (architecture, hypercellularity, sclerosis, crescents), immunofluorescence microscopy (granular/linear/pauci-immune pattern; IgA/IgG/complement identification), electron microscopy (foot process effacement, subepithelial humps, subendothelial deposits, tram-tracks)
4. Imaging & adjunctsUltrasonography (kidney size, echogenicity, cysts, obstruction); selective proteinuria index; genetic testing (Alport, steroid-resistant NS genes) in selected cases

📌 Indication rule: typical childhood steroid-responsive nephrotic syndrome (as here) is treated without biopsy; biopsy reserved for atypical age (>8 yr), hematuria/HTN/renal failure, non-response to steroids, or relapse.

🎯 Remember: "Child + full nephrotic + steroids cure = MCD; diagnose glomerular disease with Urine → Blood → Biopsy (LM + IF + EM)."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 20 (Kidney), pp. 895–952 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Renal System, pp. 225–249
