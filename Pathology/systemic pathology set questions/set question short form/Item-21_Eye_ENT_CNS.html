Q1a. Definition

🔴 Retinoblastoma = the most common primary intraocular malignancy of CHILDREN (~3% of childhood cancers; ~1 in 15,000–20,000 live births). Cell of origin = a neuronal progenitor cell (not a bipotential "retinoblast"). ~40% are heritable (germline RB1 mutation); peak age <3 yr.

Q1b. Pathogenesis — Knudson TWO-HIT hypothesis

🔴 RB1 tumor-suppressor gene (chromosome 13q14) — the first discovered tumor suppressor gene; both alleles must be inactivated.

FeatureHeritable (germline)Non-heritable (sporadic)
First hitGermline RB1 mutation (inherited or de novo)Somatic mutation in one retinal cell
Second hitSomatic loss of the remaining allele (LOH) in a retinal cellSecond somatic hit in the same cell
Laterality / multiplicityOften bilateral + multifocalUsually unilateral, unifocal
Age at onsetEarlier (<1–2 yr)Later (2–4 yr)
🔴 Trilateral retinoblastomaBilateral RB + pinealoblastoma (dismal)Absent
🔴 Second primary cancersOsteosarcoma, soft-tissue sarcomas, melanoma (radiation-induced in field)Not increased
Q1c. Morphology
  • Gross: white mass filling the globe; endophytic (into vitreous) or exophytic (subretinal) growth; 🔴 focal dystrophic calcification is characteristic (radio-opaque on CT/ultrasound).
  • Microscopy: small round blue cells, hyperchromatic nuclei, scant cytoplasm; viable cells encircle blood vessels with sleeves, beyond which lie zones of necrosis;
  • 🔴 Flexner–Wintersteiner rosettes = tumor cells arranged around a central lumen (photoreceptor differentiation) — characteristic;
  • Fleurettes = better-differentiated photoreceptor clusters;
  • Homer Wright rosettes may occur (less specific).
Q1d. Clinical features & spread
  • 🔴 Leukocoria ("cat's eye" white pupillary reflex) — most common presentation; strabismus second; advanced → glaucoma, proptosis.
  • Spread: optic nerve → brain (subarachnoid space); choroid → hematogenous to bone/bone marrow (lungs rare). 🔴 Optic-nerve invasion beyond lamina cribrosa = worst prognostic sign; extraocular extension poor prognosis.
  • Treatment: enucleation for large tumors; chemoreduction + laser/cryotherapy preserves vision in smaller tumors. Survival >90% with modern therapy.
🎯 "Child white pupil (leukocoria) = retinoblastoma → RB1 two-hit (13q14) · Flexner-Wintersteiner rosettes + dystrophic calcification · bilateral/pineal = germline · optic nerve invasion = bad."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 29 (The Eye), pp. 1305–1323 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I The Eye, pp. 373–375

# Part B — ENT: Sinusitis & Otitis Media (Q2–6)

Q2a. Definition

🔴 Acute sinusitis = acute inflammation (<4 weeks) of the mucosa of paranasal sinuses (maxillary most commonly involved), almost always following viral upper-respiratory infection or allergic rhinitis.

Q2b. Pathogenesis (arrow-chain)

(ostia are narrow — esp. maxillary & anterior ethmoid)

Q2c. Predisposing factors
  • Viral URIs, allergic rhinitis; nasal polyps, deviated nasal septum, turbinate hypertrophy.
  • Dental root infection (maxillary odontogenic sinusitis).
  • Impaired ciliary clearance — 🔴 primary ciliary dyskinesia/Kartagener syndrome (sinusitis + bronchiectasis + situs inversus), cystic fibrosis.
  • Swimming/diving, barotrauma; immunodeficiency; nasogastric tubes.
Q2d. Complications (must mention)
  • Orbital cellulitis/abscess (via thin lamina papyracea — ethmoid).
  • Osteomyelitis of frontal bone ("Pott's puffy tumor").
  • 🔴 Septic thrombophlebitis of cavernous/dural venous sinuses, meningitis, brain abscess.
  • ⚠️ In diabetics: rhinocerebral mucormycosis — angioinvasive fungal infection, emergency.
🎯 "Viral URI → swollen ostium blocked → cilia paralysed + stagnant mucus → pneumococcus/H. flu/Moraxella move in → pus; diabetics beware Mucor; watch out for orbit and cavernous sinus."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 16 (Head and Neck), pp. 731–751 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Head, Neck and Oral Cavity, pp. 136–143
Q3a. Definition

🔴 Chronic sinusitis = inflammation of paranasal sinus mucosa persisting >12 weeks (some classifications ≥8–12 weeks), with irreversible mucosal changes.

Q3b. Pathogenesis (arrow-chain)

Q3c. Key points
  • Polymicrobial — aerobic + anaerobes (Peptostreptococcus, Bacteroides); fungi may colonize (fungal ball/mycetoma; invasive disease only if immunosuppressed).
  • Biofilms explain chronicity and failure of standard antibiotics.
  • Component of 🔴 Kartagener syndrome (ciliary defect); cystic fibrosis (viscid secretions).
  • Complications: mucocele formation, bone erosion, orbital/intracranial extension (less common than acute).
  • Management logic: treat cause (surgery FESS to restore ostial drainage) + long antibiotics/intranasal steroids.
🎯 "Acute repeats itself until it becomes permanent: blocked ostium + broken cilia → thickened polypoid fibrotic mucosa + biofilm polymicrobial anaerobes (+ fungus in atopics) → drain it surgically."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 16 (Head and Neck), pp. 731–751 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Head, Neck and Oral Cavity, pp. 136–143
Q4a. Definition

🔴 Otitis media = inflammation of the middle ear cleft (tympanic cavity, mastoid air cells, eustachian tube), predominantly an infants' and children's disease because their short, horizontal eustachian tube permits reflux of nasopharyngeal secretions.

Q6a. Definition

🔴 CSOM = persistent (>2 weeks) purulent discharge from the middle ear through a non-intact (perforated) tympanic membrane, with chronic inflammation of mucosa and/or bone; sequel of inadequately treated acute otitis media, usually beginning in childhood.

Q6b. Two clinicopathological types
Feature**Tubotympanic — SAFE type****Atticoantral — UNSAFE type**
PerforationCentral, pars tensaAttic (marginal), pars flaccida/tensa edge
PathologyMucosa only — congestion, granulations, polypBone-invading: cholesteatoma + granulation tissue
DischargeMucopurulent, intermittent, odorlessPurulent, foul-smelling (keratin debris), continuous
DeafnessMild–moderate conductiveModerate–severe conductive (ossicular erosion)
ComplicationsRare🔴 Mastoiditis, facial nerve palsy, labyrinthitis, extradural/subdural abscess, brain abscess, meningitis
Q6c. Organisms

Mixed: Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, Proteus, E. coli, anaerobes ± fungi.

Q6d. Cholesteatoma (key concept)
  • Keratinizing squamous epithelium abnormally present in the middle ear (retraction pocket/immigration through perforation/congenital rest).
  • Layers: matrix (epithelium) → keratin debris accumulation → bone erosion by pressure + enzymatic (collagenase) activity.
  • Grossly pearly-white mass; microscopically keratin flakes beneath squamous lining.
Q6e. Management principles

Safe type: aural toilet, antibiotics, dry-ear closure (myringoplasty ± cortical mastoidectomy). Unsafe type: mastoidectomy (modified radical/radical) to exteriorize/remove cholesteatoma — prevent intracranial catastrophe.

🎯 "Discharging ear >2 weeks through a hole: SAFE central hole = mucosa only; UNSAFE attic hole + smelly cholesteatoma eats bone → facial palsy & brain abscess → surgery."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 16 (Head and Neck), pp. 731–751 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Head, Neck and Oral Cavity, pp. 136–143

Q8. Overview / Q8. Discuss the special characteristics of the CNS tumours.
  1. 🔴 No TNM staging — behaviour is governed by anatomical LOCATION and grade, not by stage; even histologically BENIGN tumours can kill by compressing vital medullary centres in the confined, non-expandable skull (a benign posterior-fossa meningioma can be fatal).
  2. 🔴 Location matters more than histology — a "benign" tumour in an eloquent/inaccessible site (brainstem, motor cortex) may be untreatable, while a malignant one in resectable frontal pole may do well.
  3. 🔴 Infiltrative growth: diffuse gliomas interdigitate with functioning brain — surgical excision can never be complete → recurrence inevitable; no true capsule.
  4. 🔴 Metastasis outside the CNS is extraordinarily rare — gliomas practically never leave the neuraxis (short survival + barriers); but spread within CSF pathways occurs (medulloblastoma "drop metastases" to cauda equina; "icing" over surfaces), rarely via ventriculoperitoneal shunts.
  5. Same histological type varies in prognosis by age and site (cerebellar pilocytic astrocytoma curable vs diffuse midline glioma fatal).
  6. Molecular markers now define entities: IDH mutation, 1p/19q codeletion (oligodendroglioma), MGMT-promoter methylation (temozolomide response) — incorporated into WHO grading.
  7. Incidence ~10–17/100,000; ~20% of childhood cancers; adult tumours mostly supratentorial, childhood mostly posterior fossa.
  8. 🔴 Most common intracranial tumour overall = metastatic carcinoma (¼–½ of hospitalized cancer patients; lung, breast, melanoma, kidney, GI).
🎯 "No TNM, no capsules, no distant mets — benign kills by compression, gliomas kill by infiltration, children seed via CSF; location > histology; metastases outnumber everyone."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 28 (Central Nervous System), pp. 1241–1304 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Central Nervous System, pp. 363–372 · Unit-II Body Fluids (CSF), pp. 456–469

🔴 Glioma = the umbrella term for primary brain tumours arising from glial cells — astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and ependymal cells; together they constitute the most common group of primary brain neoplasms.

Q10a. Definition

🔴 Schwannoma (neurilemmoma) = benign, encapsulated tumour of SCHWANN cells of cranial/spinal/peripheral nerves; sporadic in 90%; the commonest intracranial site is CN VIII at the cerebellopontine angle (vestibular/acoustic schwannoma — tinnitus + sensorineural hearing loss).

Q10c. Genetics & associations
  • 🔴 Biallelic loss of NF2 gene (merlin, chromosome 22q12).
  • 🔴 Bilateral vestibular schwannomas = hallmark of NEUROFIBROMATOSIS TYPE 2 (NF2 also → multiple meningiomas, spinal ependymomas).
  • Schwannomatosis: multiple NON-vestibular schwannomas (SMARCB1/LZTR1).
Q10d. Behaviour

Benign; recurs rarely after complete excision; 🔴 malignant transformation exceedingly rare (<1%); treatment = surgical enucleation preserving the parent nerve.

🎯 "Schwann cell tumour in a capsule sitting beside the nerve: Antoni A palisades make Verocay bodies, Antoni B is myxoid mush, S-100 lights up, NF2/merlin gone — bilateral acoustic = NF2."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 28 (Central Nervous System), pp. 1241–1304 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Central Nervous System, pp. 363–372 · Unit-II Body Fluids (CSF), pp. 456–469
Q11a. Definition

🔴 Neurofibroma = benign peripheral-nerve-sheath tumour containing a MIXED population of Schwann cells, perineurial-like cells, fibroblasts and mast cells, with entrapped axons — often solitary in normal individuals, multiple when part of NEUROFIBROMATOSIS TYPE 1 (NF1, neurofibromin, 17q11.2).

Q11b. Variants
VariantFeatures
Localized cutaneous (most common)Solitary nodular skin lesion; not NF1-linked when solitary
DiffusePlaque-like subcutaneous expansion, children, head & neck
🔴 PlexiformAlong large deep nerves — nerve expanded into a tortuous rope/"bag of worms"; 🔴 PATHOGNOMONIC of NF1; congenital; risk of malignant change
Q11c. Morphology
  • Gross: unencapsulated, fusiform expansion of the whole nerve segment — cannot be separated from the nerve.
  • Microscopy: haphazardly arranged spindle cells in myxoid matrix; 🔴 "shredded-carrot" collagen (wispy strands); mast cells; axons run through the lesion; S-100 positive but patchy (vs diffuse in schwannoma).
Q11d. Behaviour
  • Benign and slow-growing; recurrence if incompletely excised.
  • 🔴 Plexiform neurofibroma undergoes malignant transformation to malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (MPNST) in 3–5% — sudden growth/pain = alarm.
🎯 "Neurofibroma = mixed-cell tangle inside the nerve with shredded-carrot collagen — cutaneous ones are common; plexiform 'bag-of-worms' screams NF1 and may turn sarcoma."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 28 (Central Nervous System), pp. 1241–1304 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Central Nervous System, pp. 363–372 · Unit-II Body Fluids (CSF), pp. 456–469

🔴 Meningitis = inflammation of the meninges (leptomeninges — pia and arachnoid — and the CSF within the subarachnoid space), clinically marked by fever, headache, neck stiffness and photophobia; meningeal irritation gives Kernig and Brudzinski signs.

Q17b. Spider-web (cobweb) clot

🔴 A fine cobweb/spider-web coagulum forms when CSF protein (fibrinogen) is markedly elevated — the meningeal inflammation lets plasma proteins including fibrinogen leak in; on standing, fibrin strands weave a delicate web (classically over hours).

  • 🔴 Classic setting = TUBERCULOUS meningitis (protein often >100–500 mg/dL) — the web may trap AFB, raising smear yield.
  • Also seen whenever CSF protein is very high: Froin syndrome (meningeal block — xanthochromia + high protein + spontaneous coagulation), complete spinal block, pyogenic meningitis (heavier clot).
🎯 "Normal CSF = crystal-clear, 60–180 mm water pressure, 0–5 lymphocytes, protein 15–45, sugar ⅔ of blood; SPIDER-WEB = leaked fibrinogen from HIGH PROTEIN → think TB meningitis."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 28 (Central Nervous System), pp. 1241–1304 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Central Nervous System, pp. 363–372 · Unit-II Body Fluids (CSF), pp. 456–469

Q21c. Complications
  1. 🔴 Post-LP (post–dural-puncture) headache — most COMMON (10–30%); low-pressure headache worse upright, relieved lying flat; managed by bed rest, fluids, analgesics, caffeine, epidural blood patch.
  2. 🔴 Cerebral/tonsillar herniation (coning) — most FEARED; follows LP in raised ICT with focal lesion.
  3. Infection — local sepsis, arachnoiditis, discitis, iatrogenic meningitis.
  4. Bleeding — spinal subdural/epidural hematoma → cord compression emergency.
  5. Nerve root injury/radiculopathy, transient leg pain/back pain; late epidermoid tumor if stylet technique not used.
🎯 "Do it for diagnosis & intrathecal drugs; DON'T do it if the brain is full (CT first!), skin is dirty, or blood won't clot — fear CONING most, headache most often."

Q23a. Likely diagnosis

🔴 Acute PYOGENIC (bacterial) meningitis — a medical emergency.

Reasoning from the stem (say these in viva):

ClueInterpretation
Young adult + high feverAcute infection
UnconsciousnessCerebral involvement/toxic encephalopathy
🔴 Neck rigidity + Kernig sign positiveMeningeal irritation (meningism)
🔴 TURBID CSF with increased pressurePurulent (bacterial) exudate — clear fluid would suggest viral/TB

Likely organisms at this age: Neisseria meningitidis (look for petechial rash) or Streptococcus pneumoniae.

Q23b. How to confirm
  1. 🔴 CSF analysis: turbid appearance, opening pressure ↑, neutrophilic pleocytosis (hundreds–thousands/µL), protein ↑, glucose ↓↓ (<40 mg/dL, <40% of paired blood glucose) — confirms bacterial meningitis.
  2. 🔴 Gram stain of centrifuged CSF deposit — shows the organism (gram-negative diplococci / gram-positive diplococci) in 60–90% before antibiotics.
  3. 🔴 CSF culture & sensitivity (± blood cultures taken before antibiotics) — gold standard; latex agglutination antigen tests and multiplex PCR help if antibiotics were already given.
  4. Supporting: CBC neutrophilic leukocytosis, ↑CRP/procalcitonin, CT head if focal signs/impaired consciousness before LP.

⚠️ Do not delay treatment: after drawing blood/CSF cultures, start empirical IV third-generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone) ± vancomycin + dexamethasone immediately.

🎯 "Fever + stiff neck + Kernig + TURBID CSF = pyogenic meningitis → prove it: neutrophils↑↑, protein↑, glucose↓↓, GRAM STAIN + CULTURE (and blood cultures) → antibiotics without waiting."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 28 (Central Nervous System), pp. 1241–1304 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Central Nervous System, pp. 363–372 · Unit-II Body Fluids (CSF), pp. 456–469

# Part E — Cerebrovascular Disease (Q24)

Q24a. Definition

🔴 Cerebrovascular disease (CVD) = any abnormality of the brain caused by a pathological process involving its blood vessels — chiefly thrombotic or embolic arterial occlusion, systemic hypoperfusion, or vessel rupture with hemorrhage.

📌 Stroke = sudden onset of focal/global neurologic deficit of vascular origin persisting >24 h; resolving within 24 h = transient ischemic attack (TIA).

🔴 ISCHEMIC stroke (~85%)ThromboticAtherosclerotic plaque (HTN/diabetes) ulcerates at 🔴 carotid bifurcation, MCA origin, ends of basilar → platelet-fibrin thrombus → occlusion → pale infarct
