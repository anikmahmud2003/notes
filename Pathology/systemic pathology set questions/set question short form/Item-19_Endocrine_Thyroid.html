Q2a. Definitions

🔴 Thyrotoxicosis = the clinical syndrome of excess thyroid hormone action in the tissues, regardless of source. This is the broader term — it can result from overproduction of thyroid hormones, release of preformed hormones (thyroiditis), exogenous hormone intake, or ectopic sources.

🔴 Hyperthyroidism = a specific cause of thyrotoxicosis where the thyroid gland itself is overfunctioning. This is the subset of thyrotoxicosis due to increased synthesis and secretion of thyroid hormones by the thyroid gland.

Q2b. Relationship
  • All hyperthyroidism causes thyrotoxicosis (but not all thyrotoxicosis is hyperthyroidism)
  • Thyrotoxicosis WITHOUT hyperthyroidism: subacute thyroiditis (viral release of preformed hormones), struma ovarii (ectopic thyroid tissue in ovarian teratoma), factitious thyrotoxicosis (exogenous thyroxine ingestion), ectopic thyroid tissue
  • TSH level distinguishes: In true hyperthyroidism, TSH is suppressed (except TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma). In thyroiditis/factitious/ectopic, TSH is also suppressed (low radioiodine uptake differentiates these from Graves)
Q2ca. Clinical Features of Thyrotoxicosis — Hypermetabolic state
  • Hypermetabolic state: weight loss despite increased appetite, heat intolerance, sweating, warm moist skin, increased BMR
Q2cb. Clinical Features of Thyrotoxicosis — Cardiac
  • Cardiac: tachycardia, palpitations, atrial fibrillation (older patients), heart failure
Q2cc. Clinical Features of Thyrotoxicosis — Sympathetic overactivity
  • Sympathetic overactivity: tremor, anxiety, insomnia, lid lag, wide staring gaze
Q2cd. Clinical Features of Thyrotoxicosis — GI
  • GI: hyperdefecation
Q2ce. Clinical Features of Thyrotoxicosis — Musculoskeletal
  • Musculoskeletal: proximal muscle weakness (thyroid myopathy), osteoporosis
Q2cf. Clinical Features of Thyrotoxicosis — Reproductive
  • Reproductive: menstrual irregularities, gynecomastia in men
Q2cg. Clinical Features of Thyrotoxicosis — Thyroid storm
  • Thyroid storm: abrupt severe thyrotoxicosis — fever + tachycardia out of proportion; medical emergency; untreated → cardiac arrhythmia → death
Q2cx. Clinical Features of Thyrotoxicosis — recap
🎯 "Thyrotoxicosis = excess hormone in tissues (any cause); hyperthyroidism = thyroid overfunction. TSH is the best single screening test."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 24 (Endocrine System — Thyroid), pp. 1065–1132 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Endocrine System, pp. 310–335

# Part B — Thyroiditis & Goiter (Q5–7)

Q5a. Definition

🔴 Hashimoto thyroiditis (chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis) = the most common cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient areas and the most common nonendemic goiter in children. An autoimmune disorder characterized by thyroid destruction by autoreactive lymphocytes.

Q5b. Epidemiology
  • Peak: 45–65 years; F:M = 10–20:1
  • Most common cause of primary hypothyroidism in developed countries
  • Associated with other autoimmune diseases (T1D, SLE, myasthenia gravis, Sjögren syndrome, pernicious anemia)
Q5c. Pathogenesis

🔴 Breakdown of self-tolerance → lymphocytic attack on thyroid follicular cells:

  1. CD8+ cytotoxic T-cell killing of follicular cells (primary mechanism)
  2. CD4+ Th1 cells → IFN-γ → macrophage activation → tissue damage
  3. Antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) — minor role

Autoantibodies:

  • 🔴 Anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) — in vast majority (>90%)
  • 🔴 Anti-thyroglobulin — in ~60–80%
  • Both are markers of autoimmune destruction, not primary mediators

Susceptibility genes: HLA-DR3, DR5; CTLA4, PTPN22, IL2RA (shared with Graves and T1D)

Q5d. Morphology
FeatureDescription
GrossDiffuse, symmetric enlargement of both thyroid lobes; firm, pale, lobulated cut surface
Microscopy🔴 Dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate with prominent germinal centers; atrophic thyroid follicles lined by Hürthle (oxyphil/oncocytes) cells — abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm (packed mitochondria, a metaplastic response to chronic injury); minimal/no colloid; fibrosis (does NOT extend beyond capsule vs Riedel)
Q5e. Clinical Features
  • Painless, firm, diffuse goiter (the "classic" presentation)
  • Gradual onset of hypothyroidism: fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, menstrual irregularities, depression
  • Hashitoxicosis — transient phase of thyrotoxicosis (from follicular disruption releasing preformed hormones) → hypothyroidism
  • In some cases: presents as hypothyroidism from the start (no goiter phase)
Q5f. Associations
  • Other autoimmune diseases (T1D, Addison, pernicious anemia, Sjögren, MG, celiac)
  • 🔴 ↑ risk of extranodal marginal zone B-cell lymphoma (MALT lymphoma) of thyroid
  • Possible predisposition to papillary carcinoma (controversial)
Q5g. Treatment
  • Levothyroxine replacement (lifelong for hypothyroidism)
  • Monitoring TSH levels
🎯 "Hashimoto = anti-TPO + anti-Tg + Hürthle cells + lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate + germinal centers → hypothyroidism. Most common cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient areas."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 24 (Endocrine System — Thyroid), pp. 1065–1132 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Endocrine System, pp. 310–335
Q6a. Definition

🔴 Goiter = abnormal enlargement of the thyroid gland. It is a descriptive term, not a specific diagnosis. Can be diffuse or nodular, toxic or nontoxic.

Q6b. Classification
TypeDescription
Diffuse nontoxic (simple) goiterSymmetrical enlargement without nodules or hyperfunction
Multinodular goiter (MNG)Long-standing goiter converted to irregular nodular enlargement
Toxic goiterGoiter with hyperthyroidism (Graves, toxic MNG)
Q6c. Pathogenesis of Goiter in Iodine Deficiency

🔴 The fundamental mechanism: impaired thyroid hormone synthesis → compensatory TSH elevation → follicular hypertrophy/hyperplasia → thyroid enlargement.

Step-by-Step Pathogenesis:

↓ Iodide trapping by thyroid follicular cells
   ↓
↓ Thyroid hormone (T3/T4) synthesis
   ↓
↓ Serum T3/T4 levels
   ↓
Loss of negative feedback on anterior pituitary
   ↑ TSH secretion
   ↓
TSH stimulates thyroid follicular cells
   → Hypertrophy (larger cells) + Hyperplasia (more cells)
   ↓
THYROID ENLARGEMENT = GOITER
   ↓
Compensatory increase in iodide trapping efficiency
   → May restore euthyroidism
   → Or persistent goiter with euthyroid state

Goitrogens — Substances That Inhibit Thyroid Hormone Synthesis

  • Brassicaceae vegetables: cabbage, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, turnips
  • Cassava — contains thiocyanate (inhibits iodide transport)
  • Drugs: lithium, amiodarone, sulfonamides, aminobenzoic acid derivatives
  • Thiocyanate, perchlorate — inhibit sodium-iodide symporter (NIS)

Dyshormonogenetic Goiter

  • Inborn errors of thyroid hormone synthesis (autosomal recessive)
  • Defects in: thyroglobulin synthesis, iodide trapping, organification, coupling, deiodinase
  • Presents in childhood as large goiter with euthyroid or hypothyroid state
Q6d. Clinical Features
  • Goiter: visible/palpable enlargement; may cause compressive symptoms (dysphagia, dyspnea, hoarseness)
  • Most patients are euthyroid (compensated)
  • Serum TSH at upper limit of normal
  • Large goiters → compressive symptoms, cosmetic concerns, retrosternal extension

Q7a. Definition

🔴 Graves disease = the most common cause of endogenous hyperthyroidism (~85% of thyrotoxicosis cases). An autoimmune disorder characterized by stimulatory autoantibodies against the TSH receptor, causing diffuse thyroid hyperplasia, hyperthyroidism, and characteristic extrathyroidal manifestations.

Q7b. Epidemiology
  • Peak: 20–40 years
  • F:M = 10:1
  • 1.5–2% of US women
  • Monozygotic concordance 30–40% (dizygotic <5%)
  • Genetic associations: HLA-DR3, CTLA4, PTPN22, IL2RA, TSHR locus
Q7c. Pathogenesis — Detailed

🔴 Core mechanism: thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulin (TSI) mimics TSH → uncontrolled thyroid hormone production.

The Autoimmune Cascade:

Loss of immune tolerance to TSH receptor
   ↓
B-cell activation → production of autoantibodies
   ↓
TSH RECEPTOR ANTIBODIES:
   1. TSI (Thyroid-Stimulating Immunoglobulin) = ~90%
      → Mimics TSH → activates adenylyl cyclase → ↑cAMP
      → ↑ thyroid hormone synthesis + secretion
      → ↑ iodide uptake + organification
      → Thyroid growth (diffuse hyperplasia)
   2. Blocking antibodies (rare)
      → Block TSH binding → HYPOTHYROIDISM (rare presentation)
   3. Neutral antibodies (no functional effect)

Extrathyroidal Manifestations:

##### 1. Ophthalmopathy (30–50% of patients)

  • 🔴 CD4+ T cells infiltrate retro-orbital tissues
  • Release cytokines → stimulate fibroblasts → glycosaminoglycan (GAG) deposition in extraocular muscles and retro-orbital connective tissue
  • Edema, mononuclear cell infiltrate, ↑ adipocytes → swelling of extraocular muscles
  • Proptosis (exophthalmos) — eye pushed forward
  • Can persist/progress despite treating thyrotoxicosis
  • Severe → corneal exposure → ulceration; compressive optic neuropathy

##### 2. Pretibial Myxedema (Dermopathy) — <5% of patients

  • Localized accumulation of GAGs (hyaluronic acid) in dermis of pretibial skin
  • Non-pitting edema, thickened, "orange peel" appearance
  • Usually over pretibial area (also dorsum of feet/hands)

##### 3. Thyroid Acropachy — Very rare

  • Clubbing of fingers and toes
  • Periosteal new bone formation

Why Does Autoimmunity Develop?

  • Genetic susceptibility (HLA-DR3, CTLA4, PTPN22, TSHR polymorphisms)
  • Environmental triggers: stress, smoking (↑ ophthalmopathy), iodine excess, postpartum state, infection
  • Defective regulatory T-cells → failure to suppress autoreactive clones

  • Goiter: diffuse, symmetric, non-tender, firm; audible bruit (increased vascularity)

Q7ec. Clinical Features — Eye signs
  • Eye signs: lid lag, wide staring gaze (sympathetic overactivity) + true exophthalmos (ophthalmopathy)
Q7ed. Clinical Features — Skin
  • Skin: warm, moist, pretibial myxedema

  • Cardiac: atrial fibrillation (older patients), high-output heart failure

  • Thyroid storm: fever + tachycardia out of proportion → medical emergency

# Part C — Papillary Carcinoma & Investigation (Q8–9)

Q8a. Definition

🔴 Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) = the most common thyroid malignancy (~80–85% of thyroid cancers) and the most common endocrine malignancy overall. Arises from follicular epithelial cells.

Q8b. Epidemiology
  • Peak: 25–50 years (younger than follicular)
  • F:M = 3–4:1
  • Incidence rising (more microcarcinomas found incidentally)
  • Most common radiation-associated thyroid cancer (Chernobyl children)
Q8c. Pathogenesis
Genetic AlterationFrequencyDetails
BRAF V600E50–80%Point mutation → constitutive MAPK activation; associated with classic and tall-cell variants; aggressive features
RET/PTC fusions~10–20%Chromosomal rearrangement (10q11); radiation-associated; children > adults
NTRK fusions~5%Fusion oncogenes; across all ages
RAS~10–15%More in follicular variant PTC
Q8d. Morphology — EXAM FAVORITE

Gross

  • Single or multiple; may be multifocal (20–40%)
  • Cut surface: white-gray, solid, well-circumscribed or infiltrative
  • May show psammoma bodies (gritty sensation on cutting)

Microscopy — Nuclear Features 🔴🔴

🔴 The nuclear features can diagnose PTC even WITHOUT papillae!

FeatureDescription
Orphan Annie eye nucleiFinely dispersed chromatin → optically clear, ground-glass appearance; most characteristic feature
Nuclear groovesLongitudinal folds in the nuclear membrane
Nuclear pseudo-inclusionsInvaginations of cytoplasm into the nucleus (appear as intranuclear cytoplasmic inclusions)
Psammoma bodies🔴 Concentrically calcified spheres — essentially NEVER in follicular or medullary carcinoma; strong indicator of PTC on FNA

Architectural Patterns

PatternDescription
Classic (papillary)Branching papillae with fibrovascular cores lined by tumor cells
Follicular variantPapillary nuclear features + follicular architecture
Tall cell variantTall cells (height >3× width); older patients; aggressive; virtually always BRAF V600E

NIFTP (Noninvasive Follicular Thyroid Neoplasm with Papillary-like Nuclear Features)

  • 🔴 Encapsulated, no capsular/lymphatic/vascular invasion → ultra-low risk
  • "Carcinoma" removed from name
  • Lobectomy alone suffices (avoids total thyroidectomy complications)
  • Excellent prognosis
Q8e. Spread
  • 🔴 Lymphatic spread is the dominant route → cervical lymph node metastases in up to 50%
  • Blood vessel invasion is uncommon (especially in small lesions)
  • May be multifocal (satellite lesions within the gland)
  • Distant mets: lung, bone (rare, usually late)
  • Cervical lymph node mets do NOT worsen prognosis (unlike in most other cancers)
Q8f. Clinical Features
  • Asymptomatic thyroid nodule (most common presentation)
  • Cervical lymph node mass — may be the presenting complaint (especially in children/young adults)
  • Cold on scintigraphy
  • Incidental finding on ultrasound (thyroid incidentaloma)
Q8g. Prognosis
  • 🔴 10-yr survival >95% (excellent overall)
  • Microcarcinomas (<1 cm): near-100% cure with lobectomy
  • Poorer prognosis with: age >40, extrathyroidal extension, distant metastases, tall-cell variant
  • 5–20% recurrence locally; 10–15% distant mets
Q8h. Treatment
  • Surgery: total thyroidectomy or lobectomy (based on size/risk)
  • Radioactive iodine (I-131) ablation for high-risk cases
  • TSH suppression with levothyroxine
  • Monitoring: serum thyroglobulin + neck ultrasound
🎯 "PTC = most common thyroid cancer; Orphan Annie nuclei + grooves + pseudo-inclusions + psammoma bodies; lymphatic spread; BRAF V600E; >95% 10-yr survival."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 24 (Endocrine System — Thyroid), pp. 1065–1132 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Endocrine System, pp. 310–335

Causes of Primary Hypothyroidism:

  • 🔴 Hashimoto thyroiditis (most common in iodine-sufficient areas)
  • Iodine deficiency (most common worldwide)
  • Postablative (surgery, radioiodine, external radiation)
  • Drugs (lithium, amiodarone, interferon-α)
  • Congenital (dyshormonogenetic goiter, thyroid agenesis)
  • Infiltrative (amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, hemochromatosis)

Q9eb. Special Situations — Pregnancy
  • Pregnancy: ↑ TBG → ↑ total T4/T3; free T4 may be low-normal; TSH lower trimesters
Q9ec. Special Situations — Amiodarone
  • Amiodarone: type 1 (iodine-induced hyperthyroidism, ↑ uptake) vs type 2 (destructive thyroiditis, ↓ uptake)

  • Age/Sex: 24-year-old female

Q11a. Definition

🔴 Thyroglossal duct cyst = the most common clinically significant congenital anomaly of the thyroid gland. A remnant of the thyroglossal duct — the tract along which the thyroid descends from the foramen cecum of the tongue to its final position in the anterior neck during embryological development.

Q11b. Embryology
  • Thyroid gland develops from the floor of the pharynx (foramen cecum) at ~3–4 weeks gestation
  • Descends along the thyroglossal duct to its final position in the anterior neck
  • The duct normally obliterates by the 7th–10th week
  • Failure of obliteration → persistence of thyroglossal duct → cyst formation
Q11c. Epidemiology
  • 🔴 Most common congenital neck mass (after branchial cleft anomalies)
  • Accounts for ~70% of congenital neck midline masses
  • Can present at any age; most common in children (but also adults)
  • 75% occur below the hyoid bone (the hyoid bone is intimately related to the duct)

Q11e. Clinical Features
  • Painless, fluctuant midline neck mass that moves with:
  • 🔴 Deglutition (swallowing) — attached to hyoid bone
  • 🔴 Protrusion of tongue — attached to foramen cecum via fibrous band
  • Usually 1–4 cm; may enlarge during URTI
  • Infection: painful, red, may drain externally → sinus tract
  • Recurrent infections → fibrosis, sinus formation

Q11g. Investigations
  • Thyroid function tests — ensure normal thyroid function before surgery
  • Ultrasound — confirm cystic nature; locate normal thyroid gland
  • Thyroid scintigraphy (scan) — rule out ectopic thyroid (if scan shows absent thyroid in normal position → the ectopic tissue may be the only functioning thyroid)
  • FNA — if suspicious features (solid component, suspicious cytology)
Q11h. Treatment

🔴 Sistrunk procedure = the gold standard surgical treatment:

  1. Complete excision of the cyst + entire thyroglossal duct tract
  2. Resection of the central portion of the hyoid bone (where the duct passes through)
  3. Excision of a cuff of tissue up to the foramen cecum
  4. Recurrence rate <5% with Sistrunk procedure (vs 30–50% with simple excision)
Q11i. Complications
  • Infection → abscess formation → may require I&D before definitive surgery
  • Recurrent sinus tract (if incompletely excised)
  • Rarely: thyroglossal duct cyst carcinoma — usually papillary thyroid carcinoma (~1%); requires total thyroidectomy + neck dissection
  • Recurrence if Sistrunk procedure not performed
Q11j. Key Exam Points
  • 🔴 Moves with swallowing and tongue protrusion (hyoid connection)
  • 🔴 Sistrunk procedure = excision + central hyoid resection + tract up to foramen cecum
  • 🔴 Must confirm presence of normal thyroid gland in normal position before surgery (ectopic thyroid may be the only functioning tissue)
