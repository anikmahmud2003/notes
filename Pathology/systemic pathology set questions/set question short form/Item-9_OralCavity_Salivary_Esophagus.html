Q1a. Overview

📌 The oral cavity extends from the lips to the palatoglossal arch; its diseases fall into 6 major groups — developmental/tooth-related, inflammatory/reactive, infections, premalignant, malignant, and odontogenic lesions.

Q3ca. Squamous cell carcinoma — essentials — Risk factors
  • 🔴 Risk factors: smoked tobacco + alcohol (synergistic, oral cavity); betel-quid/paan chewing (India/Asia); HPV-16 (oropharynx — tonsil, base of tongue); actinic radiation + pipe smoking (lower lip).
Q3cb. Squamous cell carcinoma — essentials — Sites
  • Sites: ventral/lateral tongue, floor of mouth, lower lip, soft palate, gingiva.
Q3cc. Squamous cell carcinoma — essentials — Histology
  • Histology: invasive nests/sheets of keratinizing squamous cells with intercellular bridges, keratin pearls, dysplasia at margins; preceded by leukoplakia/erythroplakia.
Q3cd. Squamous cell carcinoma — essentials — Spread
  • Spread: local infiltration → lymphatics to submandibular, jugulodigastric, cervical nodes; distant mets late (lungs, liver, bone).
Q3ce. Squamous cell carcinoma — essentials — Molecular
  • Molecular: TP53, CDKN2A, PIK3CA, NOTCH1 mutations; field cancerization → second primaries 3–7%/yr.
Q3cf. Squamous cell carcinoma — essentials — Prognosis
  • Prognosis: 5-yr survival ~80% (early) vs ~20% (late); HPV-positive oropharyngeal SCC behaves better.
Q3cx. Squamous cell carcinoma — essentials — recap
🎯 "Erythroplakia > leukoplakia in danger (90% vs 5–25%) · SCC = 95% of oral cancer · tobacco+alcohol (oral) vs HPV-16 (oropharynx)."

Q4a. General rules (say these first)
  • Salivary tumours = <2% of all human tumours; 65–80% arise in the PAROTID; only ~5% occur before age 16.
  • 🔴 Malignant potential is INVERSELY proportional to gland size: parotid ~15–20% malignant → submandibular ~40% → minor glands ~50% → sublingual 70–90% malignant.

Q5a. Definition

Pleomorphic adenoma (mixed tumour) = the most common benign salivary gland neoplasm, composed of a mixture of ductal (epithelial), myoepithelial and mesenchymal-like (chondromyxoid) elements — hence "mixed tumour." Accounts for ~60% of parotid tumours; also submandibular/minor glands (palate). Molecular driver: PLAG1 overexpression (chromosomal rearrangement); HMGA2 in PLAG1-negative cases.

Q5b. Clinical features
  • Painless, slow-growing, mobile, firm swelling (parotid/preauricular region or palate); face of a middle-aged woman/man; 30–50 yr typical.
  • Facial nerve not involved (unlike carcinoma) — nerve sparing is a hallmark.
Q5c. Gross morphology
  • Rounded, well-demarcated, encapsulated-feeling mass, 4–6 cm; 🔴 capsule is INCOMPLETE/thin (especially in palate) — tumour tongues extend beyond it.
  • Cut surface: gray-white, glistening, myxoid/chondroid (translucent blue-grey), firm areas, occasional cysts, haemorrhage.
Q5d. Histology (microscopic)
  • Ductal epithelial cells forming ducts/acini lined by low cuboidal-to-columnar cells, often with an outer layer of modified myoepithelial cells.
  • Myoepithelial cells — plasmacytoid, spindled, stellate or clear — in sheets, cords and islands.
  • 🔴 Mesenchymal-like stroma: myxoid, chondroid (cartilage-like), mucinous, hyalinized, even osseous — produced by the modified myoepithelial cells, giving the "pleomorphic" appearance.
  • No anaplasia/mitoses; intact basement membranes around ducts.
Q5e. Behaviour & complications
PointData
Recurrence~25% after simple enucleation vs only ~4% after superficial parotidectomy (because capsule incomplete → satellite nodules left behind); recurrences often multinodular
Malignant transformationCarcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma: ~2% (<5 yr duration) → ~10% (>15 yr); heralded by rapid growth, fixation, facial palsy; extremely aggressive (30–50% 5-yr mortality)
TreatmentSuperficial (conservative) parotidectomy with facial-nerve preservation
🎯 "Mixed = epithelial + myoepithelial + chondromyxoid · incomplete capsule → recur (25% enucleation) · long-standing PA → carcinoma ex PA."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 16 (Head and Neck), pp. 731–751 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Head, Neck and Oral Cavity, pp. 136–143
Q6. Overview / Q6. Why is it called pleomorphic adenoma?
  • 🔴 The term "pleomorphic" describes the ARCHITECTURAL diversity, not cellular pleomorphism (anaplasia) — individual cells are actually bland and benign-looking.
  • One tumour shows multiple patterns simultaneously: ducts, tubules, acini, solid sheets, cords, islands and cysts formed by epithelial + myoepithelial cells.
  • In addition, the myoepithelial cells elaborate a mesenchymal-like stroma (myxoid, chondroid, osseous) — so the same tumour mimics both an epithelial tumour AND a connective-tissue tumour → old name "mixed tumour."
  • Hence: epithelium + mesenchyme-like matrix in varying proportions = pleomorphic appearance (Willis: "structural polymorphism").
  • 📌 Contrast: true nuclear pleomorphism/anaplasia would suggest carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma, which arises in long-standing cases.
Modified myoepithelial cell
   → secretes basement-membrane-like ground substance
   → myxoid / chondroid / osseous stroma
   + proliferating ductal epithelium (ducts, acini, sheets)
   = structurally "mixed"/pleomorphic tumour
🎯 "Pleomorphic = many architectural patterns (ducts + sheets + cartilage-like stroma), NOT many abnormal nuclei — cells stay benign."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 16 (Head and Neck), pp. 731–751 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Head, Neck and Oral Cavity, pp. 136–143

Q8a. Definition

Achalasia = a disorder of esophageal motility characterized by the triad of (1) incomplete relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES), (2) increased LES resting tone, and (3) aperistalsis of the esophageal body.

Q8b. Pathogenesis 
Loss/degeneration of NO-producing inhibitory neurons
of the myenteric (Auerbach) plexus
   → LES cannot relax + lost peristaltic wave (aperistalsis)
   → functional obstruction → food retention
   → progressive esophageal dilation + wall hypertrophy
   → "bird-beak" tapering on barium swallow
Q8c. Causes
TypeCauses
Primary achalasiaDegeneration of nitric oxide–producing (nitrergic) inhibitory neurons of LES; rare; slight ↑ esophageal SCC risk (insufficient for routine surveillance)
Secondary (pseudo-) achalasia🔴 Chagas disease (Trypanosoma cruzi destroys myenteric plexus — also duodenum/colon/ureters) · Diabetic autonomic neuropathy · Amyloidosis · Sarcoidosis · Systemic sclerosis · Infiltrating gastric cardia carcinoma (tumour pseudoachalasia) · Polio · Allgrove (triple-A) syndrome: achalasia + alacrima + ACTH-resistant adrenal insufficiency
Q8d. Clinical features
  • Progressive dysphagia to BOTH solids and liquids, regurgitation of undigested food, chest pain, weight loss, difficulty belching.
  • Barium swallow: dilated esophagus with smooth bird-beak tapering at GEJ; manometry confirms the triad.
  • Treatment: laparoscopic Heller myotomy, pneumatic balloon dilation, botulinum toxin.
🎯 "Triad: no relaxation + ↑ tone + aperistalsis · primary = nitrergic neuron loss · secondary = Chagas · bird-beak."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 17 (Gastrointestinal Tract), pp. 753–822 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Gastrointestinal System, pp. 144–191
Q9a. Definition

Barrett esophagus = intestinal metaplasia of the distal esophageal squamous mucosa caused by chronic gastroesophageal reflux (GERD), in which the normal stratified squamous epithelium is replaced by columnar epithelium containing goblet cells.

Q9b. Key facts
AspectDetails
EpidemiologyUp to 10% of symptomatic GERD, ~2% of general population; white males 40–60 yr; incidence rising
🔴 Diagnostic requirementPresence of GOBLET cells (pale-blue mucin vacuoles, wine-goblet shaped) in columnar-lined esophagus above the GEJ; non-goblet cardiac/foveolar metaplasia alone is controversial
ExtentLong segment ≥3 cm vs short segment <3 cm (short segment → lower dysplasia/cancer risk)

Q10a. Definition

Esophageal varices = abnormally dilated, tortuous subepithelial veins of the distal esophagus (and proximal stomach) that develop as portosystemic collateral channels when portal hypertension forces blood to shunt from the portal system into the systemic circulation.

Q10b. Pathogenesis 
Cirrhosis / hepatic schistosomiasis / other portal HTN
   → portal venous pressure ↑ (impaired flow through liver)
   → reversal of flow through LEFT GASTRIC (coronary) vein
   → engorgement of lower esophageal + proximal gastric veins
   → tortuous bulging submucosal varices
   → mucosal erosion / ↑ venous pressure → RUPTURE → hematemesis
Q10c. Key facts
  • 🔴 Cause: portal hypertension — most common = cirrhosis (esp. alcoholic liver disease); worldwide #2 = hepatic schistosomiasis.
  • Present in 30% of compensated and ~60% of decompensated cirrhosis.
  • Rupture risk factors: large/tortuous varices, raised hepatic venous pressure gradient, previous bleed, advanced liver disease (red wale marks, cherry-red spots).
  • 🔴 Each bleed carries 15–20% mortality; >50% rebleed within 1 year.
  • Morphology: longitudinal columns of dilated bluish submucosal veins just above the GEJ, thin-walled, eroded epithelium, thrombi possible.
  • Treatment: prophylaxis β-blockers (↓ portal flow) + endoscopic variceal ligation; active bleed — sclerotherapy, balloon tamponade, TIPS.
🎯 "Portal HTN → left gastric vein shunting → varices · cirrhosis (US) vs schistosomiasis (worldwide) · 15–20% mortality per bleed, 50% rebleed."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 17 (Gastrointestinal Tract), pp. 753–822 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Gastrointestinal System, pp. 144–191

Q13a. Explanation (step-wise)

Barrett esophagus is premalignant because the metaplastic goblet-cell epithelium accumulates sequential genetic alterations and passes through recognizable histologic stages ending in adenocarcinoma:

Chronic GERD (acid + bile reflux)
   → squamous injury → repair
   → INTESTINAL METAPLASIA (goblet cells) = Barrett
   → LOW-GRADE dysplasia
   → HIGH-GRADE dysplasia (budding, cribriform glands)
   → intramucosal ADENOCARCINOMA (lamina propria invasion)
   → invasive adenocarcinoma of distal esophagus
Q13b. Molecular evidence (why the sequence is real)
StageMolecular events
Barrett without dysplasiaEarly: TP53 loss/mutation + CDKN2A (p16) silencing by deletion or promoter hypermethylation — drivers detectable even BEFORE histologic dysplasia
ProgressionAmplification of EGFR, ERBB2 (HER2), MET, cyclin D1, cyclin E
Q13c. Epidemiologic evidence
  • Barrett carries 30–125× increased relative risk of adenocarcinoma; adenocarcinoma arises only within Barrett mucosa (distal third).
  • Incidence of Barrett-associated adenocarcinoma has risen in parallel with GERD/obesity epidemics.
Q13d. Practical implications (what makes it clinically "premalignant")
  • Requires surveillance endoscopy with biopsies (4-quadrant protocol) to detect dysplasia.
  • High-grade dysplasia / multifocal lesions → endoscopic mucosal resection, radiofrequency ablation, photodynamic therapy or esophagectomy.
  • 📌 Caveat: most patients with Barrett NEVER develop cancer — risk is real but modest per year; surveillance benefit debated in trials.
🎯 "Metaplasia → LGD → HGD → adenoCa with TP53 + CDKN2A first, growth-factor amplification later — hence Barrett = premalignant (surveillance!)."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 17 (Gastrointestinal Tract), pp. 753–822 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Gastrointestinal System, pp. 144–191
Q14a. Malignant tumours
  1. 🔴 Squamous cell carcinoma — most common worldwide; middle third; keratinizing/nonkeratinizing nests recapitulating squamous epithelium; variants: verrucous, basaloid, spindle-cell.
  2. 🔴 Adenocarcinoma — distal third; arises in Barrett esophagus; mucin-producing intestinal-type glands; signet-ring/small-cell variants possible; now >50% of US esophageal cancers.
  3. Rare primary: small-cell (neuroendocrine) carcinoma, adenosquamous carcinoma, mucoepidermoid carcinoma, melanoma, lymphoma.
  4. Secondary (metastatic) tumours — direct invasion from gastric cardia; distant metastases uncommon to esophagus.

Q15a. (a) Aphthous ulceration (canker sore)

Definition: a recurrent, painful, shallow round-to-oval ulceration of the nonkeratinized oral mucosa with an erythematous halo, occurring singly or in crops.

  • Most common oral mucosal lesion; up to 40% of the population; begins in the first two decades, recurs periodically, heals spontaneously in 7–10 days.
  • Site: lips, tongue, buccal mucosa, floor of mouth (movable, nonkeratinized mucosa).
  • Microscopy: superficial ulcer covered by fibrinopurulent membrane over granulation tissue; mononuclear infiltrate at the base.
  • 🔴 Associations (systemic): celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn/UC), Behçet disease; also stress, hormonal changes, trauma, nutritional deficiency.
  • Treatment: topical steroids/anaesthetics; self-limiting.
Q15b. (b) Leukoplakia

Definition (WHO): a predominantly white lesion/plaque of the oral mucosa that CANNOT be characterised clinically or pathologically as any other disease — i.e., a persistent, NON-SCRAPABLE white patch.

  • Frequency ~3% of adults; peak 40–70 yr, male predominance; tobacco is the common antecedent (also alcohol, chronic friction, HPV).
  • Sites: buccal mucosa, floor of mouth, ventral tongue, palate, gingiva.
  • Microscopy spectrum: hyperkeratosis/parakeratosis + acanthosis → mild/moderate/severe epithelial dysplasia → carcinoma in situ → invasive SCC.
  • 🔴 Premalignant: 5–25% show dysplasia or progress to carcinoma — therefore biopsy every lesion; excision of dysplastic lesions.
  • Related lesion: erythroplakia (red velvety) — far more ominous (~90% severe dysplasia/CIS).
🎯 "Aphthous = painful recurrent shallow ulcer + celiac/IBD/Behçet · Leukoplakia = non-scrapable white patch, 5–25% precancer, always biopsy."
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 16 (Head and Neck), pp. 731–751 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Head, Neck and Oral Cavity, pp. 136–143
