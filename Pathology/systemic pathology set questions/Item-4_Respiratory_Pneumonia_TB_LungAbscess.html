Q1. Mention the causes of pulmonary edema. What is heart failure cell?

Definition

🔴 Pulmonary edema = abnormal accumulation of fluid in the extravascular compartments of the lung (interstitium + alveolar spaces).

Causes — two main categories

CategoryMechanismCauses
A. Hemodynamic (cardiogenic / transudative)↑ Hydrostatic pressure🔴 Left-sided congestive heart failure (most common), mitral stenosis, volume overload, pulmonary vein obstruction
↓ Oncotic pressureHypoalbuminemia — nephrotic syndrome, chronic liver disease, protein-losing enteropathy
Lymphatic obstructionRare — tumor infiltration, fibrosis
B. Microvascular (alveolar) injury — non-cardiogenic, exudativeDirect alveolar/endothelial injuryBacterial pneumonia, inhaled gases (O₂ toxicity, smoke, corrosive fumes), liquid aspiration (gastric contents, near-drowning), radiation, lung contusion
Indirect injury🔴 Sepsis/SIRS, burns, pancreatitis, extensive trauma, TRALI, DIC; drugs — bleomycin, amphotericin B, heroin, methadone, cocaine, paraquat
Undetermined originHigh-altitude pulmonary edema, neurogenic (CNS injury)

Pathogenesis (arrow chain)

Left heart failure / mitral stenosis
→ ↑ pulmonary venous & capillary hydrostatic pressure
→ fluid transudes into interstitium (perivascular/peribronchial cuffing)
→ exceeds lymphatic drainage capacity
→ flooding of alveolar spaces with pale pink granular transudate
→ impaired gas exchange → dyspnea, orthopnea, hypoxemia
Microvascular injury (sepsis/pneumonia/aspiration/toxins)
→ endothelial + type I pneumocyte damage
→ ↑ capillary permeability → protein-rich exudate fills alveoli
→ hyaline membranes (if severe = ARDS/diffuse alveolar damage)

Morphology (brief)

  • Heavy, wet, boggy lungs; frothy fluid on cut surface.
  • Dependent basal regions of lower lobes first (gravitational).
  • Microscopy: engorged alveolar capillaries + finely granular pale pink intra-alveolar transudate.

🔴 Heart Failure Cell

Hemosiderin-laden alveolar macrophages seen in chronic pulmonary congestion (left heart failure, mitral stenosis): RBCs escape into alveoli due to congestion → macrophages phagocytose them → iron converted to hemosiderin (golden-brown granules; Prussian blue positive). Repeated episodes → fibrosis + thickened alveolar walls = brown induration of the lung (firm, brown, soggy lungs; predisposes to infection).

🎯 Heart failure cell = hemosiderin-filled alveolar macrophage in chronic pulmonary venous congestion; many such cells + fibrosis = brown induration.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 15 (Lung), pp. 673–728 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Respiratory System, pp. 88–135
Q2. What are the common fates of pulmonary embolism? What is the most common source of such embolus?

🔴 Most common source

Deep leg vein thrombi (>95% of pulmonary emboli) — popliteal, femoral and iliac veins above the knee (formed by Virchow triad: endothelial injury, stasis, hypercoagulability). Other sources: deep pelvic veins, right heart chambers, indwelling central venous catheters (right atrial thrombus); rare unusual emboli — fat, air/amniotic fluid, tumor.

Common fates / outcomes of pulmonary embolism

FateFrequencyExplanation
Clinically silent — resolved by fibrinolysis🔴 Majority (60–80%)Small emboli; organized/resolved by fibrinolytic system; no sequelae
Acute massive PE → sudden death / acute cor pulmonale~2.5–5%Large embolus straddles bifurcation (saddle embolus) or occludes main PA branches → electromechanical dissociation, instantaneous death
Pulmonary infarctionOnly ~10% of emboli infarctRequires compromised bronchial arterial circulation (heart/lung disease); wedge-shaped, apex toward hilum, hemorrhagic; heals by scar
Recurrent small emboliChronic→ chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension + chronic cor pulmonale
Septic emboliInfected thrombi→ pulmonary abscess / septic infarcts

Morphology (must say)

  • Large emboli: coiled/saddle mass in main pulmonary artery; distinguish postmortem clot by lines of Zahn.
  • Infarct: wedge-shaped, hemorrhagic, apex toward hilum, 75% lower lobes, often multiple; fibrinous pleuritis over it; RBC lysis → red-brown (hemosiderin) → fibrous scar.

Diagnosis (one line)

D-dimer (normal excludes) → CT pulmonary angiography (definitive); duplex US for leg DVT; CXR wedge infiltrate appears 12–36 h after infarction.

🎯 PE source = deep leg veins >95%; fates: mostly silent/fibrinolyzed, some sudden death (saddle), ~10% infarction, recurrent → pulmonary HTN, septic → abscess.

Q3. What is Pneumonia? Classify Pneumonia according to etiology.

Definition

🔴 Pneumonia = any infection of the lung parenchyma (distal airways + alveoli) associated with exudation of inflammatory cells into the air spaces. It is the most common infectious cause of death worldwide; defense failures (lost cough reflex, mucociliary dysfunction, pooled secretions, impaired alveolar macrophages, congestion/edema, immune defects) allow pathogens to reach the lower respiratory tract.

Classification according to ETIOLOGY

TypeOrganisms / Agents
1. Bacterial — Typical (pyogenic)🔴 Streptococcus pneumoniae (#1), H. influenzae, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Moraxella catarrhalis, Streptococcus pyogenes
2. Bacterial — Atypical🔴 Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila, Chlamydophila pneumoniae (intracellular organisms; not visible on routine Gram stain)
3. ViralInfluenza A/B, RSV, rhinovirus, human metapneumovirus, adenovirus, measles, CMV, SARS-CoV-2; often followed by bacterial superinfection (S. aureus)
4. FungalEndemic dimorphic — Histoplasma capsulatum, Blastomyces dermatitidis, Coccidioides immitis; opportunistic — Aspergillus, Candida, _Pneumocystis jirovecii_ (in immunocompromised)
5. Protozoal / parasiticToxoplasma, Strongyloides, migrating parasites (rare)
6. Aspiration (mixed chemical + bacterial)Oral flora — anaerobes (Bacteroides, Fusobacterium, Peptococcus) ± aerobes; debilitated/unconscious patients
7. Non-infective (inflammatory mimics)Radiation pneumonitis, chemical pneumonitis, lipoid pneumonia, hypersensitivity pneumonitis

📌 Other parallel classifications: by anatomical pattern — lobar, bronchopneumonia (lobular), interstitial (viral/mycoplasmal); by setting — community-acquired, hospital-acquired/nosocomial, health care–associated, aspiration, chronic, necrotizing (abscess), pneumonia in immunocompromised host.

🎯 Etiology in one line: typical pyogenic bacteria (pneumococcus chief), atypicals (Mycoplasma/Legionella/Chlamydia), viruses, fungi, parasites, aspiration.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 15 (Lung), pp. 673–728 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Respiratory System, pp. 88–135

How pneumonia is CONFIRMED in the laboratory

  1. Sputum examination
  • Macroscopic: amount, color — rusty brown (pneumococcal), greenish-yellow (purulent), foul odor (anaerobic/abscess).
  • Gram staining: pus cells + predominant organism (e.g., gram-positive lanceolate diplococci = pneumococcus; gram-negative encapsulated rods = Klebsiella).
  • Culture & antibiotic sensitivity (blood/chocolate agar; avoid salivary contamination — examine pus cells & epithelial cells per HPF first).
  • Special stains/culture: ZN stain for AFB (TB), KOH mount for fungi.
  1. Blood investigations: total + differential WBC count (neutrophilic leukocytosis in bacterial; normal/low in viral/atypical), blood culture (positive in 20–30% pneumococcal cases), CRP/procalcitonin, ABG.
  2. Urinary antigen tests: Legionella and S. pneumoniae antigens (rapid).
  3. Serology / molecular: paired sera for Mycoplasma (cold agglutinins, IgM ELISA); PCR/multiplex panels for respiratory viruses & atypicals.
  4. Imaging support: CXR (consolidation with air bronchogram), HRCT if needed.
  5. Pleural fluid analysis (if effusion/empyema): exudate, neutrophils, Gram stain, culture.
  6. Bronchoscopy with BAL in non-resolving pneumonia (immunocompromised).
🎯 Confirm pneumonia = sputum Gram stain + culture & sensitivity + blood culture + urinary antigen/PCR; rusty sputum with gram-positive diplococci = pneumococcus.

Q5. What are the stages of lobar pneumonia? Discuss about morphology/pathology of each stage of Lobar (acute bacterial) Pneumonia.

Definition & organism

🔴 Lobar pneumonia = acute bacterial pneumonia in which consolidation (exudative solidification) involves an entire lobe (or large part of it) uniformly. Most common organism: Streptococcus pneumoniae; occasionally Klebsiella. Spread occurs rapidly across alveoli through pores of Kohn + channels of Lambert.

🔴 The FOUR classic stages (with antibiotics, stages blur)

Stage 1 — CONGESTION (first 24 hours)

  • Gross: lobe heavy, dark red, boggy; cut surface oozing frothy bloody fluid.
  • Microscopic: dilated, engorged alveolar capillaries; intra-alveolar serous (protein-rich) exudate containing MANY bacteria and FEW neutrophils; scattered RBCs.

Stage 2 — RED HEPATIZATION (days 2–4)

  • Gross: lobe red, firm, airless, liver-like consistency ("hepatization"); gray-red dry cut surface; overlying fibrinous pleuritis.
  • Microscopic: alveoli packed with fibrinosuppurative exudate — neutrophils + abundant fibrin + many RBCs; fibrin strands pass between adjacent alveoli through pores of Kohn (rapid spread); capillaries still congested.

Stage 3 — GRAY HEPATIZATION (days 4–6)

  • Gross: lobe grayish-brown, firm, dry, cut surface granular; still consolidated.
  • Microscopic: persistent fibrinosuppurative exudate; RBCs have disintegrated, dense fibrin network packed with neutrophils; capillary congestion compressed → grossly gray.

Stage 4 — RESOLUTION (after day 7, ~1 week onward)

  • Exudate digested enzymatically by neutrophil proteases/macrophages → granular debris resorbed by macrophages, expectorated in sputum, or organized by connective tissue ingrowth.
  • Alveolar architecture restored; capillary congestion subsides; patient afebrile within 48–72 h of effective antibiotics.
  • Pleural fibrinous reaction may organize → pleural adhesions.

Clinical correlate

Abrupt onset high fever with rigors, pleuritic chest pain + friction rub, cough with rusty sputum, neutrophilic leukocytosis; CXR = uniformly radiopaque consolidated lobe with air bronchogram.

🎯 Stages: Congestion → Red hepatization → Gray hepatization → Resolution — "R-R-G-R"; whole-lobe fibrinosuppurative consolidation by pneumococcus.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 15 (Lung), pp. 673–728 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Respiratory System, pp. 88–135

Q8. Write/describe Lab Diagnosis of Lobar Pneumonia.

1. Specimen: Sputum (mainstay)

  • Collection: deep-cough early-morning sample in sterile wide-mouth container before antibiotics; reject saliva-contaminated samples (<10 pus cells/>25 epithelial cells per HPF).
  • Macroscopic: 🔴 rusty-brown sticky sputum (RBC + altered hemoglobin — classical of pneumococcal lobar pneumonia); purulent, tenacious.
  • Microscopic — Gram stain: 🔴 numerous neutrophils + gram-positive, lancet-shaped diplococci (S. pneumoniae); capsule demonstrated by Quellung reaction / India ink.
  • Culture & sensitivity: blood agar (draughtsman/coin colonies, α-hemolytic, optochin-sensitive, bile-soluble) — identifies organism + guides antibiotics.
  • Additional stains: ZN for AFB (exclude TB), PAS/KOH for fungi when indicated.

2. Blood investigations

  • CBC: 🔴 neutrophilic leukocytosis (total count 15–30 × 10⁹/L, left shift; toxic granulation).
  • Blood culture: positive in 20–30% early pneumococcal pneumonia — confirms etiology.
  • CRP markedly ↑; procalcitonin ↑; ESR ↑.
  • ABG: hypoxemia (↓PaO₂), respiratory alkalosis early.
  • Urea/electrolytes — assess severity (CURB-65).

3. Rapid antigen / molecular tests

  • Urinary pneumococcal antigen (immunochromatography) — rapid, stays positive during antibiotics.
  • Multiplex PCR panel for atypicals/viruses (to exclude).

4. Imaging (radiological confirmation)

  • 🔴 CXR: homogeneous dense opacity occupying a whole lobe (lower lobe commonest) with air bronchogram; no shift of mediastinum; mild ipsilateral pleural effusion possible.

5. Pleural fluid analysis (if parapneumonic effusion/empyema)

  • Exudate (Light criteria), turbid, protein >30 g/L, LDH high, glucose low, pH <7.2, neutrophil predominance; Gram stain + culture; pH <7.2 = drain.

6. Follow-up

Repeat CXR after 6 weeks (complete clearance expected — persistent shadow demands exclusion of carcinoma/TB).

🎯 Rusty sputum + gram-positive diplococci + neutrophilic leukocytosis + lobar consolidation with air bronchogram = lab picture of lobar pneumonia.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 15 (Lung), pp. 673–728 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Respiratory System, pp. 88–135 · Unit-II Sputum Examination, pp. 497–498
Q9. Name the types of Pulmonary TB. Discuss the pathogenesis of Primary Pulmonary TB.

Types of pulmonary tuberculosis

TypeFeatures
🔴 1. Primary (childhood) TBFirst infection; non-immune host; subpleural Ghon focus + hilar nodes = Ghon complex; usually asymptomatic; 95% heal
🔴 2. Secondary (post-primary/adult/reactivation) TBReactivation of latent focus (or reinfection); immune host; apical cavitation; the classic "chronic" TB

4. Miliary / disseminated TBHematogenous/lymphatic seeding — millet-seed granulomas in lungs + organs

Pathogenesis of PRIMARY PULMONARY TB (arrow chain)

Inhalation of aerosolized droplet nuclei (1–5 µm, 1–10 bacilli)
→ lodge in distal alveoli of SUBPLEURAL parenchyma
   [lower part of upper lobe / upper part of lower lobe]
→ bacilli phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages
→ VIRULENT strains BLOCK phagosome–lysosome fusion
→ bacilli MULTIPLY inside macrophages (log-phase growth ~3 weeks)
→ macrophage death + release of chemokines
→ recruitment of MORE monocytes/macrophages (they also get infected)
→ antigen presented via MHC-II to naive CD4+ T cells
→ Th1 polarization (IL-12 from macrophages drives IFN-γ production)
→ IFN-γ + TNF activate macrophages (iNOS → NO → kill bacilli)
→ TYPE IV hypersensitivity develops (caseous necrosis appears)
→ GRANULOMA formation contains the infection
→ GHOHN FOCUS: 1–1.5 cm subpleural area of caseating granulomatous inflammation
→ spread via lymphatics to hilar/mediastinal lymph nodes
→ node involvement completes the GHOHN COMPLEX
→ in 95%: cell-mediated immunity CONTAINS bacilli (latent infection,
   focus heals by fibrosis ± calcification)
→ in 5% (children, malnutrition, HIV): progression → progressive primary/
   miliary TB

📌 Key point: tissue-destructive lesions (caseation) appear ONLY after development of specific anti-TB cell-mediated immunity (~3 weeks) — the same immunity that kills bacilli also destroys tissue (hypersensitivity = protective immunity's double edge).

🎯 Primary TB: droplet nucleus → subpleural focus → macrophage multiplication blocked only after Th1/IFN-γ immunity → Ghon focus + hilar nodes = Ghon complex → 95% heal, 5% progress.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 15 (Lung), pp. 673–728 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Respiratory System, pp. 88–135

Part B — 🔴 Most common SITES of primary TB

  1. Subpleural focus in the LOWER PART of the UPPER LOBE or the UPPER PART of the LOWER LOBE (just above or below the interlobar fissure) — well-aerated mid-zone.
  2. Usually the RIGHT lung (more vertical right main bronchus).
  3. Always accompanied by ipsilateral hilar/mediastinal lymph node involvement (part of the Ghon complex).

Part C — Pathogenesis of SECONDARY (post-primary) TB (arrow chain)

ENDOGENOUS reactivation of dormant bacilli from healed primary focus
   (commoner) — or EXOGENOUS reinfection
→ triggered by waning immunity (age, malnutrition, DM, HIV, steroid therapy)
→ lesion arises in APEX / posterior segment of UPPER LOBE
   [highest alveolar PO₂ favours bacillary growth + poor lymphatic drainage]
→ pre-sensitized host → prompt, VIGOROUS Th1-type IV hypersensitivity
→ marked CASEOUS NECROSIS (tissue destruction outpaces containment)
→ liquefaction of caseous center + erosion into bronchi
→ CAVITY formation (communication with airway → cough, sputum, AFB discharge)
→ AEROGENOUS (canaliculate) spread within the same/other lung
→ fibrosis + scarring around lesions; pleural involvement
→ occasional lymphatic/hematogenous spread (miliary TB, esp. immunosuppressed)
🎯 Chronic pneumonia = TB + fungi + actinomyces + aspiration; primary TB site = subpleural mid-zone (lower-upper / upper-lower lobe); secondary = apical reactivation with cavitation.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 15 (Lung), pp. 673–728 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Respiratory System, pp. 88–135

🔴 MILIARY TUBERCULOSIS — definition

Massive lymphohematogenous dissemination of tubercle bacilli producing innumerable tiny (1–2 mm), discrete, yellow-white granulomas resembling MILLET SEEDS ("miliary") in the lungs and other organs.

  • May complicate either primary or secondary TB (especially immunosuppression, pregnancy, childhood).
  • Organs involved: lungs (both fields studded with seed-like nodules), liver, spleen, kidney, bone marrow, meninges, adrenal glands, retina (choroid tubercles).
  • Microscopy: each nodule = granuloma ± central caseation; AFB demonstrable.
  • Clinical: insidious or acute — fever, night sweats, weight loss, dyspnea, tuberculous meningitis is a feared association; CXR = bilateral fine miliary nodularity; often tuberculin test negative (anergy).
🎯 Primary TB: 95% heal to latent calcified Ranke complex, 5% progress locally/lymphatically/hematogenously; miliary TB = blood-borne millet-seed granulomatosis of lungs + organs.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 15 (Lung), pp. 673–728 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Respiratory System, pp. 88–135
Q12. What is Ghon focus and Ghon complex? Mention the difference between Primary TB & Secondary TB.

Definitions

  • 🔴 Ghon focus = the initial 1–1.5 cm subpleural parenchymal lesion of primary TB — area of caseating granulomatous inflammation located in the lower part of the upper lobe / upper part of the lower lobe.
  • 🔴 Ghon complex = Ghon focus + involved regional (hilar/mediastinal) lymph nodes drained by lymphatics of the focus. When healed by fibrosis + calcium deposition it is called the Ranke complex (radiologically detectable calcified complex).

Q13. Give the pathological / morphological features of secondary TB.

Site & general plan

🔴 Apical/posterior segments of one or both upper lobes — combination of caseating granulomas + cavitation + fibrosis; lesion may remain localized for years or smoulder/extend.

Gross morphology

  1. Fibrocaseous lesion(s) — sharply delimited areas of caseous necrosis enveloped by fibrous capsule; variable size.
  2. 🔴 CAVITATION — hallmark: caseous center liquefies and drains into a bronchus leaving an irregular ragged-walled cavity lined by caseous debris (inner layer), granulation tissue (middle) + fibrous collar (outer); cavity communicates with airway → bacilli discharged in sputum (source of spread).
  3. Aerogenous spread — satellite caseating foci in the rest of the same lobe, opposite apex ("adult-type bronchopneumonia"), lower parts of lung in advanced disease.
  4. Pleural involvement — fibrous adhesions, serofibrinous pleurisy, tuberculous empyema.
  5. Healing — fibrous scarring + calcification; retracted scars with traction bronchiectasis; upper-lobe shrinkage.
  6. End-stage — extensive destruction, honeycomb change, secondary amyloidosis (AA).

Microscopic morphology

  • 🔴 Caseating (necrotizing) granulomas: central amorphous granular caseous necrosis (lipids from dead macrophages; acid-fast bacilli demonstrable on ZN stain at the edge of necrosis) → palisaded epithelioid histiocytes → Langhans giant cells (peripheral horseshoe nuclei) → rim of CD4+ lymphocytes → outer fibroblasts + collagen.
  • Cavity wall: inner caseous layer (AFB abundant) → granulation tissue with giant cells → dense fibrosis; thrombosed vessels (source of hemoptysis).
  • Surrounding lung: obstructive lipoid pneumonia, bronchopneumonic spread foci, anthracotic pigmentation.

Complications of secondary TB (mention briefly)

Progressive cavitary disease, tuberculous empyema, hemoptysis (eroded vessel/aspergilloma in old cavity), laryngeal/gut TB (swallowed bacilli), miliary dissemination (immunosuppression), isolated-organ TB (kidney, adrenal, bone, meninges).

🎯 Secondary TB = apical fibrocaseous lesions + cavitation + aerogenous spread; microscope = caseating granuloma with Langhans giant cells.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 15 (Lung), pp. 673–728 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Respiratory System, pp. 88–135

  • Definition: granuloma = focal aggregate of activated (epithelioid) macrophages ± multinucleate giant cells, surrounded by lymphocytes — a type IV (delayed, cell-mediated) hypersensitivity reaction attempting to wall-off indigestible bacilli.
  • Function: contains bacilli (granuloma = both defence AND source of tissue destruction); caseation = hypersensitivity-dependent necrosis.

🔴 TUBERCULIN / MANTOUX TEST

An in-vivo test of delayed-type (type IV) hypersensitivity against M. tuberculosis antigens, used to detect prior INFECTION (latent or active) — NOT proof of active disease.

Procedure:

  1. Inject 0.1 mL of purified protein derivative (PPD — 5 TU) intradermally into volar forearm (wheal raised with beveled 27-gauge needle).
  2. Read at 48–72 hours: measure the INDURATION (hard swelling), NOT erythema, transversely to the long axis of the arm, in millimetres.

Interpretation (induration diameter):

ReadingPositive in
≥5 mmHIV+, close contacts of cases, organ transplant/immunosuppressed (≥15 mg prednisone), fibrotic changes on CXR
≥10 mmRecent immigrants from high-prevalence regions (incl. Bangladesh), IV drug users, diabetics/CKD/silicosis/malignancy/malnutrition, children <4 yrs, staff of high-risk facilities
≥15 mmAll others (low-risk people)

Positive result means: cell-mediated immunity present — past exposure/latent infection/active disease/BCG vaccination (cross-reactivity).

False NEGATIVE (anergy): miliary/extensive TB, HIV/AIDS, sarcoidosis, measles, Hodgkin lymphoma, severe malnutrition, corticosteroid/immunosuppressive therapy, very early infection (<4–8 weeks — window period), improper technique. False POSITIVE: BCG vaccination, infection with environmental atypical mycobacteria.

🎯 Granuloma: caseation → epithelioid cells → Langhans giant cells → CD4 lymphocytes → fibrous rim; Mantoux = 5 TU PPD intradermal, read induration at 48–72 h.

Q15. Describe the lab diagnosis of Pulmonary TB.

1. Specimen collection

🔴 Three consecutive early-morning deep-cough sputum samples (spot-morning-spot); if non-productive — gastric aspirate (children), induced sputum, bronchoscopy with BAL, pleural fluid, tissue biopsy (lymph node, lung).

2. Direct smear microscopy — Ziehl–Neelsen (ZN) stain

  • 🔴 Carbol-fuchsin + heat → acid wash (20% H₂SO₄/alcohol) → methylene blue counterstain → slender, straight/beaded RED acid-fast bacilli on blue background.
  • Fluorescence microscopy (auramine-rhodamine) = more sensitive, faster screening.
  • Sensitivity 50–60% (needs ~5,000–10,000 AFB/mL), specificity high; grading: scanty / 1+ (10–99 AFB)/100 fields / 2+ / 3+.

3. 🔴 Molecular test — GeneXpert MTB/RIF (NAAT)

  • Automated real-time PCR detecting M. tuberculosis DNA + rifampicin resistance (rpoB gene) within ~2 hours; WHO-recommended initial diagnostic test; usable on sputum/tissue; far more sensitive than smear (detects paucibacillary/smear-negative disease).

4. 🔴 Culture — GOLD STANDARD

  • Solid: Löwenstein–Jensen (egg-based) medium — rough, tough, buff-coloured colonies in 3–6 weeks (slow grower; niacin +).
  • Liquid: MGIT/BACTEC automated systems — growth in 1–3 weeks.
  • Enables drug-sensitivity testing (DST) for first/second-line drugs — mandatory for treatment guidance.

5. Immunological tests

  • Tuberculin (Mantoux) test — supportive; cannot separate latent from active disease.
  • IGRA (QuantiFERON-TB Gold, T-SPOT.TB) — interferon-γ release assay on blood; not affected by BCG or most environmental mycobacteria; also cannot differentiate active vs latent.

6. Histopathology (biopsy)

🔴 Caseating granulomas with Langhans giant cells + AFB on ZN stain — diagnostic in tissue (lymph node, pleura, lung).

7. Supporting investigations

  • CBC: relative lymphocytosis; ESR markedly elevated; anemia of chronic disease.
  • CXR/CT: 🔴 upper-zone patchy infiltrates ± cavitation, fibrosis, hilar calcified (Ranke) nodes; miliary nodularity if disseminated.
  • Pleural fluid (if effusion): exudate, lymphocyte-predominant, protein high, glucose low, ADA (adenosine deaminase) >40 U/L supports TB; AFB/culture/NAAT on fluid; pleural biopsy highest yield.
  • HIV testing in every confirmed case.
🎯 Diagnosis ladder: ZN smear (fast, cheap) → GeneXpert MTB/RIF (rapid + resistance) → LJ/MGIT culture (gold standard + DST); histology = caseating granuloma.

Q16. Short Note: BCG Vaccine, Tuberculin test, Lung Abscess, ARDS, Acute lung injury, Pulmonary edema.

(a) BCG Vaccine

🔴 Live attenuated Mycobacterium bovis (Bacille Calmette–Guérin) administered intradermally, usually at birth (Bangladesh EPI schedule).

  • Protection: high (~70–80%) against miliary TB & tuberculous meningitis in children; variable (0–80%) against adult pulmonary TB.
  • Effect: induces cell-mediated immunity; makes tuberculin test positive (limits Mantoux utility).
  • Local reaction: papule → small ulcer → heaped scar (upper arm); complications — regional suppurative lymphadenitis; disseminated BCGitis in severely immunodeficient (SCID) — contraindication.
  • 🎯 BCG = live attenuated M. bovis, ID at birth, protects children from miliary/TBM, turns tuberculin positive.

(b) Tuberculin Test

See Q14 for details — 🔴 intradermal 5 TU PPD; induration read at 48–72 h; measures type IV hypersensitivity = prior infection (not necessarily disease); false-negative in anergy (miliary TB, HIV, sarcoidosis, steroids); false-positive after BCG. 🎯 PPD skin test — induration at 48–72 h detects infection, not disease.

(c) Lung Abscess

🔴 Localized suppurative necrosis of lung parenchyma forming a cavity.

  • Mechanisms: 🔴 aspiration of infective material (most frequent) — suppressed cough reflex (alcohol, coma, anesthesia, seizures), poor dentition → right lung, single; antecedent necrotizing pneumonia (S. aureus, Klebsiella, pneumococcus type 3); septic emboli (right-sided endocarditis — multiple); bronchial obstruction by tumor (rule out carcinoma in 10–15%); direct extension.
  • Organisms: 🔴 anaerobes (Bacteroides, Fusobacterium, Peptococcus) sole isolates in ~60%; mixed aerobes/anaerobes.
  • Morphology: cavity up to 5–6 cm; suppurative destruction, central cavitation, air-fluid level; chronic → thick fibrous wall; gangrene if extensive.
  • Clinical: 🔴 cough with copious foul-smelling purulent/sanguineous sputum, fever, weight loss, clubbing; complications — empyema, hemorrhage, brain abscess.
  • 🎯 Aspiration → right-sided foul sputum abscess; always exclude underlying cancer.

(d) ARDS (Adult/Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome)

🔴 Severe form of acute lung injury causing refractory hypoxemic respiratory failure; histologic substrate = DIFFUSE ALVEOLAR DAMAGE.

  • Triggers (>50%): 🔴 sepsis, diffuse pulmonary infection, gastric aspiration, mechanical trauma; also burns, pancreatitis, TRALI, near-drowning, drugs.
  • Morphology: exudative stage — heavy red boggy lungs with 🔴 hyaline membranes (fibrin-rich edema + necrotic epithelial remnants lining alveoli) → proliferative (type II hyperplasia) → fibrotic stages.
  • Clinical: profound dyspnea, tachypnea, cyanosis, diffuse bilateral infiltrates; hypoxemia refractory to O₂; stiff lungs; mortality 35–46% (death usually from sepsis/multiorgan failure).
  • 🎯 ARDS = hyaline membranes + refractory hypoxemia after sepsis/aspiration/trauma.

(e) Acute Lung Injury (ALI)

🔴 Abrupt-onset respiratory insufficiency with bilateral pulmonary infiltrates and hypoxemia WITHOUT cardiac (hydrostatic) failure — ARDS is its most severe end (severity graded by PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio).

  • Same spectrum, same triggers, same pathology (diffuse alveolar damage) as ARDS; milder degree.
  • Pathogenesis: endothelial/pneumocyte injury → neutrophil-mediated damage (ROS, proteases, NETs) → leaky capillaries → alveolar edema + surfactant loss.
  • 🎯 ALI = mild end of the DAD spectrum; ARDS = severe end.

(f) Pulmonary Edema

🔴 Abnormal accumulation of fluid in lung interstitium and alveoli — see Q1.

  • Hemodynamic (transudate): left heart failure (most common), hypoalbuminemia, lymphatic obstruction → dependent basal edema; chronic → heart failure cells + brown induration.
  • Microvascular injury (exudate): pneumonia, aspiration, sepsis, toxins, high altitude, neurogenic → protein-rich fluid, hyaline membranes if severe.
  • Clinically: dyspnea, orthopnea, crackles; heavy wet lungs.
  • 🎯 Edema = cardiogenic transudate vs injured-capillary exudate.
📖 Robbins 10e, Ch 15 (Lung), pp. 673–728 · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-I Respiratory System, pp. 88–135
Q17 (PBQ). A 78 year old man is brought to the emergency department. For the last several days he has fatigue, high fever & productive cough. A chest radiograph shows consolidation in right lower lobe. A gram stain of sputum shows a predominance of gram positive diplococci. What is the most likely diagnosis?

🔴 Diagnosis

ACUTE BACTERIAL (LOBAR) PNEUMONIA due to Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcal pneumonia) — right lower lobe.

  4. Repeat CXR after treatment (expect clearance; persistent shadow → exclude TB/carcinoma).

Q18 (PBQ). A 35 years old Farmer presented with Productive Cough, Occasional hemoptysis, low grade fever at night for 6 months. Chest X-Ray shows multiple patchy opacities at right upper lung with right sided mild pleural effusion. What is your diagnosis? How will you confirm the diagnosis?

🔴 Diagnosis

PULMONARY TUBERCULOSIS (secondary/post-primary type, right upper zone) with right-sided tuberculous pleural effusion.

Justification

FindingInterpretation
Young adult (35 yr)Peak age for secondary TB
🔴 Chronic symptoms >6 months — productive cough, occasional hemoptysisClassic chronic TB triad (cough + hemoptysis + constitutional symptoms)

FarmerOccupational exposure/poor socioeconomic setting raises pretest probability (also consider endemic fungi abroad, but in Bangladesh TB is overwhelmingly most likely)

How to CONFIRM the diagnosis

  1. 🔴 Sputum for AFB — Ziehl–Neelsen stain ×3 consecutive early-morning samples (red acid-fast bacilli) — corner-stone bedside confirmation; grade the smear (1+/2+/3+).
  2. 🔴 GeneXpert MTB/RIF (NAAT) on sputum — confirms M. tuberculosis DNA + screens rifampicin resistance within hours (WHO first-choice).
  3. Sputum culture — Löwenstein–Jensen (buff rough colonies, 3–6 wk) or MGIT liquid medium (gold standard) + drug-sensitivity testing.
  4. Pleural fluid analysis — exudate, straw-coloured, lymphocyte-predominant, protein >30 g/L, glucose low, 🔴 ADA >40 U/L; send AFB smear/culture/NAAT; pleural biopsy (caseating granuloma = highest yield).
  5. Supportive: Mantoux test (≥10 mm in our setting), ESR ↑, relative lymphocytosis, anemia; HRCT chest (patchy apical infiltrates, cavitation, tree-in-bud); HIV test.
  6. Response to empirical anti-tubercular chemotherapy (ATT) — clinical-radiological improvement supports diagnosis where microbiology negative.
🎯 Young adult + 6-month cough + hemoptysis + night fever + apical patches ± effusion = secondary pulmonary TB; confirm by sputum AFB ×3 + GeneXpert + culture (± ADA/pleural biopsy).
