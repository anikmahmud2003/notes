Q1. What is biopsy and autopsy? How histopathology helps the autopsy procedure?

Definitions

  • 🔴 Biopsy = removal of a small piece of tissue from a LIVING patient for diagnostic examination (e.g., tumour typing/grading, confirming inflammation, transplant surveillance).
  • 🔴 Autopsy (post-mortem examination / necropsy) = systematic examination of a DEAD body to determine the cause of death, document the extent of disease, and assess the quality of medical care.

Types of autopsy

  • Clinical/academic (hospital): done with consent; goals = confirm diagnosis, quality assurance, teaching, research.
  • Medicolegal/forensic: ordered by the coroner/authority in unnatural, suspicious or sudden deaths; answers legal questions (natural vs unnatural death, injury dating).
  • Complete vs limited — e.g., organ-restricted examination (heart/brain only).

How histopathology helps the autopsy

  1. Confirms the gross (macroscopic) diagnosis and corrects discrepancies between clinical and post-mortem findings.
  2. Identifies the cause of death when gross findings are inconclusive — e.g., 🔴 early myocardial infarction is grossly invisible for hours but shows coagulation necrosis microscopically within ~4–6 h; also pneumonia, myocarditis, sepsis, pulmonary embolism.
  3. Detects clinically silent disease — occult tumours, incidental findings (small pulmonary emboli).
  4. Documents disease extent — staging, spread and grading.
  5. Negative (exclusionary) findings — ruling out suspected infection or neoplasm.
  6. Quality assurance/audit — comparing antemortem vs post-mortem diagnoses; monitoring therapy.
  7. Medicolegal — establishing natural vs unnatural death and dating injuries (age of wound by histology).
🎯 "Autopsy = external → internal gross exam + sampling → histopathology. Microscopy is the final arbiter of the autopsy's conclusions."
📖 Bancroft & Gamble, Theory and Practice of Histological Techniques, 8th ed. — fixation, processing, staining chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499
Q2. Define & classify biopsy. Write shortly on core biopsy.

Definition

Biopsy = removal of tissue from a living patient for diagnostic (microscopic) examination. The technique is chosen by the site, size and nature of the lesion and by how much tissue is needed.

Classification

TypeDescription & use
Incisional biopsyExcision of a portion of a large lesion (breast lump, soft-tissue mass)
Excisional biopsyRemoval of the entire lesion (small skin nodule, lymph node) — diagnosis + treatment
Core needle biopsyWide-bore needle (14–18 G) obtains a core of tissue preserving architecture
Fine needle aspiration (FNA/FNAC)Thin needle (22–25 G); obtains cells, not tissue — cytology
Punch biopsyCircular blade cuts a full-thickness disc — skin
Shave biopsySuperficial skin lesions (naevi, polyps)
Endoscopic biopsyForceps through an endoscope — GI, bronchus, bladder
Frozen sectionRapid intraoperative diagnosis (fresh tissue, cryostat)
SpecialBone-marrow trephine/aspiration; cone biopsy (cervix); curettage (endometrium)

Core biopsy (shortly)

  • Wide-bore needle (14–18 G, spring-loaded or vacuum-assisted) takes a tissue core that preserves architecture — stroma + glandular pattern retained (unlike FNAC).
  • Uses: prostate (TRUS-guided, often 12 cores), breast, liver, kidney, lung, soft tissue — the standard for most solid-organ diagnosis.
  • Advantages: allows grading and assessment of invasion; provides tissue for IHC, special stains, molecular tests; fewer false negatives than FNAC; image-guided, local anaesthesia.
  • Risks: bleeding, infection, rarely pneumothorax or needle-tract seeding — higher than FNAC, much lower than open biopsy.
🎯 "FNAC = cells (screening); core biopsy = tissue/architecture (grading, invasion, IHC)."
📖 Bancroft & Gamble, Theory and Practice of Histological Techniques, 8th ed. — fixation, processing, staining chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499
Q3. State the steps in histopathology. 
Fixation → Dehydration → Clearing → Wax impregnation → Embedding → Sectioning → Staining → Mounting → Microscopy & report
  1. Fixation — preserve tissue, prevent autolysis/putrefaction, harden it (10% neutral buffered formalin, ~24 h).
  2. Dehydration — remove water with ascending grades of alcohol (70% → 95% → 100% ethanol); water will not mix with paraffin.
  3. Clearing — replace alcohol with a clearing agent miscible with wax (xylene); the tissue becomes translucent.
  4. Wax impregnation — infiltrate with molten paraffin wax (44–60 °C oven).
  5. Embedding — orient the tissue in a wax block that sets hard for cutting.
  6. Sectioning — the microtome cuts ribbons of 3–5 µm sections floated onto slides.
  7. Staining — routine H&E (or special stains).
  8. Mounting — coverslip with a resinous mountant (DPX) to protect the section.
  9. Reporting — microscopic examination + a written histopathology report.
🎯 "Water and paraffin are immiscible → fix → alcohol → xylene → wax. Skipping a step = poor penetration and cutting artifacts."
📖 Bancroft & Gamble, Theory and Practice of Histological Techniques, 8th ed. — fixation, processing, staining chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499
Q4. What is Frozen Section? Write its indication, use, limitation.

Definition

🔴 A frozen section is a rapid tissue diagnosis made WHILE THE PATIENT IS STILL ON THE OPERATING TABLE by freezing the fresh tissue instead of the multi-day paraffin process.

  • Principle: fresh (unfixed) tissue is frozen (cryostat at −20 °C), cut into thin sections, stained with quick H&E / toluidine blue → diagnosis in ~10–20 minutes.

Indications

  1. Margin assessment — breast lumpectomy, skin (BCC/SCC), soft-tissue sarcoma (is the excision clear?).
  2. Lymph node staging — sentinel lymph node for metastasis (breast, melanoma) → decide node dissection.
  3. Tissue identification — confirming parathyroid vs lymph node vs fat during neck/thyroid surgery.
  4. Determining specimen adequacy — is there enough diagnostic tissue?

Uses

Guides the surgical decision in the same sitting and avoids a second operation.

Limitations

  • 🔴 Ice-crystal artifacts distort cell morphology (bubbly cytoplasm, crushed cells).
  • Thicker sections + poorer morphology than paraffin — subtle lesions hard to grade.
  • Sampling error — only a small portion examined.
  • Limited special stains/IHC; tissue not permanently preserved.
⚠️ The frozen-section report is provisional — permanent paraffin sections remain the gold standard for the final diagnosis.
🎯 "Frozen = fresh tissue, cryostat (−20 °C), ~10–20 min, ON the operating table — for margins/sentinel node/tissue ID; ice-crystal artefact is the price; paraffin gives the FINAL word."
📖 Bancroft & Gamble, Theory and Practice of Histological Techniques, 8th ed. — fixation, processing, staining chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499
Q5. What is tissue preservatives? Name some preservatives & aims. Mention steps of tissue processing.

Definition

Tissue preservatives = agents that keep tissue from deteriorating after removal, preventing autolysis (self-digestion by the tissue's own enzymes) and putrefaction (decay by bacteria), so the sample remains fit for study.

Common preservatives

  • 10% neutral buffered formalin — the most common (both preservative and fixative).
  • Alcohol (ethanol/methanol) — cytology smears (wet fixation).
  • Glutaraldehyde — fixative of choice for electron microscopy.
  • Cold (4 °C) / freezing (−20/−80 °C) — short-term preservation without chemical alteration.
  • Compound fixatives — Bouin's, Carnoy's, Zenker's (specific structures/enzymes).

Aims of preservation

  1. Prevent autolysis and putrefaction from the moment of removal.
  2. Preserve morphology close to the living state.
  3. Keep tissue suitable for later processing and staining.

Steps of tissue processing

Fixation → Dehydration (ascending alcohol) → Clearing (xylene) → Wax impregnation
  → Embedding → Sectioning (microtome) → Staining → Mounting
  • Dehydration removes water (ascending 70% → 100% alcohol); clearing removes alcohol (xylene) and makes tissue miscible with wax; impregnation fills the tissue with molten paraffin; embedding creates a firm block; sectioning cuts 3–5 µm slices; staining (H&E) makes structures visible; mounting preserves the section under a coverslip.
🎯 "Preserve = STOP autolysis/putrefaction (formalin = routine, alcohol = smears, glutaraldehyde = EM, cold = fresh); process = fix → alcohol → xylene → wax → block → cut → stain → mount."
📖 Bancroft & Gamble, Theory and Practice of Histological Techniques, 8th ed. — fixation, processing, staining chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499
Q6. What is fixatives? Mention aim of fixation. Classification of fixatives.

Definition

Fixative = a chemical (or physical) agent used to FIX (preserve) tissue so that morphology is retained close to the living state and the tissue is hardened for sectioning. Fixation is the FIRST and MOST IMPORTANT step of tissue handling.

Aims of fixation

  1. 🔴 Preserve cells/tissue close to the living state (stop all ongoing cellular activity).
  2. 🔴 Prevent autolysis (enzymatic self-digestion) and putrefaction (bacterial decay).
  3. Harden the tissue so thin sections (3–5 µm) can be cut.
  4. Permit staining — cross-linking/denaturing proteins lets dyes bind and produce contrast.
  5. Preserve antigens/nucleic acids for IHC/molecular tests (in compatible fixatives).

Classification with examples

ClassExamplesNotes
Simple10% formalin, glutaraldehyde, alcohol, acetoneSingle chemical
Compound/mixturesBouin's, Zenker's, Carnoy's, formol-salineCombine fixatives + additives (picric acid, mercury)
MicroanatomicalFormaldehyde, glutaraldehyde, osmium tetroxidePreserve fine structure (EM = glutaraldehyde + osmium)
CytologicalAlcohol (95% ethanol), acetonePreserve individual cells (cytology smears)
HistochemicalBouin's, Carnoy'sPreserve carbohydrates, enzymes for special staining
ImmunohistochemicalFormalin (neutral buffered)Preserve antigens for IHC

Why 10% neutral buffered formalin dominates

  • Preserves antigens for IHC, is compatible with routine H&E, and penetrates well.
  • "Neutral buffered" (pH 7) prevents formalin pigment (acid hematin) — the commonest artefact of poor fixation.
🎯 "Most common fixative = 10% neutral buffered formalin; glutaraldehyde = EM; alcohol = cytology."
📖 Bancroft & Gamble, Theory and Practice of Histological Techniques, 8th ed. — fixation, processing, staining chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499
Q7. Mention the steps of tissue processing. Why tissue must undergo processing for histopathology?

Steps (in order)

Fixation → Dehydration → Clearing → Wax impregnation → Embedding → Sectioning → Staining → Mounting
  • Fixation — formalin preserves & hardens (stops autolysis).
  • Dehydration — ascending alcohols (70% → 100%) remove all water (water and wax do not mix).
  • Clearing — xylene replaces alcohol; tissue becomes translucent and miscible with molten wax.
  • Wax impregnation — molten paraffin infiltrates the tissue (44–60 °C oven).
  • Embedding — tissue oriented in a wax block that sets hard.
  • Sectioning — the microtome cuts ribbons of 3–5 µm sections onto slides.
  • Staining + Mounting — H&E (or special stains), then a coverslip for permanence.

Why tissue must undergo processing

  1. 🔴 Fresh tissue is soft, watery and colourless — it would crush under the microtome blade and show no contrast.
  2. Processing replaces water with paraffin wax, producing a firm block that supports the tissue during slicing.
  3. Wax acts as the support medium — architecture is preserved while cutting.
  4. It enables staining (fixed, sectioned tissue takes up dyes) and gives permanent, archivable slides for later review.
🎯 "Fixative cross-links proteins → alcohol removes water → xylene removes alcohol → wax replaces xylene → solid cuttable block."
📖 Bancroft & Gamble, Theory and Practice of Histological Techniques, 8th ed. — fixation, processing, staining chapters · 📗 Arif 15e (2025), Vol-2, Unit-II Miscellaneous, p. 499
Q8. What are the staining methods used in histopathology? What is the purpose of staining?

Purpose of staining

  • Fixed tissue sections are colourless and show no contrast — stains make components visible under the microscope.
  • Dyes selectively bind different structures → identify morphology, differentiate cell types, and help make a diagnosis (e.g., the cell of origin of a tumour).

Routine stain — H&E

  • 🔴 Hematoxylin (basic dye) → stains nuclei blue (binds acidic DNA/RNA).
  • 🔴 Eosin (acidic dye) → stains cytoplasm & extracellular proteins pink.
  • Together → the familiar blue-pink contrast that is the basis of routine histopathology.

Common special stains

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Antibody-based staining for specific proteins/antigens (e.g., CK, CD markers, HER2, p53) — essential for tumour classification, lineage, and predictive markers.
