Acute hepatitis — causes & LFT findings

Acute hepatitis means inflammation of the liver lasting less than 6 months. It is usually a self-limited illness — the immune system clears the cause and the liver heals completely, though a small proportion progress to chronic disease or (rarely) fulminant failure.

Causes — viral (the commonest group): the hepatotropic viruses HAV, HBV (± HDV), HCV and HEV are the main culprits. Non-hepatotropic viruses can also cause acute hepatitis as part of a systemic infection — CMV, EBV, HSV, VZV, yellow fever, rubella, parvovirus B19, adenovirus. In Bangladesh the commonest acute viral hepatitis is HEV (water-borne).

Causes — non-viral:

  • Drugs/toxins: paracetamol (acetaminophen) — the classic one to quote — plus isoniazid, halothane, methyldopa, alcohol, herbal toxins.
  • Autoimmune hepatitis.
  • Metabolic: Wilson disease, acute fatty liver of pregnancy.
  • Ischemic/shock liver (low hepatic blood flow) and Budd-Chiari (hepatic vein obstruction).

Positive LFT findings in acute hepatitis: the hallmark is ALT & AST markedly raised (often >500 U/L) and crucially ALT > AST — because ALT is much more liver-specific than AST. Bilirubin rises in both conjugated and unconjugated fractions (mixed), and the urine becomes dark with raised urobilinogen. ALP is usually normal or only mildly raised (unlike obstruction), and PT may be prolonged if the hepatitis is severe.

Mechanism in brief: viruses infect hepatocytes directly (HAV/HEV reach the liver via the portal vein after entering through the gut; HBV/HCV/HDV enter through blood/needles/sex) and then the immune attack kills infected cells. As hepatocytes die, their cytoplasmic enzymes (ALT, AST) leak into the blood — more cell death = higher transaminases. Paracetamol damages the liver through its toxic metabolite NAPQI, which depletes glutathione and causes centrilobular (zone 3) necrosis; alcohol damages via acetaldehyde and oxidative stress. Autoimmune hepatitis occurs when cytotoxic T-cells attack hepatocytes carrying self-antigens (ANA, SMA, LKM-1).

Sources: Robbins & Cotran PBD ch.18; Distribution of Liver Disease in Bangladesh (PMC); Merck Manual

Viral markers of hepatitis & their diagnostic significance

Viral markers are the viral proteins (antigens) and the antibodies we produce against them that can be detected in blood. Their diagnostic significance is that they tell us three things: who is infected, who is immune, and how active/recent the infection is.

HBV markers — the ones you must know:

  • HBsAg (surface antigen) = currently infected — it is the first marker to appear and flags any acute or chronic infection.
  • Anti-HBs = immune — a protective neutralizing antibody present only after recovery or vaccination.
  • IgM anti-HBc (core antibody) = recent / acute infection — it is the window-period proof and the marker that saves the blood bank from missing a silent donor.
  • HBeAg = active replication = the patient is highly infectious (especially mother-to-child transmission).
  • Total anti-HBc = past exposure — appears in all HBV infections and stays positive for life.
  • HBV DNA = the actual viral load, used for diagnosis, prognosis and monitoring therapy.

Other viruses:

  • HAV: IgM anti-HAV (acute) vs IgG anti-HAV (immunity).
  • HCV: anti-HCV (only shows exposure) + HCV RNA (proves active disease). Anti-HCV is not protective immunity.
  • HDV: anti-HDV — only tested when HBsAg is positive (HDV needs HBV).
  • HEV: IgM anti-HEV (acute).

Mechanism in brief: HBsAg spills into blood whenever the virus replicates, so its presence equals ongoing infection. Anti-HBs only persists when the virus is gone (recovery) or after vaccination, so it equals protective immunity. The core antibody (anti-HBc) is made in every HBV infection, and because the IgM class appears first (within weeks) and falls within ~6 months, IgM anti-HBc marks recent/acute infection while IgG anti-HBc marks past exposure. HBeAg is secreted only during active replication, so HBeAg-positive patients have a high viral load and are highly infectious. HBV DNA measures the viral genome directly — the true viral load.

Sources: CDC — Clinical Testing & Diagnosis for Hepatitis B; Merck Manual Hep B serology table; Robbins ch.18

Viruses causing acute liver failure + clinical features of liver failure

Acute liver failure (ALF) is sudden, severe liver dysfunction occurring in < 6 months in a person with a previously healthy liver, defined by the combination of coagulopathy (INR ≥ 1.5) + encephalopathy.

Viruses commonly causing ALF:

  • HEV — especially in pregnancy, where ~20% die (fulminant disease).
  • HBV — can produce fulminant hepatitis, typically from an over-exuberant immune clearance storm.
  • Rarely HAV, HSV, CMV, EBV (HSV/CMV/EBV mainly in the immunosuppressed).

In Bangladesh and the developing world viral hepatitis is the top cause of ALF; in the West it is paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose.

Clinical features of liver failure — quote the full list:

  • Coagulopathy — easy bruising/bleeding, prolonged INR/PT, because the liver cannot make clotting factors.
  • Hepatic encephalopathy — confusion progressing to coma, because ammonia and toxins are not cleared.
  • Jaundice — bilirubin is not conjugated/excreted.
  • Hypoglycemia — hepatic gluconeogenesis stops.
  • Edema / ascites — albumin synthesis falls (low oncotic pressure).
  • Hepatorenal syndrome — functional kidney failure secondary to splanchnic vasodilation.

Mechanism in brief: some viruses trigger an overwhelming immune attack or massive direct cytopathic effect that kills hepatocytes faster than the liver can regenerate — HEV is notorious in pregnancy (immune/hormonal changes), HBV can cause a fulminant clearance storm. Coagulopathy arises because the liver makes most clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X, fibrinogen). Encephalopathy arises because the failing liver cannot convert ammonia to urea, so ammonia reaches the brain and poisons it. The definition needs both coagulopathy and encephalopathy — and if the patient already had cirrhosis it is called acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF), not ALF.

Sources: Merck Manual — Acute Liver Failure; Comprehensive Review of ALF (PMC); Robbins ch.18

Viruses causing chronic hepatitis + laboratory confirmation

Chronic hepatitis is inflammation of the liver continuing for more than 6 months. The two viruses that matter are HBV and HCV:

  • HBV becomes chronic in 5–10% of adults but in ~90% of infected newborns (immune tolerance).
  • HCV becomes chronic in 80–90% of those infected.
  • HDV can also cause chronic hepatitis, but only in a person already infected with HBV.

HAV and HEV never become chronic — that is a favourite exam point.

Why they persist: in children/newborns the immune system 'tolerates' HBV and does not attack it, so the virus stays. HCV mutates constantly (producing 'quasispecies') and hides inside hepatocytes, so the immune system can never fully eliminate it.

Laboratory confirmation:

  • Chronic HBV: HBsAg positive for > 6 months — a single positive HBsAg is NOT enough; it must persist.
  • Chronic HCV: anti-HCV positive AND HCV RNA positive for > 6 months.
  • Persistently raised ALT.
  • Liver biopsy / FibroScan (elastography) — shows chronic inflammation and fibrosis, staged F0–F4.

Mechanism in brief: in infants, the immune system recognizes HBV as 'self' (immune tolerance) so there is no attack and the virus persists; in adults the response is usually strong enough to clear it (~90%), but ~5–10% have a weak/incomplete response that damages the liver without clearing the virus. HCV escapes because it mutates so fast that the immune system keeps chasing new variants while the infection persists. HDV survives only as long as HBV provides its envelope (HBsAg).

Sources: Robbins ch.18; CDC Hep B testing; WHO Guidelines on Hepatitis B & C testing

Causes of chronic hepatitis & its fates/outcomes

Chronic hepatitis is liver inflammation lasting >6 months, caused by anything that either keeps injuring the liver or evades immune clearance.

Causes:

  • Viral: HBV, HCV (the main ones), HDV with HBV.
  • Drugs / alcohol: alcohol, methyldopa, isoniazid.
  • Autoimmune hepatitis.
  • Metabolic: NASH/NAFLD, Wilson disease, hemochromatosis, α1-antitrypsin deficiency.
  • Biliary: primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC).

Fates / outcomes of chronic hepatitis — the VIVA must:

  • Resolution — if the cause is removed (e.g., alcohol stopped, virus cured).
  • Carrier state — the virus persists but with little active damage.
  • Cirrhosis — progressive fibrosis is the main danger.
  • Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).
  • Liver failure / decompensation — when function is finally lost.

Remember the loop: injure → inflame → scar → cirrhosis → cancer.

Mechanism in brief: any agent that keeps injuring hepatocytes or evades immune clearance produces chronic disease. The key point: HCV mostly causes cirrhosis and HCC through chronic inflammation, whereas HBV can cause HCC even without cirrhosis because its DNA integrates into the host genome and drives oncogenesis directly. Progressive fibrosis happens because repeated injury activates hepatic stellate cells, which lay down collagen (scar); when that becomes extensive and nodular, the liver is cirrhotic and at risk of cancer and decompensation.

Sources: Robbins ch.18; HBV — leading cause of cirrhosis in Bangladesh

Liver function tests & their changes in obstructive jaundice

Liver function tests (LFT) are a group of blood tests that measure different liver jobs — cell injury, bile flow, protein synthesis and bilirubin handling. Learn them in four groups:

  • Injury markers: ALT, AST — leak into blood when hepatocytes die.
  • Cholestasis markers: ALP, GGT — rise when bile cannot flow.
  • Synthetic function: albumin ↓, PT/INR ↑ — made by the liver.
  • Bilirubin: total, direct (conjugated), indirect (unconjugated).

Changes in obstructive jaundice — the VIVA must:

  • ALP & GGT markedly raised — the hallmark of obstruction.
  • Conjugated (direct) bilirubin raised → dark urine, pale/clay stool.
  • ALT/AST only mildly raised (the hepatocytes are not primarily damaged).
  • PT prolonged but corrected by vitamin K — because bile cannot reach the gut, fat-soluble vitamin K is not absorbed, so factors II, VII, IX, X fall; giving vitamin K restores them.

Mechanism in brief: when bile cannot pass, back-pressure and bile-acid accumulation make the liver produce more ALP and GGT and regurgitate them into blood (GGT confirms the ALP is coming from liver, not bone). Conjugated bilirubin cannot drain through the blocked duct, so it refluxes into blood and is filtered by the kidney (dark urine). The crucial discriminator: in obstruction vitamin K corrects the PT, but in hepatocellular failure it does not — because there the liver simply cannot synthesize the factors at all.

Sources: Robbins ch.18; UpToDate — Liver chemistry tests; Merck Manual

Jaundice — pathological definition & classification

Jaundice (icterus) is the yellow discoloration of the skin, sclera and mucosa caused by a raised serum bilirubin (> 2 mg/dL). The sclera stains first because it is rich in elastin, which has high affinity for bilirubin.

Pathological classification — by the site of the defect:

  • Pre-hepatic — too much bilirubin is produced (hemolysis) → unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin ↑.
  • Hepatic — the liver cannot process bilirubin (viral, drugs, autoimmune, cirrhosis) → mixed (both fractions).
  • Post-hepatic — the bile duct is blocked (stone, tumour) → conjugated (direct) bilirubin ↑.

Why this classification: bilirubin metabolism has three steps — production (pre-hepatic), processing (hepatic: uptake, conjugation, excretion) and excretion (post-hepatic through the ducts). Each site of defect produces its own type of jaundice, so the classification points directly to the cause.

Mechanism in brief: bilirubin is a yellow pigment, so when the serum level exceeds ~2 mg/dL it deposits in the sclera and skin, which have a high affinity for it. Pre-hepatic = overproduction that overwhelms the liver; hepatic = the hepatocyte itself fails at uptake/conjugation/excretion; post-hepatic = conjugated bilirubin cannot drain because the duct is blocked. Jaundice is only clinically visible above about 2 mg/dL.

Sources: Robbins ch.18; Merck Manual — Jaundice

Neonatal jaundice — causes & pathogenesis

Neonatal jaundice is yellow discoloration in a newborn from high bilirubin. It is physiological in most babies (60–80%) and safe if it stays within limits.

Why physiological jaundice happens — the 'triple whammy':

  • High bilirubin load — newborns have many short-lived RBCs being destroyed.
  • Immature UGT1A1 enzyme — conjugation is slow in the first week of life.
  • High enterohepatic circulation — there is no gut flora and β-glucuronidase re-absorbs bilirubin instead of letting it be excreted.

Physiological jaundice is unconjugated, peaks around day 2–5 and resolves by ~week 1.

Pathological causes:

  • Hemolytic: ABO/Rh incompatibility, G6PD deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis.
  • Infection: sepsis, UTI, congenital infections.
  • Biliary atresia → conjugated jaundice with pale stools (surgical emergency).
  • Breast-milk jaundice (separate card).
  • Metabolic: Crigler–Najjar syndrome, hypothyroidism, galactosemia.

The danger: severe unconjugated bilirubin is lipid-soluble and crosses the immature blood-brain barrier → kernicterus (bilirubin stains the basal ganglia → brain damage). Prevent/treat with phototherapy.

Mechanism in brief: physiological jaundice combines a high bilirubin load with immature conjugation and re-absorption of bilirubin from the gut. Hemolytic causes (ABO/Rh, G6PD) destroy RBCs massively, far exceeding the immature liver's capacity. Biliary atresia blocks drainage so conjugated bilirubin rises — the key warning sign is pale stools. Phototherapy works because light converts bilirubin into water-soluble isomers that can be excreted without conjugation.

Sources: StatPearls — Neonatal Jaundice; Merck Manual — Neonatal Hyperbilirubinemia

Differentiating jaundice types without blood tests

Some features are visible with the naked eye — they come directly from how bilirubin behaves in the body. Four clinical points separate the types:

  • Urine color: hemolytic = pale (no bile in urine) · obstructive = dark tea-colored (bile present).
  • Stool color: hemolytic = normal/dark · obstructive = clay/pale.
  • Itching (pruritus): only in obstructive jaundice — bile salts deposit in the skin.
  • Urine urobilinogen: hemolytic = increased · obstructive = decreased/absent.

Why: unconjugated bilirubin is lipid-soluble, so the kidney does not filter it and urine stays pale (acholuric). Conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble, so it is filtered and makes urine dark (choluric). When no bile reaches the gut, no stercobilin is formed (pale stool) and no urobilinogen can be made.

Mechanism in brief: in hemolysis, excess unconjugated bilirubin is produced but never appears in urine (not water-soluble), while more bilirubin reaches the gut and is converted to stercobilin and urobilinogen — hence dark stool, high urobilinogen. In obstruction, bile is dammed behind the block: conjugated bilirubin refluxes into blood and urine (dark urine), no bilirubin reaches the gut (pale stool), and bile salts accumulating in the skin cause itching. Exam trap: obstructive jaundice has choluria (bile in urine) but no urobilinogen; hemolytic has the exact opposite.

Sources: Robbins ch.18; Ghai Pediatrics (neonatal clinical signs); Merck Manual

Pre-hepatic jaundice — causes & LFT diagnosis

Pre-hepatic jaundice is jaundice caused by too much bilirubin production (hemolysis) — the liver is normal but is overwhelmed before it can handle the bilirubin load.

Causes:

  • Hereditary spherocytosis, G6PD deficiency.
  • Sickle cell disease, thalassemia.
  • Autoimmune hemolytic anemia, mismatched transfusion, malaria.
  • Ineffective erythropoiesis (B12/folate deficiency) and reabsorption of a large hematoma.

How LFT diagnoses pre-hepatic jaundice:

  • Unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin raised — the key finding.
  • Urine bilirubin: absent — unconjugated bilirubin is not water-soluble.
  • Urine urobilinogen: increased — more bilirubin reaches the gut.
  • ALT, AST, ALP all normal — the liver itself is fine.
  • Supporting evidence of hemolysis: reticulocytosis ↑, haptoglobin ↓, LDH ↑.

Mechanism in brief: RBCs are destroyed faster than normal, producing excess unconjugated bilirubin that the liver cannot conjugate fast enough, so the level rises before the liver (hence 'pre-hepatic'). Because the liver is healthy, all liver enzymes are normal — only the indirect bilirubin is high. The hemolysis markers (reticulocytosis, low haptoglobin, high LDH) confirm that the problem is red-cell destruction rather than liver disease.

Sources: Robbins ch.18; Merck Manual; UpToDate — Diagnostic approach to jaundice

LFT changes in hepatocellular jaundice

Hepatocellular jaundice is jaundice caused by damage to the hepatocytes themselves — viral, drug-induced, autoimmune, or cirrhotic liver disease.

The LFT changes — VIVA must:

  • Both conjugated & unconjugated bilirubin rise (mixed hyperbilirubinemia) — because the sick hepatocyte fails at both conjugation and excretion.
  • ALT & AST markedly raised — the direct marker of hepatocyte injury.
  • ALP only mildly raised — some intrahepatic cholestasis, but no big duct block.
  • PT/INR raised but NOT corrected by vitamin K — synthetic failure of the liver.
  • Albumin low (if chronic) → edema.

Why mixed bilirubin: the damaged hepatocyte cannot conjugate bilirubin (so unconjugated piles up) AND cannot excrete it (so conjugated leaks back into blood). Both fractions therefore rise.

Mechanism in brief: when hepatocytes die, their enzymes (ALT/AST) leak into blood — the higher the enzymes, the more severe the injury. ALP rises only mildly because there is intrahepatic cholestasis but no extrahepatic duct obstruction (compare: obstruction gives ALP/GGT ↑↑). PT stays high despite vitamin K because the liver's synthetic machinery is broken — the organ literally cannot make the clotting factors (unlike obstruction, where giving vitamin K fixes the PT). Low albumin reflects impaired protein synthesis when the disease is chronic.

Sources: Robbins ch.18; UpToDate — Liver chemistry tests

HBV infection — pathogenesis & outcome

HBV is a DNA virus that infects hepatocytes, but here is the key insight: the liver damage is caused by our own immune response, not by the virus directly.

Pathogenesis:

  • The virus enters hepatocytes (via the NTCP receptor) and viral antigens appear on the cell surface.
  • CD8+ cytotoxic T-cells attack and kill the infected hepatocytes — this is an 'immune clearance attempt' and it IS the hepatitis.
  • A strong response → acute hepatitis with virus clearance · a weak response → chronic infection · a massive response → fulminant hepatitis.

Outcomes — the single most important point is that AGE decides the outcome:

  • Adult infection → ~90% recover with immunity (anti-HBs positive).
  • Newborn/baby infection → ~90% become chronic (immune tolerance).
  • Chronic infection → cirrhosis → HCC.
  • An inactive carrier state is also possible (HBsAg+, low replication).

Extrahepatic disease: excess HBsAg combines with antibody to form immune complexes that deposit in vessels and kidneys → polyarteritis nodosa and membranous glomerulonephritis.

Mechanism in brief: HBV is not directly cytopathic — cytotoxic CD8+ T-cells recognize viral antigens on hepatocytes and kill them. Perinatal/childhood infection produces immune tolerance, so ~90% become chronic carriers; adult infection produces a competent immune response, so ~90% clear the virus. Chronic infection causes repeated injury and regeneration, and because HBV DNA can integrate into the host genome, it drives oncogenesis — which is why HBV causes HCC even without cirrhosis.

Sources: Robbins ch.18; CDC — Hepatitis B; WHO fact sheet

Laboratory diagnosis of viral hepatitis

Lab diagnosis of viral hepatitis means identifying which virus is responsible and whether the infection is recent, chronic, or past.

The fundamental serology rule:

  • IgM antibody = recent/acute infection (the first antibody class produced; disappears in months).
  • IgG antibody = past infection / immunity (appears later, persists for life).

Tests for each virus:

  • HAV: IgM anti-HAV (acute).
  • HBV: HBsAg, HBeAg, IgM anti-HBc, total anti-HBc, HBV DNA.
  • HCV: anti-HCV + HCV RNA (RNA proves active disease).
  • HDV: anti-HDV (only in HBsAg-positive patients).
  • HEV: IgM anti-HEV.

Supporting tests: LFT (ALT raised) and PCR for viral DNA/RNA — nucleic acid testing finds the virus itself, even during the window period before antibodies appear.

Mechanism in brief: the immune system makes antibodies against each virus, and detecting the specific class tells when infection occurred — IgM appears first (within a week) and marks acute disease, while IgG appears later and persists as a memory of past infection. In the window period (HBsAg already gone, anti-HBs not yet made) the only positive HBV test is IgM anti-HBc — plus HBV DNA by PCR. This is why nucleic acid testing is used in blood banks.

Sources: Robbins ch.18; CDC; WHO testing guidelines

Hepatotropic viruses

Hepatotropic viruses are the 5 viruses that primarily target and live in hepatocytes — A, B, C, D and E. They are specially adapted to infect liver cells via specific receptors and replicate there.

VIVA must — route and behaviour:

  • A & E — fecal-oral (food/water), acute only. They are small, non-enveloped RNA viruses that survive stomach acid and the gut, are excreted in stool, and reach the liver via the portal vein.
  • B, C, D — blood/sex/needle, can be chronic. They are enveloped (fragile outside the body) so they must be transferred directly via blood, needles, sex, or mother-to-child.
  • HDV is defective — it has RNA but cannot build its own envelope, so it borrows HBsAg from HBV. HDV can only infect a person who already has (or simultaneously gets) HBV.
  • HEV in pregnancy → ~20% die (fulminant hepatitis).

Remember: the enteric pair = A & E (fecal-oral) · the blood-borne trio = B, C, D.

Mechanism in brief: A and E survive the environment and spread through contaminated food/water; B, C and D are enveloped and fragile, so they need direct blood/body-fluid contact. HDV is the defective one — it cannot replicate its envelope and depends on HBsAg, which is why HDV infection is always accompanied by HBV.

Sources: Robbins ch.18; WHO viral hepatitis fact sheet

Causes of hepatitis

Hepatitis means inflammation of the liver — caused by infection, toxins, autoimmunity, or metabolic disease.

Causes — VIVA must:

  • Viruses: HAV, HBV, HCV, HDV, HEV + CMV, EBV, HSV (non-hepatotropic).
  • Drugs: paracetamol, isoniazid, halothane, methyldopa.
  • Alcohol → alcoholic hepatitis.
  • Autoimmune hepatitis (ANA/SMA/LKM-1 positive).
  • Metabolic: NASH/NAFLD, Wilson disease, hemochromatosis.
  • Ischemic: shock, heart failure (zone 3 necrosis).

Why each injures the liver:

  • Viruses → direct infection + immune attack.
  • Paracetamol → toxic metabolite NAPQI kills hepatocytes (zone 3).
  • Alcohol → acetaldehyde + oxidative stress.
  • Autoimmune → T-cells attack the liver's own cells.
  • Metabolic → toxic accumulation (fat, copper, iron).

Mechanism in brief: the liver is the main drug-metabolizing organ, so toxic metabolites damage it directly (NAPQI from paracetamol). Alcohol's metabolite acetaldehyde is directly toxic and also causes oxidative stress and fat accumulation. Autoimmune hepatitis comes from failure of immune tolerance — autoantibodies and T-cells attack hepatocytes. Metabolic causes (NAFLD fat, Wilson copper, hemochromatosis iron, α1-antitrypsin) accumulate toxic substances inside hepatocytes. The liver needs high blood flow, so shock/heart failure produce centrilobular ischemic necrosis. In Bangladesh the commonest acute viral hepatitis is HEV (water-borne), and anti-TB drug-induced hepatitis is also common.

Sources: Robbins ch.18; Distribution of Liver Disease in Bangladesh (PMC)

Serologic markers of viral hepatitis

Serologic markers are the viral antigens and our antibodies against them found in blood; each marker has a specific meaning about the infection.

HBV markers — the VIVA must:

  • HBsAg = infected now (acute or chronic).
  • Anti-HBs = immune (recovered or vaccinated).
  • IgM anti-HBc = acute/recent infection.
  • HBeAg = active replication = very infectious.
  • Total anti-HBc = past exposure, lifelong.

Other viruses:

  • HAV: IgM anti-HAV (acute) · IgG anti-HAV (immunity).
  • HCV: anti-HCV (exposure) + HCV RNA (active).
  • HEV: IgM anti-HEV.
  • HDV: anti-HDV.

Mechanism in brief: HBsAg is the viral surface protein — it is present whenever the virus replicates, appearing 1–10 weeks after exposure. Anti-HBs is a neutralizing antibody that only persists after recovery or vaccination, so it means protective immunity. Anti-HBc (core antibody) is produced in every HBV infection, and because the IgM class appears first and disappears within ~6 months, it marks recent infection. HBeAg is secreted during active replication, so HBeAg+ patients have a high viral load and are highly infectious (used for mother-to-child risk assessment). The classic trap: anti-HCV is NOT immunity — it only means exposure; active HCV is confirmed by HCV RNA.

Sources: CDC; Merck Manual; Robbins ch.18

Possible outcomes of HBV infection

After exposure to HBV, several outcomes are possible — decided mostly by the age and strength of the immune response.

Outcomes — VIVA must:

  • Subclinical infection (most common, ~65%) — no symptoms.
  • Acute hepatitis → recovery + immunity (adults ~90%).
  • Fulminant hepatitis (<1%) — a massive immune attack destroys almost all hepatocytes.
  • Chronic hepatitis — babies ~90%, adults 5–10%.
  • Inactive carrier state — virus persists with little damage (HBeAg−, low DNA).
  • Chronic infection → cirrhosis → HCC.

Remember the one-liner: Adults clear, babies carry.

Mechanism in brief: outcome depends on the strength of the immune response. A strong adult response clears the virus and leaves lifelong immunity; a weak/incomplete response lets the virus persist as chronic hepatitis; neonatal infection produces immune tolerance so ~90% become chronic carriers. Fulminant hepatitis results from an over-exuberant immune response destroying nearly all infected hepatocytes at once. Chronic infection keeps the liver in a cycle of injury and regeneration — repeated regeneration plus viral DNA integration causes the mutations that lead to cirrhosis and HCC. Exam trap: HBV can cause HCC even without cirrhosis (viral DNA integrates into host genes), unlike HCV which needs cirrhosis.

Sources: CDC; Robbins ch.18

Interpretation of HBV serologic markers

Reading an HBV serology panel means matching the marker pattern to one of 6 clinical situations.

The 6 patterns — VIVA must:

  • All negative = susceptible (never infected, not immune).
  • HBsAg + and IgM anti-HBc + = acute infection.
  • HBsAg −, IgM anti-HBc + = window period.
  • HBsAg + for > 6 months = chronic infection.
  • Anti-HBs + and anti-HBc + = recovered / natural immunity.
  • Anti-HBs + only = vaccinated.

Why each pattern means a specific status: each marker appears and disappears at a different time, so the combination tells the whole story. IgM anti-HBc is the 'recent infection' flag; HBsAg is the 'virus present now' flag; anti-HBs is the 'virus gone' flag.

Mechanism in brief: susceptible = never exposed. Acute = virus present (HBsAg+) and recent (IgM anti-HBc+). Window = HBsAg already cleared but anti-HBs not yet formed — only the core antibody (IgM) is positive. Chronic = HBsAg persists beyond 6 months with IgG (not IgM) anti-HBc. Recovered = both anti-HBs and total anti-HBc positive (had the infection, cleared it). Vaccinated = anti-HBs alone, because the person was never infected (no core antibody).

Sources: CDC — Clinical Testing & Diagnosis for Hepatitis B; Merck — Hep B serology table

Differences between Hepatitis A and Hepatitis B

HAV vs HBV — the comparison the examiners love:

  • Genome: HAV = RNA · HBV = DNA.
  • Transmission: HAV = fecal-oral · HBV = blood/sex/mother-to-child.
  • Chronicity: HAV = never chronic · HBV = can be chronic.
  • Incubation: HAV short (2–6 weeks) · HBV long (6 weeks–6 months).
  • Cirrhosis/HCC: HAV = none · HBV = yes.
  • Carrier state: HAV = none · HBV = yes.

Why HAV never becomes chronic: HAV is fully cleared by the immune system — it does not integrate into host DNA and provokes a strong protective IgG response that lasts for life; there is no latency or reservoir.

Why HBV can become chronic: HBV DNA forms a stable cccDNA in the nucleus of hepatocytes, and in children or immunocompromised hosts the immune system fails to eliminate infected cells → persistent infection.

Mechanism in brief: HAV is a hardy non-enveloped virus that survives in the environment and spreads through stool-contaminated food/water; HBV is enveloped and fragile, requiring direct blood/body-fluid contact. Only chronic infection drives years of injury-regeneration that leads to cirrhosis and HCC — and because HAV has no chronic stage, it never causes these. Both are preventable by vaccine; HAV is killed by boiling water/hygiene, HBV needs blood safety + vaccination.

Sources: Robbins ch.18; WHO fact sheets (HAV, HBV)

Window period

Window period is the gap during acute HBV when HBsAg has disappeared but anti-HBs has not yet appeared — a serologically 'silent' phase of infection.

VIVA must — the marker pattern:

  • During the window: HBsAg negative, anti-HBs negative, but IgM anti-HBc positive (and HBV DNA positive).
  • So the only positive marker is IgM anti-HBc.
  • Blood-bank danger: a donor in the window is infectious yet screening appears negative → nucleic acid testing (NAT) is used.

Why the window exists: the immune response takes time. After HBsAg rises and starts being cleared, the antibody against the surface protein lags behind — for a few weeks both HBsAg and anti-HBs are absent even though the infection is real.

Why IgM anti-HBc is the clue: the core antibody appears earlier than the surface antibody, and the IgM class marks recent infection — so during the window, IgM anti-HBc (and HBV DNA) are the only positive tests.

Mechanism in brief: the immune system clears the virus's surface antigen but needs weeks to mount the anti-surface antibody response; in the meantime the core antibody (IgM) is already positive. In blood banking this matters because a window-period donor can transmit HBV despite a negative HBsAg screen — hence NAT for viral DNA.

Acute viral hepatitis — definition & causes

Acute viral hepatitis is diffuse inflammation of the liver caused by a virus, lasting < 6 months and usually healing completely.

Causes:

  • Hepatotropic: HAV, HBV (± HDV), HCV, HEV.
  • Non-hepatotropic: CMV, EBV, HSV, VZV, yellow fever, rubella, parvovirus B19.

Clinical phases — describe them:

  • Prodromal (pre-icteric): fever, malaise, nausea, anorexia — before jaundice appears.
  • Icteric: jaundice, dark urine, pale stool; transaminases peak.
  • Convalescence: slow recovery over weeks.

Mechanism in brief: the 6-month cut-off separates self-limited infections (immune system clears the virus and the liver heals) from persistent infections that become chronic. The prodromal phase reflects early viral replication and the immune response before liver dysfunction is severe enough to cause jaundice; jaundice appears only when enough hepatocytes are damaged that bilirubin handling fails, and transaminases peak at that time. Exam trap: acute hepatitis can be completely silent (~65% of HBV) — jaundice is not always present.

Morphology of acute viral hepatitis

Under the microscope, acute viral hepatitis has a classic triad — memorise all three:

  • Ballooning degeneration — swollen, pale hepatocytes (the cell membrane pump fails, water enters).
  • Councilman (acidophil) bodies — dying/apoptotic hepatocytes appearing as round, dense eosinophilic bodies.
  • Mononuclear inflammation — lymphocytes in the portal tracts and lobule, with Kupffer cell hyperplasia.

Other features to mention: spotty necrosis (scattered dead hepatocytes), lobular disarray, bile plugs (cholestasis in some cases), and — critically — the architecture is preserved, NO fibrosis/cirrhosis.

Mechanism in brief: ballooning degeneration happens when damaged hepatocytes lose ATP and the Na+/K+ pump fails, so water floods in. Councilman bodies are apoptotic hepatocytes killed by cytotoxic T-cells — programmed death without inflammation, hence the dense eosinophilic appearance. The infiltrate is mononuclear because viral hepatitis is a cell-mediated (lymphocyte) disease — neutrophils would suggest bacterial infection or cholangitis. The lobular architecture is preserved because necrosis is spotty/scattered and there is no time for fibrous scarring. Exam trap: severe cases show bridging necrosis (portal-to-portal or portal-to-central), which carries a high risk of progression to chronic disease.

Sources: Robbins ch.18

Complications of acute viral hepatitis

Most patients recover completely, but a few develop serious complications.

Complications — VIVA must:

  • Fulminant hepatitis / acute liver failure — most feared; HEV in pregnancy (20% mortality), HBV.
  • Chronic hepatitis → cirrhosis → HCC (HBV, HCV).
  • Carrier state (HBV, HCV).
  • Cholestatic hepatitis — prolonged obstructive-type jaundice with mild enzymes.
  • Extrahepatic immune-complex disease — PAN and glomerulonephritis (HBV); cryoglobulinemia (HCV).

Mechanism in brief: fulminant hepatitis occurs when necrosis becomes submassive/massive — more than ~80–90% of hepatocytes die and the liver fails acutely (HEV in pregnancy, HBV). Chronicity happens because HBV/HCV evade clearance, keeping inflammation going until fibrosis → cirrhosis. Immune-complex complications arise because viral antigens combine with antibodies into circulating complexes that deposit in small vessels (PAN, glomerulonephritis) or form cryoglobulins (HCV), damaging organs away from the liver. Cholestatic hepatitis comes from intrahepatic bile-flow impairment by inflammation/canalicular injury. The most feared complication is acute liver failure; the most likely long-term risk is chronicity.

Liver function tests — the tests & their indications

Liver function tests (LFT) are blood tests that check how the liver is doing, grouped by the job they measure.

The four groups — VIVA must:

  • Injury markers: ALT, AST (ALT is the more liver-specific).
  • Cholestasis markers: ALP, GGT.
  • Synthetic function: albumin, PT/INR.
  • Bilirubin: total / direct / indirect.

Indications — when to do them:

  • Jaundice / suspected liver disease.
  • Monitoring drug therapy (hepatotoxic drugs).
  • Pre-operative and blood-donor screening.
  • Following chronic liver disease and assessing severity (e.g., MELD score).

Why ALT > AST in viral hepatitis: ALT lives almost only in hepatocytes, while AST is also found in muscle, heart and RBCs — so a high ALT is a reliable sign of liver injury.

Mechanism in brief: each group reflects a different liver job. Injury → enzymes leak (ALT/AST). Bile flow → ALP/GGT rise when bile is stagnant. Synthesis → albumin and clotting factors fall. Clotting factors have short half-lives, so PT prolongs early and sensitively when synthesis fails. Exam trap pattern recognition: Alcohol → AST>ALT (~2:1) + GGT↑ · Viral → ALT>AST, very high · Obstruction → ALP/GGT ↑↑.

Jaundice — definition & classification

Jaundice is the yellow discoloration of skin, sclera and mucosa due to hyperbilirubinemia (bilirubin > 2 mg/dL).

Classification — VIVA must:

  • Pre-hepatic — hemolysis → unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin ↑.
  • Hepatic — hepatocyte disease (Gilbert, viral, drugs, cirrhosis) → mixed bilirubin.
  • Post-hepatic — obstruction → conjugated (direct) bilirubin ↑.

The logic: bilirubin is produced (pre-hepatic), processed by the liver (hepatic — uptake/conjugation/excretion), and drained through the bile ducts (post-hepatic). The defective step defines the type of jaundice.

Mechanism in brief: normal total bilirubin is ~0.3–1.2 mg/dL; above ~2 mg/dL bilirubin diffuses into tissues — the sclera stains first because it is rich in elastin. Unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin rises in overproduction or conjugation defects; conjugated (direct) rises in excretion/obstruction problems — so the fraction points to the mechanism. Neonatal jaundice is mostly physiological (unconjugated); persistent conjugated neonatal jaundice suggests biliary atresia.

Acholuric vs choluric jaundice

These two terms describe the urine color in jaundice:

  • Acholuric = no bile in urine (urine stays pale) — the type seen in hemolytic jaundice.
  • Choluric = bile in urine (urine is dark tea-colored) — the type seen in obstructive jaundice.

The full comparison — VIVA must:

  • Acholuric → hemolytic; bilirubin is unconjugated → lipid-soluble → not filtered by the kidney.
  • Choluric → obstructive; bilirubin is conjugated → water-soluble → filtered → dark urine.
  • Stool: acholuric = normal/dark · choluric = pale/clay.
  • Urine urobilinogen: acholuric = increased · choluric = decreased/absent.

Mechanism in brief: water solubility decides everything. Unconjugated bilirubin stays in the blood (pale urine) because the kidney cannot filter it; conjugated bilirubin reaches the urine (dark) and the gut (stool color). Urobilinogen is made from bilirubin by gut bacteria and reabsorbed: in hemolysis more bilirubin reaches the gut so more urobilinogen is absorbed (urine ↑); in obstruction no bilirubin reaches the gut so urobilinogen disappears. Exam trap: 'acholuric' means no bile in the urine — the urine is pale, not the patient.

Obstructive jaundice — etiology & pathogenesis

Obstructive jaundice is jaundice because bile (with its conjugated bilirubin) cannot flow out of the liver.

Etiology:

  • Extrahepatic: gallstones (commonest), carcinoma of the pancreas / ampulla / bile duct, strictures, parasites (ascaris).
  • Intrahepatic: drugs (chlorpromazine), PBC, PSC, cholestatic viral hepatitis.

Pathogenesis — the sequence:

  • Bile duct blocked → back-pressure → conjugated bilirubin regurgitates into the blood.
  • Result: jaundice, dark urine, pale/clay stool, pruritus (bile salts deposit in skin).
  • Fats and vitamins A/D/E/K are not absorbed → steatorrhea, easy bruising (vit K), night blindness (vit A).
  • Chronic obstruction → ductular proliferation → secondary biliary cirrhosis.

Mechanism in brief: blocked flow raises bile-duct pressure, and bile-acid accumulation makes the biliary epithelium produce and regurgitate more ALP and GGT — the hallmark. Conjugated bilirubin cannot proceed so it refluxes through hepatocytes into the blood and is filtered by the kidney (dark urine). Without bile in the gut there is no stercobilin (pale stool) and no fat-soluble vitamin absorption. Pruritus comes from bile salts depositing in the skin. Persistent back-pressure plus bile-salt toxicity drives ductular proliferation and periductal fibrosis → secondary biliary cirrhosis. And remember: the PT prolongs but corrects with vitamin K in obstruction.

Sources: Robbins ch.18; Merck Manual — Cholestasis
12B · Q16Direct vs indirect

Direct and indirect bilirubin

These two forms of bilirubin in blood are named after the van den Bergh test: direct = conjugated (reacts directly with the reagent), indirect = unconjugated (needs alcohol to react).

VIVA must:

  • Indirect (unconjugated) — lipid-soluble, travels bound to albumin, NOT present in urine. High in hemolysis and neonatal jaundice.
  • Direct (conjugated) — water-soluble, present in urine (dark) and excreted in bile. High in obstruction.

Why it matters: conjugation (adding glucuronic acid) turns bilirubin water-soluble — that is how the body gets rid of it, into bile and urine. It is the liver's 'detox' step.

Mechanism in brief: unconjugated bilirubin is hydrophobic, so in the test it only reacts with the diazo reagent after alcohol is added (the 'indirect' reaction); conjugated bilirubin reacts directly (the 'direct' reaction). Unconjugated bilirubin binds albumin and cannot be filtered by the kidney — so urine has no bilirubin. Because it is lipid-soluble, it can cross the blood-brain barrier → kernicterus — the unconjugated form is the dangerous one for the brain; conjugated bilirubin is harmless to the brain.

Physiological jaundice & causes of neonatal jaundice

Physiological jaundice is the normal, transient unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia of newborns — appearing day 2–3, peaking day 4–5, and resolving by ~day 10.

Why it happens — the triple mechanism:

  • High bilirubin load — newborns have many short-lived RBCs.
  • Immature UGT1A1 — the conjugation enzyme is slow in the first week.
  • High enterohepatic circulation — no gut flora and β-glucuronidase re-absorb bilirubin.

Pathological causes of neonatal jaundice:

  • Hemolysis: ABO/Rh incompatibility, G6PD deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis.
  • Infection: sepsis, congenital infections.
  • Biliary atresia — conjugated jaundice, pale stools (surgical emergency).
  • Breast-milk jaundice.
  • Metabolic: Crigler–Najjar, hypothyroidism, galactosemia.

Danger: severe unconjugated bilirubin → kernicterus (stains basal ganglia). Prevent/treat with phototherapy.

Mechanism in brief: physiological jaundice combines a high bilirubin load, immature conjugation and re-absorption of bilirubin from the gut. Pathological causes add extra load on top — hemolysis (ABO/Rh, G6PD, spherocytosis) destroys RBCs massively; sepsis injures the liver and causes hemolysis; biliary atresia blocks drainage so conjugated bilirubin rises (the red flag for surgery); metabolic defects impair conjugation. Phototherapy works by converting bilirubin to water-soluble isomers excreted without conjugation. Exam trap: physiological = unconjugated; conjugated neonatal jaundice → think biliary atresia.

Sources: StatPearls — Breast Milk Jaundice; Robbins ch.18

Breast milk jaundice

Breast-milk jaundice is prolonged, harmless unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia in a healthy breastfed baby, caused by substances in breast milk that block bilirubin conjugation.

VIVA must — key features:

  • Onset ~2 weeks, peaking at 2–3 weeks; the baby is healthy and gaining weight.
  • Cause: breast milk contains β-glucuronidase (deconjugates bilirubin in the gut, increasing re-absorption) and pregnane-3α,20β-diol (inhibits UGT1A1 conjugation).
  • Diagnosis is one of exclusion — rule out sepsis, hemolysis and biliary atresia first.
  • Do NOT stop breastfeeding. A transient 24–48 h interruption can help confirm the diagnosis.

Mechanism in brief: β-glucuronidase in breast milk deconjugates bilirubin in the gut so more free bilirubin is re-absorbed (enterohepatic circulation ↑); the pregnancy hormone inhibits the conjugation enzyme. The result is unconjugated bilirubin staying high, peaking at about 2 weeks. Note that early (intake-related) jaundice in the first days is different — poor feeding → dehydration and fewer stools → less bilirubin excretion. Breast-milk jaundice is benign (levels are moderate and the baby is well), but you must exclude pathological causes before calling it that.

Bilirubin — definition & sources

Bilirubin is the yellow pigment produced from the breakdown of heme (mainly from hemoglobin). It is the pigment that causes jaundice when its level rises.

Sources — VIVA must:

  • ~80–85% from senescent (old) RBCs destroyed in the spleen, liver and bone marrow (reticuloendothelial system).
  • ~15–20% from ineffective erythropoiesis plus other heme proteins (myoglobin, cytochromes, catalase, peroxidases).

The pathway — remember the two enzymes:

  • Hemoglobin → heme → (enzyme: heme oxygenase) → biliverdin → (enzyme: biliverdin reductase) → bilirubin.

Mechanism in brief: heme is broken down so its iron can be recycled. RBCs live ~120 days; senescent RBCs are phagocytosed, releasing hemoglobin, and heme is converted through biliverdin to bilirubin. The remaining ~15–20% comes from 'early-labeled' bilirubin — marrow destroying some red-cell precursors before release (ineffective erythropoiesis) — plus catabolism of other heme proteins like myoglobin and the cytochromes.

Cirrhosis & chronic liver disease

Cirrhosis is the end-stage of chronic liver disease defined by THREE hallmarks: (1) diffuse fibrosis, (2) regenerative nodules, and (3) destruction of normal liver architecture.

Chronic liver disease (CLD) is ongoing liver injury/fibrosis for > 6 months — cirrhosis is its end-stage.

VIVA must:

  • Cirrhosis must be diffuse — focal scarring is NOT cirrhosis.
  • The result is liver failure + portal hypertension.
  • It is irreversible — treatment aims to stop progression.

Mechanism in brief: repeated hepatocyte injury activates hepatic stellate cells, which lay down collagen scar (fibrosis) around regenerating islands of hepatocytes. The surviving hepatocytes proliferate into nodules, but the scar separates them from normal portal tracts and central veins, so the lobular architecture and its blood-flow 'wiring' are destroyed. Because the collagen scaffold is cross-linked and the nodule-to-blood connections are gone, the process is irreversible. All three features — fibrosis, nodules, architectural distortion — must be present; missing one means it is not cirrhosis.

Classification of cirrhosis

Cirrhosis is classified in two complementary ways:

  • A. Etiologic (clinical): viral, alcoholic, metabolic (NASH), biliary, autoimmune, cryptogenic — tells the cause and guides treatment.
  • B. Morphologic (pathologic): micronodular / macronodular / mixed — describes what the liver looks like.

  • Mixed — both patterns together (transition).

Remember: Micro = alcohol & biliary · Macro = viral & metabolic.

Grading and staging of chronic hepatitis

Two separate systems answer two different questions about chronic hepatitis — do not mix them up:

13 · Q5Pathogenesis

Pathogenesis of cirrhosis in chronic viral hepatitis

Chronic viral hepatitis progresses to cirrhosis through a repeated injury → inflammation → fibrosis cycle.

The steps — VIVA must:

  • The virus persists (HBV/HCV) → CD8+ T-cells keep attacking hepatocytes but never fully clear the virus.
  • Chronic necroinflammation → stellate cells (Ito cells) activate under the influence of TGF-β and PDGF.
  • Stellate cells become myofibroblasts → deposit collagen in the space of Disse and portal tracts (septa).
  • Fibrosis → nodules → capillarization of sinusoids → portal hypertension.
  • End-stage: cirrhosis → HCC.

Remember the chain: Injure → Inflame → Activate stellate → Collagen → Nodules → Cirrhosis.

Mechanism in brief: years of immune attack without clearance mean years of injury and regeneration, so fibrosis accumulates. Damaged hepatocytes and Kupffer cells release TGF-β and PDGF, which transform quiescent stellate cells into collagen-secreting myofibroblasts — the scar is laid in the space of Disse (perisinusoidal) and portal tracts. 'Capillarization' means the sinusoids lose their holes (fenestrae) and acquire a basal lamina, so hepatocytes lose contact with blood — worsening function and raising portal pressure. Chronic inflammation and regeneration cause repeated DNA damage, and HBV DNA can integrate into host DNA, driving mutations that lead to HCC.

NASH & its progression to cirrhosis

NASH = Non-Alcoholic SteatoHepatitis — fat + inflammation + hepatocyte ballooning in the absence of significant alcohol. NAFLD is fat alone; NASH is the dangerous inflammatory form. (The newer name is MASLD.)

Ascites — definition & pathogenesis in cirrhosis

Ascites is abnormal fluid collection in the peritoneal cavity — a transudate in cirrhosis.

Pathogenesis — the three forces (VIVA must):

  • Portal hypertension → high hydrostatic pressure in liver/splanchnic capillaries → fluid leaks out.
  • Low albumin → low oncotic pressure → cannot pull the fluid back in.
  • Splanchnic vasodilation → 'arterial underfilling' → RAAS + aldosterone + ADH → the kidney holds salt and water → more fluid → more ascites.

Remember: Portal HTN + low albumin + salt/water retention = the three causes of ascites.

Mechanism in brief: portal hypertension pushes fluid out of the sinusoids (capillarization makes them rigid and leaky → lymph overflow), and low albumin cannot pull it back. The kidney then becomes a major player: splanchnic vasodilation drops the effective arterial volume ('underfilling'), which switches on RAAS, aldosterone and ADH, so the kidney retains sodium and water — feeding more ascites. Morphology of cirrhosis

Gross appearance: the liver is shrunken, hard, with a diffusely nodular surface ('hob-nail liver'); the cut surface shows nodules separated by fibrous septa.

  • Nodules of regenerating hepatocytes surrounded by fibrous bands.
  • Architecture lost — no normal lobules or central veins; the microcirculation is re-routed.
  • Bridging fibrosis (portal–portal / portal–central).
  • Ductular proliferation + chronic inflammation in the septa.
  • Special stains: Masson trichrome (blue collagen/fibrosis), reticulin, iron, PAS-diastase.

Mechanism in brief: fibrous scar contracts, shrinking the liver, while regenerating hepatocytes push outward between the scar bands — producing the surface nodules. The fibrous septa separate islands of hepatocytes from their portal tracts and central veins, so the normal lobular wiring is destroyed. Ductular proliferation is a reaction to fibrosis and obstruction at the septa. Special stains matter: Masson trichrome highlights collagen, reticulin shows nodular regeneration, iron stain reveals hemochromatosis and PAS-diastase reveals α1-antitrypsin inclusions. Cirrhosis is a pre-malignant state because the nodules contain dysplastic hepatocytes from years of injury-regeneration.

Sources: Robbins ch.18

Clinical features of cirrhosis

Present cirrhosis as compensated vs decompensated:

  • Compensated: few symptoms (fatigue, anorexia, hepatomegaly) — the liver's reserve still meets needs.
  • Decompensated: ascites, variceal bleeding, encephalopathy, jaundice, edema, coagulopathy — the complications appear.

Signs to quote:

  • Skin: spider angiomata, palmar erythema, white nails.
  • Endocrine (men): gynecomastia, testicular atrophy — estrogen is not cleared.
  • Hematology: anemia, thrombocytopenia (hypersplenism).
  • Cause clues: Kayser-Fleischer ring (Wilson), bronze skin (hemochromatosis), Dupuytren contracture (alcohol).

Remember decompensation as A-V-ENCE: Ascites · Varices · Encephalopathy · No—jaundice · Coagulopathy · Edema (+ HRS).

Mechanism in brief: the liver has huge functional reserve — ~70% can be lost before symptoms appear — which is why compensated cirrhosis may be silent. Decompensation happens when portal hypertension or synthetic failure overtakes the reserve. Spider angiomata and gynecomastia arise because the failing liver cannot metabolize estrogen, leaving an excess that dilates skin arterioles and grows breast tissue in men. Bruising reflects low clotting factors and platelets (hypersplenism). The Kayser-Fleischer ring is copper deposited in Descemet's membrane — a clue pointing to the cause.

Complications of cirrhosis

Complications — VIVA must:

  • Portal hypertension → esophageal varices → variceal hemorrhage — life-threatening, the most feared complication.
  • Ascites → spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) — the commonest infection.
  • Hepatic encephalopathy.

Mechanism of edema in cirrhosis

Edema in cirrhosis is fluid accumulation in the tissues from Starling-force problems combined with kidney salt/water retention.

The mechanisms — VIVA must:

  • Portal hypertension → high hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out (ascites + leg edema).
  • Hypoalbuminemia → low oncotic pressure cannot pull fluid back in (generalized edema).
  • RAAS + aldosterone + ADH → the kidney retains sodium and water → more volume → more edema.

Remember: Push out (pressure) + can't pull back (albumin) + kidney keeps more = edema.

Mechanism in brief: fluid leaks from capillaries faster than it is reabsorbed. Portal hypertension raises capillary hydrostatic pressure, pushing fluid out — mostly in the lower limbs (gravity) and peritoneum (ascites). Low albumin lowers oncotic pressure so fluid stays out — generalized edema. Splancnic vasodilation causes 'arterial underfilling', which activates RAAS/aldosterone/ADH, so the kidney retains salt and water, expanding volume and worsening the edema. The edema is dependent (legs, sacrum) and in cirrhosis it typically follows ascites.

Sources: Robbins ch.18; clinical medicine texts

Acute liver failure — definition & causes

Acute liver failure (ALF) is severe liver dysfunction occurring in < 6 months, in a patient with no prior liver disease, defined by coagulopathy (INR ≥ 1.5) + encephalopathy.

Causes — VIVA must:

  • Paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose — the commonest cause in the West (toxic NAPQI metabolite kills hepatocytes).
  • Viral — HBV, HEV — commonest in Bangladesh/the developing world; HEV in pregnancy ~20% die.
  • Autoimmune hepatitis, ischemic hepatitis, Wilson disease, toxins (Amanita mushroom).

Mechanism in brief: ALF is a sudden, massive loss of liver function in a previously healthy liver. The definition is strict — no prior liver disease — because if cirrhosis already exists, it is called acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF), with different prognosis and management. Encephalopathy and coagulopathy must both be present because they are the functional consequences of massive necrosis: clotting factors are not made (INR ≥ 1.5) and ammonia is not detoxified. In Bangladesh, viral hepatitis (HEV — 20% fatal in pregnancy — and HBV) leads because of water-borne/spread infection; HEV in pregnancy is worse due to hormonal/immune changes that enhance replication and the immune response.

Liver neoplasms & morphology of hepatocellular carcinoma

Neoplasms of the liver:

  • Benign: hemangioma (the commonest), hepatocellular adenoma.
  • Malignant: hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) — the commonest primary — plus cholangiocarcinoma, hepatoblastoma, angiosarcoma.
  • Metastatic carcinoma is the commonest liver malignancy overall (more than all primaries).

HCC morphology — VIVA must:

  • Gross: a large soft mass, often on a cirrhotic liver, may be multinodular.
  • Growth patterns: trabecular ~70% (thick cords of hepatocytes separated by sinusoid-like spaces), solid 20%, pseudoglandular 10%, macrotrabecular 1%.
  • Cells: large polygonal cells with prominent nucleoli; clear cell change, fatty change, bile production (diagnostic of hepatocyte origin), Mallory-Denk bodies; vascular invasion is common.

Mechanism in brief: HCC arises on a background of chronic injury → regeneration → accumulated mutations (and in HBV, viral DNA integration) that transform hepatocytes. The trabecular pattern mimics normal liver cords — hepatocytes grow as thick trabeculae between sinusoid-like blood spaces, the 'look-alike' pattern of hepatocellular origin. Bile production is diagnostic because making bile is a hepatocyte-specific function — it proves hepatocellular origin versus cholangiocarcinoma or metastasis. Immunohistochemistry: HepPar-1, AFP, GPC-3.

13 · Q14Hepatorenal syndrome
What is hepatorenal syndrome?
↺ Tap to flip →

Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS)

HRS is functional kidney failure in advanced cirrhosis/ascites — the kidney is structurally normal but fails because of severe renal vasoconstriction.

VIVA must:

  • Pathogenesis: splanchnic vasodilation → 'arterial underfilling' → RAAS / sympathetic / ADH → renal vasoconstriction → ↓GFR.
  • Type 1: rapid — creatinine > 2.5 mg/dL within 2 weeks (very poor prognosis).
  • Type 2: slow, usually with refractory ascites.
  • Diagnosis: creatinine > 1.5, no improvement after albumin + stopping diuretics, no shock/nephrotoxins, normal urine sediment and USG.

Mechanism in brief: it is a functional prerenal failure. Cirrhosis causes splanchnic vasodilation, so the effective blood volume falls; the kidney responds to the perceived underfilling (via RAAS, sympathetic activation, ADH) by constricting its own vessels — blood is shunted away from the kidneys, GFR drops and azotemia appears. The kidney itself is fine (hence transplantable!). You must first exclude other renal causes — acute tubular necrosis, shock, nephrotoxic drugs, intrinsic disease — because the treatment differs. Treatment: albumin + terlipressin; the best option is liver transplant, after which the kidneys recover.

Sources: StatPearls — Hepatorenal Syndrome; Robbins ch.18

Hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS)

HPS is intrapulmonary vasodilation causing low blood oxygen (hypoxemia) in a patient with liver disease/portal hypertension.

VIVA must:

  • Why: the failing liver fails to clear NO and VEGF → pulmonary capillaries dilate → blood flows past the air-exchange surface → shunt + V/Q mismatch → low oxygen.
  • Platypnea-orthodeoxia — breathlessness and hypoxia worse on standing (gravity increases flow to dilated lower-zone vessels).
  • Clubbing, cyanosis, spider angiomata.
  • Confirmed by contrast (bubble) echocardiography — microbubbles pass through the dilated lung vessels into the left heart.

Mechanism in brief: liver failure leaves vasodilatory mediators (NO, VEGF) uncleared, so pulmonary pre-capillary and capillary vessels dilate. Blood then flows past the exchange surface too fast to oxygenate properly — a shunt with V/Q mismatch — causing hypoxemia. In the upright position, gravity increases blood flow to the lower zones where the dilated vessels sit, worsening oxygenation (platypnea-orthodeoxia). Contrast echo proves the intrapulmonary shunt by showing bubbles pass through the lungs. Removing the diseased liver (transplant) removes the mediator source and cures it. Exam trap: HPS = dilated vessels + hypoxemia; portopulmonary hypertension (PoPH) = constricted vessels + high PA pressure — the opposite.

Liver failure — clinical features & laboratory findings

Clinical features — VIVA must:

  • Jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy (confusion → coma), coagulopathy (bleeding), hypoalbuminemia → edema/ascites, hypoglycemia, hepatorenal syndrome, infections (SBP).

Laboratory findings — VIVA must:

  • PT/INR ↑ — the earliest and most sensitive marker (factor VII has the shortest half-life).
  • Bilirubin ↑, ALT/AST ↑ (acute), albumin ↓, ammonia ↑, glucose ↓.
  • Platelets ↓ (hypersplenism), Na⁺ ↓ (dilutional hyponatremia), creatinine ↑ (HRS).
  • AFP ↑ → think HCC (surveillance).

Mechanism in brief: all these features appear together because the liver is multifunctional — each pathway's failure produces one feature: jaundice = bilirubin not processed; encephalopathy = ammonia not cleared; coagulopathy = clotting factors not made; hypoglycemia = gluconeogenesis stops; edema/ascites = albumin falls; hepatorenal syndrome = splanchnic vasodilation → renal vasoconstriction; infections = Kupffer-cell defense lost. PT/INR is the first lab sign because clotting factors have short half-lives (factor VII ~hours), so prolongation appears before albumin falls. Platelets drop from hypersplenism; hyponatremia is dilutional from ADH excess.

Hepatic encephalopathy — definition & pathogenesis

HE is a neuropsychiatric syndrome (confusion, asterixis, coma) caused by liver failure or portosystemic shunting — the brain is poisoned by substances the liver should have cleared.

Pathogenesis — the four mechanisms (VIVA must):

  • Ammonia (the main one): the liver cannot convert it to urea → it crosses the blood-brain barrier and combines with glutamate to form glutamine in astrocytes → astrocyte swelling (Alzheimer type II cells) → brain edema and dysfunction.
  • GABA/benzodiazepine pathway: increased inhibitory signaling → the brain 'shuts down'.
  • False neurotransmitters: aromatic amino acids are converted to weak transmitters (e.g., octopamine) that displace dopamine.
  • Manganese: accumulates in the basal ganglia → Parkinson-like features.

Precipitants — VIVA must: GI bleeding (blood = protein → ammonia burst), constipation, high-protein diet, infection, dehydration, diuretics (hypokalemia), sedatives.

Mechanism in brief: ammonia from gut protein and bacteria is normally converted to urea; in liver failure or shunting it rises and enters the brain, where glutamine synthesis swells the astrocytes. Hepatic failure also increases endogenous benzodiazepine-like substances and GABAergic tone (sedation/coma), upsets the amino-acid balance toward false transmitters, and fails to clear manganese. Precipitants work by adding more ammonia (GI bleed, constipation, high protein) or worsening liver/brain function (infection, dehydration, hypokalemia, sedatives). Grades I–IV; treat with lactulose + rifaximin to reduce gut ammonia. Asterixis ('flapping tremor') is the classic sign.

Pathogenesis of coagulopathy in liver disease

The mechanisms — VIVA must:

  • ↓ Clotting factor synthesis — the liver makes factors II, V, VII, IX, X, XI, XII and fibrinogen. Factor VII has the shortest half-life, so PT rises first.
  • Thrombocytopenia — hypersplenism sequesters platelets + the liver makes less thrombopoietin.
  • Vitamin K malabsorption (cholestasis) → factors II, VII, IX, X fall.
  • Platelet dysfunction and dysfibrinogenemia.
  • Hyperfibrinolysis — the liver cannot clear tPA and makes less α2-antiplasmin.

Mechanism in brief: the liver synthesizes almost all clotting factors; in liver failure, synthesis falls — and because factor VII has the shortest half-life, PT/INR prolongs first, making it the earliest, most sensitive marker. In cholestasis, bile does not reach the gut, so vitamin K (needed to activate II, VII, IX, X) is not absorbed — but these factors recover with injected vitamin K. Platelets fall from hypersplenism (portal hypertension) plus low thrombopoietin. Even though anticoagulant proteins also fall, the balance tips toward bleeding because of platelet dysfunction, dysfibrinogenemia and accelerated fibrinolysis. Exam trap: obstructive jaundice corrects with vitamin K; hepatocellular failure does not — the best discriminator.

13 · Q19Portal hypertension
How does portal hypertension develop in cirrhosis?
↺ Tap to flip →

Development of portal hypertension in cirrhosis

Portal hypertension is portal pressure > 12 mmHg, caused by increased resistance + increased blood inflow.

The mechanisms — VIVA must:

  • 1. Increased resistance (the main mechanism):
    • Structural: fibrosis + nodules physically squeeze and distort the sinusoids; capillarization (loss of fenestrae, basal lamina) makes them rigid.
    • Dynamic: activated stellate cells contract (myofibroblasts) raising sinusoidal tone; ↓ intrahepatic NO, ↑ endothelin-1 (a vasoconstrictor).
  • 2. Increased inflow: splanchnic vasodilation (NO) dilates gut vessels → more blood flows into the portal vein (hyperdynamic circulation).

Remember: Resistance + inflow = portal HTN. Resistance from scar + contraction; inflow from vasodilation.

Mechanism in brief: resistance rises mechanically (scar squeezing the sinusoids, capillarization) and dynamically (stellate cell contraction, endothelin-1). At the same time splanchnic vasodilation increases portal blood flow, pushing the pressure further up. Above 12 mmHg, collaterals open and varices form — the threshold where variceal bleeding and ascites become clinically significant.

Sources: PMC — Portal hypertension mechanisms; Robbins ch.18

A 50-year-old male, known HBV carrier, presents with weakness and tiredness for 2 months. On examination: hepatomegaly. USG shows cirrhosis with a space-occupying lesion in the right lobe. What is your diagnosis? How will you confirm?

PBL — HCC on cirrhosis in a chronic HBV carrier

Diagnosis: Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) on a background of cirrhosis (HBV-related).

Why (VIVA must):

  • Chronic HBV → cirrhosis → HCC is the classic sequence; HBV additionally integrates into host DNA (HBx protein) so it can cause HCC even before cirrhosis.
  • Age 50 + hepatomegaly + constitutional symptoms (weakness) in a known carrier.
  • USG: cirrhotic liver + space-occupying lesion (SOL) = HCC until proven otherwise.

Confirmation — VIVA must:

  • Serum AFP ↑ (> 400 ng/mL is very suggestive, but ~40% of HCCs have normal AFP).
  • Triple-phase CT / MRI — arterial enhancement + portal-venous washout is the hallmark vascular signature (HCC is arterial-fed).
  • Liver biopsy (if imaging is unclear) — trabecular pattern, bile production, HepPar-1 positive.
  • HBV/HCV serology, LFTs, CBC.

Differentials to exclude: liver abscess (common in Bangladesh — fever, pus on USG), metastasis, cholangiocarcinoma, hemangioma.

Treatment & surveillance: resection/transplant (early), TACE, sorafenib/lenvatinib/atezolizumab-bevacizumab (advanced). Surveillance: USG + AFP every 6 months in all HBV carriers.

Mechanism in brief: chronic HBV drives necroinflammation → cirrhosis → repeated regeneration with DNA damage, and HBV DNA can integrate near cancer genes and express HBx, which alters growth — so HCC risk is high. AFP is a fetal protein that HCC often produces, but normal AFP cannot exclude it. On triple-phase imaging the tumour 'lights up' in the arterial phase (it is fed by arteries, unlike the normal portal-fed liver) and washes out in the portal/venous phase — a specific HCC signature. Biopsy gives definitive proof when imaging is atypical.

Sources: PMC — HCC pathology; Robbins ch.18
