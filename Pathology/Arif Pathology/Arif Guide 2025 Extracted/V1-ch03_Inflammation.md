Ans.

Goal of inflammation:
1. To get rid of initial cause of cell injury (e.g. microbes, toxins etc.)
2. To get rid of consequences of cell injury (e.g. necrotic cells and tissues).
(Ref: Robbins/10"/71)

ingest and destroy microbes and dead cells.
4. ‘Regulation of the response is important for. terminating the reaction when it has accomplished its purpose.

Q. What are the major components of inflammation? (***Very Important for VIVA***)
Ans

Major components of inflammation: Inflammation has 2 main components:
1. Vascular - Blood vessels

2. Cellular - Leukocytes (White Blood Cells).

Vascular (exudative) component involves dilatation of blood vessels (causing redness and heat) and
movement of fluid containing many selected proteins such as fibrin and immunoglobulins (antibodies) from
the intravascular space into the extravascular damaged tissue / site of infection (vascular leakage) giving

rise to edema (exudative edema) or swelling. The swelling distends the tissues, compresses nerve endings, and
So causes pain.

Cellular component involves the movement of leukocytes (White Blood Cells) from blood vessels into the
site of infection / focus of injury (leukocyte extravasation), and act as phagocytes, picking up microbes, dead
cells, foreign bodies and other unwanted materials in the tissues.

(Ref: Robbins/10"/72; Shahzad G. Raja/258)

Introduction to Acute & Chronic Inflammation

Q. Define acute inflammation. (***Very Important for VIVA***)

Ans.

Acute inflammation: Acute inflammation is a rapid, often self-limited response to offending agents that are
readily eliminated (such as many bacteria & fungi, and dead cells), typically develops within minutes or
hours and is of short duration (lasting for several hours to a few days), characterized by the exudation of
fluid and plasma proteins (edema) and the emigration of leukocytes, predominantly neutrophils (also
called polymorphonuclear leukocytes).

(Ref: Robbins/10"/73)

If the offending stimulus is eliminated, the reaction subsides, and residual injury is repaired.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/73)

21M,17Ju)

Q. Short note: Fates of acute inflammation. (CMU-22Nv, RU-18M) (V.V./. for VIVA)

Ans.

Fates / outcomes of acute inflammation: All acute inflammatory reactions typically have one of three
outcomes-

1. Complete resolution (restoration of the site of acute inflammation to normal)

= When the injury is limited or short-lived
= When there has been little tissue destruction
= When the damaged parenchymal cells can regenerate.

Resolution involves removal of cellular debris and microbes by macrophages and resorption of
edema fluid by lymphatics, followed by regeneration of the damaged tissue.

2. Healing by connective tissue replacement (scarring, or fibrosis). This occurs when the injured
tissue cannot regenerate.

3. Progression to chronic inflammation. This occurs owing to the persistence of the injurious agents or
to some interference of the normal process of healing (e.g. pneumonia to chronic lung abscess).

(Ref: Robbins/10"/95)

(N.B: Most of the time, when the acute inflammation fulfills it’s goals, that is to stop or contain the offending
agent and also to remove the damaged tissue, usually acute inflammation subsides, However if the acute
inflammatory reaction fails to remove the offending agent, the reaction may progress to a further prolonges
Phase, and that is known as chronic inflammation.)

Q. What are the morphological patterns of acute inflammation? (RMU-23M, CU-15/14Ju,06S)
Q. Write the morphological types of acute inflammation with example of each. (CMU-21Nv)
Q. Classify acute inflammation morphologically. (SU-14Ju, RU-13Ja)

Ans.

Morphological types of acute inflammation:

1. Serous inflammation: It is marked by the exudation of cell poor fluid into spaces created by cell
injury or into body cavities lined by the peritoneum, pleura, or pericardium. Accumulation of fluid
in these cavities is called an effusion (e.g. pleural effusion). The skin blister resulting from a burn or
viral infection is another example.

2, Fibrinous inflammation: Fibrinous exudate develops when the vascular leaks are large enough to
pass large molecules such as fibrinogen out of the blood, e.g. fibrinous pericarditis after acute
myocardial infarction; also occur in meninges, pleura etc.

3. Purulent (suppurative) inflammation and abscess:

* Purulent (suppurative) inflammation is characterized by the production of pus or purulent
exudates consisting of neutrophils, the liquefied debris of necrotic cells, and edema fluid. It is
caused by pyogenic (pus-producing) bacteria e.g. staphylococci. A common example of an acute
suppurative inflammation is acute appendicitis.

« Abscesses are localized collections of pus caused by suppuration buried in a tissue, an organ, or
a confined space. They are produced by seeding of pyogenic bacteria into a tissue.

4. Ulcers: An ulcer is a local defect, or excavation, of the surface of an organ or tissue that is produced
by the sloughing (shedding) of inflamed necrotic tissue e.g. peptic ulcer of stomach or duodenum (in

which acute and chronic inflammation coexist), pressure sore, leg ulcer etc.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/93)

Cellular events in acute inflammation: (Very Important for VIVA)

A. Leukocyte extravasation: The sequence of events in the journey of leukocytes (neutrophils &

monocytes) from the vessel lumen to the interstitial tissue, called extravasation is a multi-step
process that is mediated and controlled by adhesion molecules and cytokines called chemokines.

This process can be divided into the following steps:

1. In the lumen:

* Margination: Early in inflammation, as stasis develops, the leukocytes which normally are in
the centre assume a peripheral position along the endothelial surface. This process of
leukocyte accumulation is called margination.

Margination is basically a redistribution of leukocytes along the margins of the blood vessels.

* Rolling: Immediately after margination, the leukocytes adhere transiently (loose adhesion) to
the endothelium; detach and bind again, and then detach and bind again; and this particular
process is referred to as rolling. This rolling interaction is mediated by a family of proteins
called selectins.

" Adhesion: Firm attachment of leukocytes to the endothelium is called adhesion which is
mediated by a family of leukocyte surface of proteins called integrin.

2. Across the endothelium: Transmigration (also called diapedesis) (migration of the leukocytes
through the inter-endothelial spaces by squeezing themselves is called transmigration or diapedesis).

recognize and then engulf and dispose off foreign particles (e.g. bacteria).

1. Recognition and attachment of the particle to be ingested by the leukocyte

Q. Define abscess.

Q. Short note: Abscess.

Ans.

Abscess: Abscesses are localized collections of pus caused by suppuration buried in a tissue, an organ, ora
confined space.

Cause: They are produced by deep seeding of pyogenic bacteria into a tissue.

Histology:
1. A central region that appears as a mass of necrotic leukocytes & tissue cells.

2. There is a zone of preserved neutrophils around this necrotic focus.

3. Outside this region vascular dilation & parenchymal & fibroblastic proliferation occur, indicating the

4. In time it may become walled off and ultimately replaced by connective tissue.

splenic abscess, cold abscess in TB, lung abscess etc.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/94)

Q. Define ulcer.

Ulcer: An ulcer is a local defect, or excavation, of the surface of an organ or tissue that is produced by the
sloughing (shedding) of inflamed necrotic tissue.

Common sites: It is most commonly encountered in-
1. The mucosa of the mouth, stomach, intestines, or genitourinary tract, and
2. The skin and subcutaneous tissue of the lower extremities in older persons who have circulatory
disturbances that predispose to extensive ischemic necrosis.

Example: Peptic ulcer of the stomach or duodenum, mouth ulcers (aphthous ulcers or canker sores), pressure
ulcers (bedsores or decubitus ulcers), diabetic foot ulcers, genital ulcers, arterial / venous ulcers, corneal ulcers

etc.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/94)

Ans.

Morphology of granuloma / caseating granuloma / soft tubercle of tuberculosis:

1. A granuloma comprises an organized aggregation of activated macrophages; because they resemble
epithelial cells, they are called epithelioid cells.

2. Epithelioid cells have a pink granular cytoplasm with indistinct cell boundaries, often appearing to
merge into one another

3. The aggregates of epitheloid macrophages are surrounded by a collar of lymphocytes.

4. Frequently, multiple activated macrophages fuse to form multinucleated giant cells in the periphery
or sometimes in the center of granulomas.

5. These giant cells may attain diameters of 40-50 pm. They consists of a large mass of cytoplasm and
many nuclei arranged either peripherally (Langhans-type giant cell) or haphazardly (foreign-body
type giant cell).

6. Caseating granulomas / soft tubercle (the characteristic hallmark of TB) have characteristic central
caseation necrosis (so called because of its dry cheese-like appearance).

7. Older granulomas develop an enclosing rim of fibroblasts and connective tissue (indicates healing by
fibrosis).

(Ref: Robbins/10"/100; Microbiology Lecture, SSMC)

Systemic effects of inflammation / acute phase response: Cytokine-induced systemic reactions in

Or,
The systemic changes associated with acute inflammation are collectively called the “acute phase

Clinical and pathological changes: The ‘‘acute phase response”’ consists of several clinical and pathological changes.

1. Fever: Fever is produced in response to pyrogens, that stimulate synthesis of prostaglandin in
hypothalamus. Pyrogens may be exogenous or endogenous. Exogenous pyrogens are bacterial
products such as LPS that stimulate leucocytes to release endogenous pyrogens which are cytokines
such as IL-1 and TNF.

2. Production of acute-phase proteins: Acute-phase proteins are plasma proteins mostly synthesized
in the liver in response to cytokines-IL-6 (for CRP and fibrinogen) and IL-1 or TNF (for SAA).
About 12 proteins are produced. Three important ones are-
= (C-reactive protein (CRP)

» Serum amyloid A protein (SAA)
* Fibronogen

3. Leukocytosis: It is a common feature of bacterial infection. Leukocytosis occurs due to accelerated
release of leucocytes from bone marrow reserve pool by IL-1 and TNF and proliferation of leukocyte
precursors in the bone marrow by increased production of colony stimulating factor (CSF) induced by
IL-1 and TNF.
