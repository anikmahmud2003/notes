Liver Failure

Q. Mention the clinical features and complications of liver failure.

Q. Short note: Liver failure.

Ans.

Liver failure: The most severe form of liver disease is liver failure, which may be acute (due to sudden and
massive hepatic destruction) or (more commonly) chronic, following years of insidious, progressive liver injury.

In some cases, individuals with chronic liver disease develop acute-on-chronic liver failure, in which an
unrelated acute injury is superimposed on late-stage chronic disease or there is a “flare” of the chronic
disease that leads to liver decompensation.

Whatever the sequence, 80% to 90% of functional capacity must be lost before hepatic failure appears.

Q. What do you mean by hepatic encephalopathy? (DU-18M,16Ju)
Q. Short note: Hepatic encephalopathy. (Very Important for VIVA)
Ans.

Hepatic encephalopathy: Hepatic encephalopathy is a spectrum of disturbances in consciousness, ranging
from subtle behavioral abnormalities, to marked confusion and stupor, to deep coma and death.

It may progress over days, weeks, or months following acute hepatic injury.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/827)

Asustated fluctuating neurologic signs:
Rigidity and hyperreflexia.
*  Asterixis / flapping tremor (a particularly characteristic sign, which is manifested as nonrhythmic,
rapid extension-flexion movements of the head and extremities, best seen when the arms arc held in
extension with dorsiflexed wrists).

Fig: Asterixis / flapping tremor.

Pathogenesis: Hepatic encephalopathy is believed to be caused by elevated ammonia levels, which correlate
with impaired neuronal function and cerebral edema.

The principal source of the ammonia is the gastrointestinal tract, where it is produced by microorganisms
and also by enterocytes during glutamine metabolism. Normally, ammonia is transported in the portal vein to
the liver, where it is metabolized in the urea cycle; in severe liver disease, this detoxification mechanism fails.

(Ref: Robbins/10"/827)

Q. What do you mean by hepatorenal syndrome? (Very Important for VIVA)
Ans.

Hepatorenal syndrome: It is a form of renal failure occurring in individuals with liver failure in whom there
is no intrinsic morphologic or functional cause for kidney dysfunction.

The syndrome’s onset is marked by a drop in urine output and increasing levels of urea and creatinine in the blood.

Sodium retention, impaired free-water excretion, and decreased renal perfusion and glomerular filtration rate
are the main renal functional abnormalities.

The decline in renal function is reversible if liver function is restored, for example, by liver transplantation.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/827)

Cirrhosis of liver: Cirrhosis may be defined as the end-stage of chronic liver disease which is characterized by-

1. Bridging fibrous septae in the form of delicate bands or broad scars linking portal tracts with one
another and portal tracts with terminal hepatic veins

2. Parenchymal nodules containing proliferating hepatocytes encircled by fibrosis, with diameters

3. Disruption of the architecture of the entire liver.
(Ref: Robbins/8"/837)

Cirrhosis (end-stage chronic liver disease) is a condition marked by diffuse remodeling of the liver into
parenchymal nodules (often regencrative) surrounded by fibrous bands and a variable degree of vascular
(often portosystemic) shunting.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/828)

Cirrhosis refers to the diffue transformation of the liver into regenerative parenchymal nodules surrounded

Q. Why hypoproteinemic edema occurs in cirrhosis of liver? (RU-07Ja)
Ans.

Hypoproteinemic edema occurs in cirrhosis of liver: Liver is the principal site of protein (mainly albumin)

synthesis. In cirrhosis, the normal architecture of liver is altered. There is degeneration and intracellular
accumulation in the liver. The hepatocytes undergo necrosis and apoptosis. So, protein (albumin) synthesis is
decreased. Plasma oncotic pressure is decreased. As a result hypoproteinemic edema occurs.

(Ref: Robbins/10"/829; Davidson’s/23"/863)

Free copper is normally taken up by hepatocytes and incorporated into apoceruloplasmin to form

Q. Short note: Wilson disease. (Important for VIVA)

ATP7B decreases copper transport into bile, impairs its incorporation into ceruloplasmin, and inhibits
ceruloplasmin secretion into the blood. These defects lead to a decrease in circulating ceruloplasmin
accompanicd by the accumulation of copper in hepatocytes.

Acute cholecystitis: It is an acute inflammation of the gall bladder, precipitated in 90% of cases by
obstruction of the neck of the gall bladder or the cystic duct by a gallstone.

Risk factors for acute cholecystitis:

Q. What are the morphological changes in chronic cholecystitis? (CMU-22M, RU-19Nv)

Ans,

Chronic cholecystitis: Chronic inflammation of the gallbladder due to chemical irritation from longstanding
cholelithiasis, with or without superimposed bouts of acute cholecystitis.

Morphological changes in chronic cholecystitis: The morphologic changes in chronic cholecystitis are
extremely variable and sometimes minimal.

Gross:

Gallbladder may be contracted, of normal size, or enlarged.

Thickening of the wall as a result of extensive fibrosis

Serosa is usually smooth and glistening.

Subserosa and submucosa are often thickened from fibrosis (mucosa itself is generally preserved)
Occassionally the lumen contains green-yellow, mucoid bile and usually stones.

On sectioning, the wall is variably thickened, and has an opaque gray-white appearance.

Microscopic:

= Degree of inflammation is variable.

= Gallbladder mucosa and subserosal fibrous tissue may be infiltrated by chronic inflammatory cells
(e.g. lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophage etc.).

* Rokitansky-Aschoff sinuses (pseudodiverticula or pockets in the wall of the gallbladder / entrapped
epithelial crypts) may be found, which is characterized by outpouchings / herniation of gallbladder
mucosa into the muscular wall.

* Extensive dystrophic calcification within the wall may yield a porcelain gallbladder, which is
characterized by shrunken, hard gallbladder due to chronic inflammation & fibrosis.

= An atrophic, chronically obstructed, dilated gallbladder containing clear secretions (a condition known
as hydrops of the gallbladder) & xanthoma cells (lipid containing cells with foamy cytoplasm) may
be found in xanthogranulomatous cholecystitis.

(Ref: Robbins/10"/876; BRS Pathology/5'/255)

Q. Mention the morphology of carcinoma of gall bladder.
Ans.

Morphology of carcinoma of gall bladder: Carcinomas of the gall bladder show two patterns of growth-
infiltrating and exophytic.

= Infiltrating pattern: It is more common and appears diffuse thickening and induration of the
gallbladder wall. Deep ulceration can cause direct penetration of the gall bladder wall or fistula
formation to adjacent viscera into which the neoplasm has grown.

= Exophytic pattern: It grows into the lumen as an irregular, cauliflower mass. The luminal portion
may be necrotic, hemorrhagic, and ulcerated. The most common sites of involvement are the fundus

and the neck; about 20% involves the lateral walls.
(Ref: Robbins/10'"/877 + 9'"/880)
