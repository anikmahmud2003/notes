Q. Mention the morphology of non-proliferative breast changes (fibrocystic changes). (CU-03Ja)
Ans.
Morphology of non-proliferative breast changes (fibrocystic changes): There are three principal

morphologic changes: a) cystic change, often with apocrine metaplasia; b) fibrosis; and c) adenosis.

1. Cysts: Cysts are lined either by a flattened atrophic epithelium or by metaplastic apocrine cells.
Metastatic cells have an abundant granular, eosinophilic cytoplasm and round nuclei, resembling the
normal apocrine epithelium of sweat glands. Calcifications are common.

2. Fibrosis: Cysts frequently rupture, with release of secretory material into the adjacent stroma. The
resulting chronic inflammation and fibrosis contribute to palpable nodularity of the breast.

3. Adenosis: It is defined as an increase in the number of acini per lobule. It is a normal feature of
pregnancy. In nonpregnant women, adenosis can occur as a focal change. The acini are lined by
columnar epithelial cells, and calcifications are occasionally present within the lumens.

(Ref: Robbins/10'"/1042)

Q. Write down the risk factors involved in breast cancer. (DU-08Ju, SU-20Nv, CU-20Nv)
(V.V.L for VIVA & MCQ)
Ans.

Risk factors for the development of breast cancer:
1. Gender: 99% of affected patients are female.

2. Age:
" Incidence increases with increasing age (peaking at the age of 70-80 years).

= Rarely found before the age of 25 years except in some familial cases.

3. Germline mutations: Inherited mutations in the p53, BRCA-1, or BRCA-2 tumor suppressor genes are
associated with increased risk.

4. First-degree relatives with breast cancer: First-degree relatives (mother, sister, daughter) of women
with breast cancer have 2 to 6-fold higher risk of development of breast cancer.

5. Geographic and racial factors: Incidence of breast cancer is about 4-6 times higher in developed
countries (North America, North Europe, Australia), and low in developing countries of Asia and
Africa.

6. Early menarche and late menopause (increases estrogen exposure): Menarche at ages younger
than 11 years increases risk by 20% compared to menarche at ages greater than 14. Late menopause
also increases risk.

Age at first pregnancy: Nulliparity & first pregnancy after 30 years of age increases the risk.

4. Benign breast disease: Proliferative fibrocystic disease with atypical epithelial hyperplasia increases
the risk of developing invasive carcinoma.

5. Estrogen exposure: Postmenopausal hormone replacement therapy (HRT) increases the risk of breast
cancer, particularly when estrogen and a progestin are given together for a period of years. Oral
contraceptives are unlikely to increase the risk of breast cancer.

6. High breast density: High breast density is potentially susceptible to neoplastic transformation. Dense
breast also make detection of cancer more difficult by mammography.

7. Radiation exposure: Radiation of the chest, whether due to cancer therapy, atomic bomb exposure, or
nuclear accidents, results in a higher rate of breast cancer.

Q. Outline the prognostic factors involved in breast carcinoma. (DU-23/19Nv,16/14Ju, BUP-
19M, SU-08Ju)

Ans.

Prognostic factors of breast carcinoma:
A. Major prognostic factors:

1.

Invasive carcinoma versus in situ disease:

* In situ carcinoma is confined to the ductal system & cannot metastasize. So, great majority of
DICS will be cured with adequate treatment.

= In contrast, at least half of invasive carcinomas will have metastasized locally or distantly at

the time of diagnosis. Their prognosis is bad.
Distant metastases: Once distant metastases are present, cure is unlikely.
Lymph node metastases: Axillary lymph node status is the most important prognostic factor for
invasive carcinoma in the absence of distant metastasis. The less the lymph node metastasis, the
more the chance of good prognosis.
Tumor size: The size of the carcinoma is the second most important prognostic factor and is
independent from lymph node status. The more the size of the cancer, the prognosis is bad.
Locally advanced diseases: Carcinomas invading into skin or skeletal muscle are usually large
and may be difficult to treat surgically.
Inflammatory carcinoma: Breast cancer presenting with breast swelling and skin thickening due
to dermal lymphatic involvement have a particularly poor prognosis.
Lymphovascular invasion: Tumor cells are present within vascular spaces (either lymphatics or
small capillaries) in about half of all invasive carcinomas. This finding is strongly associated with

the presence of lymph node metastases. It is a poor prognostic factor for overall survival in women
without lymph node metastases and a risk factor for local recurrence.

B. Minor prognostic factors:

1.

Molecular subtype: The molecular subtype, determined by expression of ER and HER2 and
proliferation, is an important prognostic factor.

Special histologic types: The survival rate of women with some special types of invasive
carcinomas (tubular, mucinous, lobular, papillary, adenoid cystic) is greater than that of women
with cancers of no special type.

Histologic grade: All invasive carcinomas are graded using the Nottingham Histologic Score that
classifies invasive carcinomas into three groups (grade I, II, III) that are highly correlated with
disease free and overall survival.

Proliferative rate: Tumors with high proliferation rates have a poorer prognosis but may respond
better to chemotherapy.

Q. What are the causes of gynaecomastia?

Q. Short note: Gynaecomastia. (RMU-23/20M, 18Nv,17Nv/Ju,14Ju) (Important for VIVA)
Ans.

Gynaecomastia: Enlargement of the male breast is known as gynaecomastia.

It may be unilateral and bilateral and presents as a button-like subareolar enlargement. In advanced cases, the
swelling can simulate the adolescent female breast.

Pathogenesis: Gynaecomastia may occur as a result of an imbalance between estrogens, which stimulates
breast tissue, and androgens, which counteract these effects.

Causes:
1. Pubertal gynaecomastia (young boys between 13 and 17 years of age)
2. Failure of the androgenic functions of the testis (in older males)
3. Cirrhosis of liver
4. Testicular tumours
5. Pituitary tumours
6. Carcinoma of the lung
7. Exogenous oestrogen therapy as in carcinoma of the prostate
8. Testicular atrophy in Klinefelter’s syndrome (secondary gynaecomastia)
9.

Drugs (such as alcohol, marijuana, heroin, antiretroviral therapy, anabolic steroids used by some
athletes and body builders, and some psychoactive agents)
10. Idiopathic gynaecomastia (without any obvious cause).
