Q. Short note: Achalasia. (Important for VIVA)
Ans.
Achalasia: Disordered esophageal motility with inability to relax the lower esophageal sphincter (LES).
Or,
Achalasia is persistent contraction of the lower esophageal sphincter and absence of esophageal pcristalsis,
leading to dilation of the esophagus.
Or,
It is a neuromuscular dysfunction of the oesophagus due to which the cardiac sphincter fails to relax during
swallowing and results in progressive dysphagia and dilatation of the oesophagus (mega-oesophagus).

Achalasia is characterized by the triad of-
* Incomplete lower esophageal sphincter relaxation
= Increased lower esophageal sphincter tone
=  Aperistalsis of the esophagus.

Types & causes of achalasia:

1. Primary achalasia: It is rare and results from degeneration of nitric oxide—producing neurons that
normally induce lower esophageal sphincter relaxation. Degenerative changes in the extraesophageal
vagus nerve or the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus may also occur.

2. Secondary achalasia: It may arise in Chagas disease, in which Trypanosoma cruzi infection causes
destruction of the myenteric plexus, failure of peristalsis, and esophageal dilation.

Clinical features:

Dysphagia (difficulty in swallowing) for solids and liquids
Putrid breath

High LES pressure on esophageal manometry

‘Bird-beak’ sign on barium swallow study

Increased risk of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma

eS SS =

Treatment: Treatments for both primary and secondary achalasia aim to overcome the obstruction and include-
* Laparoscopic myotomy
Pneumatic balloon dilatation
* Botulinum neurotixin (Botox) injection (to inhibit contraction-promoting cholinergic neurons).
(Ref: Robbins/10°/757; BRS Pathology/5"/228; Pathoma/2014/102; Harsh Mohan/7"/521)

Q. Short note: Reflux oesophagitis /GERD. (Important for VIVA)
Ans.
Reflux esophagitis: Reflux of gastric acid into the lower esophagus duc to reduced lower esophageal
sphincter tone is termed as gastro-esophageal reflux disease (GERD).
Or,
GERD is the reflux of gastric contents into the lower esophagus.

{N.B: It is the opposite of achalasia, where LES (lower esophageal sphincter) tone is increased}.

Causes: Conditions that decrease LES tone or increase abdominal pressure contribute to GERD:
1. Transient lower esophageal sphincter relaxation (mediated via vagal pathways)
2. Abrupt increases in intra-abdominal pressure (c.g. after coughing, straining, or bending)
3. Other conditions that are associated with GERD include:

Alcohol and tobacco use

Obesity

Fat-rich diet

Caffeine

Centralnervous system depressants

Pregnancy

Hiatal hernia

Delayed gastric emptying

Increased gastric volume.

Clinical features of GERD: Typical symptoms include-
1, Heartburn (mimics cardiac chest pain, which may be mistaken for ischemic heart disease)
2. Dysphagia
3. Regurgitation of sour-tasting gastric contents (most often postprandially)
4. Asthma (adult-onset) and cough
5. Damage to enamel of teeth.

Treatment: Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs).

Complications:
1, Esophagitis
2. Esophageal ulceration with stricture
3. Barrett esophagus (columnar metaplasia of esophageal squamous epithelium).

tology:

1. Mild GERD: Simple hyperemia, evidenced by redness.

2. More significant gastric reflux is associated with erosions and influx of eosinophils into the

Hiatal hernia: Hiatus hemia is the herniation or protrusion of part of the stomach through the oesophageal
hiatus of the diaphragm.

It may cause lower esophageal sphincter incompetence and symptoms that are similar to GERD but it is
symptomatic in fewer than 10% of adults.

Anatomical types: There are 3 patterns in hiatus hernia-
1. Sliding or oesophago-gastric hernia (most common, occurring in 85% of cases): The herniated part
of the stomach appears as supra-diaphragmatic bell due to sliding up on both sides of the oesophagus

2. Rolling or para-oesophageal hernia (10% of cases): This is a true hernia in which cardiac end of the
stomach rolls up para-oesophageally, producing an intrathoracic sac.

3. Mixed or transitional hernia constitutes the remaining 5% cases in which there is combination of
sliding and rolling hiatus hernia.

Q. Short note: Barrett esophagus. (DU-06S/Ja, CMU-21Nv,20M) (Important for VIVA)

Ans.

Barrett esophagus: Barrett esophagus is a long-standing complication of chronic gastro-esophageal reflux
disease (GERD) that is characterized by intestinal metaplasia within the esophageal squamous mucosa and
is associated with an increased risk of cancer.

It is the single most important risk factors for esophageal adenocarcinoma.

Incidence: It is estimated to occur in as many as 10% of individuals with symptomatic GERD. Peak age is
40-60 years of age.

Pathogenesis: In Barrett esophagus, the distal squamous epithelium is replaced by metastatic columner
epithelium as a response to prolonged injury.

Barrett’s oesophagus is a premalignant condition evolving sequentially-from Barrett’s epithelium
(columnar metaplasia with goblet cells) to dysplasia to carcinoma in situ and finally to oesophageal
adenocarcinoma.

Fig: Barret’s esophagus.

Q. Short note: Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD). (RU-17M,09Ja)
Ans.
Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD): Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic condition resulting from
complex interactions between intestinal microbiota and host immunity in genetically predisposed
individuals that leads to inappropriate mucosal immune activation.

(Ref: Robbins/10"/798)

The two disorders that comprise IBD are ulcerative colitis and Crohn disease.

Ulcerative colitis involves only the colon and rectum and is generally limited to the mucosa and
submucosa. In contrast, Crohn disease, which has also been referred to as regional enteritis (because of
frequent ileal involvement) may involve any area of the GI tract and is typically transmural.

(Ref: Robbins/10"/799)

Etiology and pathogenesis: IBD results from a combination of abnormalities in immune regulation, host-
microbe interactions, and epithelial barrier functions in genetically susceptible individuals.

The genetic and environmental elements that contribute to disease:

1. Mucosal immunity: A plethora of immune signaling and regulatory genes including those encoding
HLA molecules and cytokines have been associated with IBD.

2. Autophagy and cellular stress responses: Genetic associations and molecular analyses indicate that
defects in autophagy and cellular stress responses contribute to IBD pathogenesis.

3. Host-microbial interactions: The gut microbiome (composed of bacteria, fungi, and viruses) can
modulate mucosal immunity. Conversely, the immune system can markedly alter the microbiome by
mechanisms that include luminal secretion of antimicrobial peptides and antibacterial IgA. This

interplay between microbes and the immune system may contribute to IBD pathogenesis.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/799)

N ow:

Autophagy: Autophagy is a normal homeostatic mechanism that clears damaged organelles and is
upregulated in response to cellular stress including nutrient deprivation and endoplasmic reticulum stress.

Autophagy is also a means of clearing sources of reactive oxygen species and intracellular pathogens.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/800)

Q. Write down the pathogenesis of colorectal carcinoma. (CMU-21M)

Ans.

Pathogenesis of colorectal carcinoma:

1. APC mutation/p-catenin mechanism:
= Loss of tumour suppressor APC (adenomatous polyposis coli) gene (80% cases of sporadic colon

cancer). Since the function of APC gene is linked to B-catenin, loss of APC gene results in
translocation of B-catenin to the nucleus where it activates transcription of other genes, mainly
MYC and cyclin D1, both of which stimulate cell proliferation.

= Point mutation in K-RAS gene follows loss of APC gene (seen in 10 to 50% cases of adenoma-
carcinoma).

= Deletion of DCC gene (60-70% cases of colon cancer).
= Loss of p53 tumour suppressor gene (70-80% cases of colon cancer).

2. Microsatellite instability (MSI) mechanism: This pathway accounts for 10-15% cases of colon
cancer. Basic mutation is loss of DNA repair gene. This results in a situation in which repetitive DNA
sequences (i.e. microsatellites) become unstable during replication cycle, termed microsatellite
instability (MSI), which is the hallmark of this pathway.

(Ref: Harsh Mohan/7°/588)
