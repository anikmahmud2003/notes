Q. Give modern definition of neoplasm. (CMU-23Nv/M)

Q. Define tumour (Willis definition). (SU-06Ja, CU-04S,03Ja, RU-03Ja)

Ans. (Very**** Important for V/VA)

Neoplasm / tumour: Neoplasia means “new growth,” and the collection of cells and stroma composing new

growths are referred to as neoplasms.

In the pre-molecular era, the eminent British oncologist Willis defines neoplasm / tumour (Willis
definition): “A neoplasm is an abnormal mass of tissue, the growth of which exceeds and is uncoordinated
with that of the normal tissues and persists in the same excessive manner after cessation of the stimuli which
evoked the change.”

(Ref: Robbins/9""/266)

In the modern era, a neoplasm is defined as a genetic disorder of cell growth that is triggered by acquired or
less commonly inherited mutations affecting a single cell and its clonal progeny.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/268)

Example:
* Benign neoplasm arising from fibrous tissue is called fibroma; whereas malignant neoplasm is

called fibrosarcoma.

« Benign neoplasm arising from bone is called osteoma; whereas malignant neoplasm is called
osteosarcoma.

* Benign neoplasm arising from cartilage is called chondroma; whereas malignant neoplasm is called
chondrosarcoma.

* Benign neoplasm arising from glandular tissue is called adenoma; whereas malignant neoplasm is
called adenocarcinoma.

Q. Short note: Lipoma. (CMU-21/20Nv)

Ans.

Lipoma: Lipoma is the benign tumour of adipose tissue. It is the commonest soft tissue tumour, which
appears as a solitary, soft, movable and painless mass which may remain stationary or grow slowly. A lipoma
rarely ever transforms into /iposarcoma (malignant tumours of adipose tissue).

Age of incidence: Lipomas occur most often in 4" to 5" decades of life and are frequent in females.

Site: They may be found at different locations in the body but most common sites are the subcutaneous tissues
in the neck, back and shoulder.

Variants of lipoma:

Angiolipoma (combination with proliferating blood vessels)

Myelolipoma (admixture with bone marrow elements as seen in adrenals)

Pleomorphic (atypical) lipoma.

* Grossly, a subcutaneous lipoma is usually small, round to oval and encapsulated mass. The cut surface
is soft, lobulated, yellowish-orange and greasy.
* Histologically, the tumour is composed of lobules of mature adipose cells separated by delicate fibrous
septa. A thin fibrous capsule surrounds the tumour.

Q. Short note: Teratoma. (RU-20Nv,08Ja, CU-05Ja)

Ans.
Teratoma: Teratoma refers to a group of complex tumors arise from totipotential cells, composed of
recognizable mature or immature cells or tissues belonging to more than one germ cell layer (and

sometimes all three).

(Ref: Robbins/10"/269)

Characteristics of teratoma: (V\V.I. For VIVA)

=  Teratomas originate from totipotent cells which are normally present in the gonads (ovary and
testis), and sometimes abnormally present in sequestered embryonic cell rests in midline locations
(extra-gonadal) such as the mediastinum, retroperitoneum, head, neck, and sacrococcygeal region.

= Teratoma is made up of a variety of parenchymal cells or tissues representing more than one germ
cell layer and often all three:

a) Ectoderm such as stratified squamous epithelial cells, skin, sebaceous glands, hair, tooth.
b) Endoderm such as gut epithelium.
c) Mesoderm such as muscle, fat, bone, cartilage.

* Skin and its appendages, tooth, gut epithelium, muscle, fat and any other tissue of the body may be
present in a teratoma.

s They may occur at any age from infancy to adult life.

s Teratomas may be benign or malignant. When all the components are well differentiated it is a
benign or mature teratoma, e.g. ovarian dermoid cyst (cystic teratoma). When a component is less
well-differentiated it is a malignant or immature teratoma.

(Ref: Robbins/10"/476,477; Prof. Khaleque/50)

Q. What is hamartoma? (RU-15/13Ja)

Q. Short note: Hamartoma. (RU-18M/Ja,14/10Ja) (Very Important For VIVA)
Ans.
Hamartoma: Hamartomas are disorganized masses composed of cells indigenous to the involved tissue.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/269)
Or,
The term hamartoma refers to an excessive, focal overgrowth of cells and tissues native to the organ in which
it occurs.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/475)
Or,
Hamartoma is a tumour-like developmental malformation formed by local tissue or tissues which are
arranged haphazardly.
(Ref: Prof. Khaleque/50)

Characteristics of hamartoma:
* It is a focal overgrowth of mature, normal tissues in abnormal proportions.
Usually there is an excess of one or more of its components
There is no capsule around a hamartoma
In any event, they are considered totally benign.
Although these might be considered neoplasms, the complete resemblance of the tissue to normal
cartilage or blood vessels and the occasional admixture of other elements suggest that the lesions
reflect anomalous development.

Example: A hamartoma in the lung may contain islands of cartilage, blood vessels, bronchial-type structures,
and lymphoid tissue. Sometimes the lesion is purely cartilaginous or purely angiomatous. But they do not form

organoid structure of lung tissue.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/269,475; Prof. Khaleque/50)

Q. Define benign tumour. (V.V.I. for VIVA)
Ans.
Benign tumour: A tumor is said to be benign when its gross and microscopic appearances are considered
relatively innocent, implying that it will remain localized at its site of origin and is generally amenable to
surgical removal.

(Ref: Robbins/10"/268)

Predictably, the patient generally survives. However, “benign” tumors may cause significant morbidity and
are sometimes even fatal, when it occurs in vulnerable locations such as the brain.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/268)

Characteristics of benign tumour / neoplasm: (V.V.I. for VIVA)
1. Differentiation: Benign tumours are well-differentiated. An example- leiomyoma resembles normal
smooth muscle. There is no undifferentiation (anaplasia). Mitotic figures are normal and rare.

2. (a) Rate of growth: The growth of most benign tumours is slow, (b) Size: Benign tumours are usually
small in size. But leiomyoma of the uterus and ovarian cystadenomas may be very large in size, (¢c)
Stroma: Well-formed.

3. (a) Capsule: Most benign tumours form capsule composed of surrounding rim of compressed
connective tissue. Capsule separates the tumour from the host tissue. Encapsulation leads to a discrete,
readily palpable and easily movable mass and can be surgically enucleated. But leiomyoma of the
uterus, haemangioma and surface growths (papilomas and adenomas) are not encapsulated, (b) No
local invasion in the surrounding tissue. Remain localized at the site of origin and do not have the
capacity to invade or infiltrate. \

Features of malignant tumour: (V.V.I. for VIVA)
1.

Differentiation and anaplasia: Malignant tumours exhibit variable differentiation ranging from
well differentiated (e.g. adenocarcinoma of thyroid) to poorly differentiated to anaplastic /

undifferentiated (most of the malignant tumours). Anaplasia is considered as a hallmark of
malignancy.

Anaplasia or lack of differentiation is marked by morphologic changes of anaplastic cells:

= Pleomorphism: Cancer cells & their nuclei often display pleomorphism (i.e. variation in cell size
and shape).

* Abnormal nuclear morphology: The nuclei are hyperchromatic and disproportionately large for

Twmeur progression: Tumour progression refers to the phenomenon whereby tumours become more
aggressive and acquire greater malignant potential over a period of time.

Example: In some instances (e.g. colon cancer), there is an orderly evolution from pre-neoplastic lesion to
benign tumors and, ultimately, invasive cancers. This phenomenon is referred to as tumour progression.

Pre-neoplastic lesion — benign tumors — invasive cancers

phenotypic attributes such as invasiveness, rate of growth, metastatic ability, karyotype, hormonal

prone to a high rate of random, spontaneous mutations during clonal expansion. This phenomenon is called
tamor heterogeneity which begins well before the tumor is clinically evident.

Q. Short note: Aneuploidy. (CU-18Nv, SU-18M)

Ans.

Aneuploidy: Normal cells are diploid, containing 46 chromosomes, 22 pairs of autosomes and 1 pair of sex
chromosomes, XX in females or XY in males.

Aneuploidy is a chromosome number that is not a multiple of 23 (the normal haploid number), for example a
human cell having 45 or 47 chromosomes instead of the usual 46.

Mechanism: It is caused most often by an addition or loss of one or two chromosomes; this change may result
from non-disjunction or anaphase lag.

Non-disjunction:
(1) It is the failure of chromosomes to separate during meiosis or mitosis (meiotic non-disjunction is the
most common).
(2) It is responsible for disorders such as trisomy 21 (the most common form of Down syndrome).

Anaphase lag:
(1) It results in the loss of a chromosome during meiotic or mitotic division.
(2) In early embryonic life, this can result in mosaicism, in which an individual develops two lines of
cells, one with a normal chromosome complement and another with monosomy, a single residual

chromosome, for the affected chromosome pair.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/165; BRS Pathology/5"/48)

{N.B: Aneuploidy is remarkably common in cancers, particularly carcinomas (i.e. cancer cells may have

abnormal number of chromosomes)}.
(Ref: Robbins BASIC PATHOLOGY/10"/203)

Q. Short note: Tissue processing. (CU-19M)

Ans.

Tissue processing: Preparation of tissue (animal or human) to visualize under light microscope is called tissue
preparation.

Purpose of tissue processing: The purpose of tissue processing is to demonstrate normal and abnormal
structures of cell.

Steps of tissue processing:

1. Sample / tissue collection - Fresh tissue specimens should be collected and handled carefully and
appropriately fixed as soon as possible after dissection.

2. Fixation - It is a process where tissues are immersed in solution called fixatives (such as 10% formal
saline / formalin) for permanent preservation of tissue structure and protection against subsequent
processing steps.

3. Dehydration - It is the process of removal of extractable or free water from the fixed tissue by
immersing specimens in a series of ethanol (alcohol).

4. Clearing - Clearing is a process by which tissue is allowed to be transparent for well visualization.
Xylene is used as routine ‘clearing agent’.

5. Embedding - Tissues are usually embedded in a solid medium to facilitate sectioning. To obtain thin
sections with the microtome, tissues must be infiltrated after fixation with embedding substances that
impart a rigid consistency to the tissue. Routinely used embedding agent is paraffin wax.

6. Sectioning / cutting - Tissues in paraffin blocks are sectioned at 5-10 micrometer thickness with a rotary
microtome.

7. Preliminary mounting - Sections were placed in hot water bath at 48-50°C. Tissue section is then taken on slide.

8. Staining - To be studied microscopically sections must typically be stained or dyed because most
tissues are colorless. Routinely used dye combination is hematoxyline & eosine (H & E).
Staining procedure:
« Deparaffinization
* Rehydration
» Staining with hematoxylin & eosine (H & E)
= Dehydration
* Clearing
* Final mounting.

Now slide is ready for visualization under light microscope.

Classification of stains:

1. Acid stains: Eosin which imparts red color.

2. Basic stains: Hacmatoxilin which imparts blue color
