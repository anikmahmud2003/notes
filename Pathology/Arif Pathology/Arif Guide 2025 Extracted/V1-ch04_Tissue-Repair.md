Q. Describe the steps in scar formation.
Steps_in_scar formation: Repair by connective tissue deposition (scar formation) consists of sequential
processes that follow tissue injury. The subsequent steps are-

1. Inflammation: Breakdown products of complement activation, chemokines released from activated
platelets, and other mediators produced at the site of injury function as chemotactic agents to recruit
neutrophils and then monocytes over the next 6 to 48 hours. These inflammatory cells eliminate the
offending agents, such as microbes that may have entered through the wound, and clear the debris. As
the injurious agents and necrotic cells are cleared, the inflammation resolves.

2. Cell proliferation: In the next stage, which takes up to 10 days, several cell types, including
epithelial cells, endothelial and other vascular cells, and fibroblasts, proliferate and migrate to close the
now clean wound. Each cell type serves unique functions.

* Epithelial cells respond to locally produced growth factors and migrate over the wound to cover it up.

* Endothelial cells and pericytes proliferate to form new blood vessels, a process known as
angiogenesis.

* Fibroblasts proliferate and migrate into the site of injury and lay down collagen fibers that form
the scar.

3. Formation of granulation tissue: Migration and proliferation of fibroblasts and deposition of loose
connective tissue, together with the blood vessels and interspersed mononuclear leukocytes, form
granulation tissue.

4. Deposition of connective tissue: Granulation tissue is progressively replaced by deposition of
collagen. The amount of connective tissue increases in the granulation tissue, eventually resulting in

the formation of a stable fibrous scar.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/105)

Q. Short note: Stem cells.

Ans.

Stem cells: Stem cells are those characterized by their self-renewal properties and their capacity to give rise
to differentiated cells and tissues.

Under conditions of homeostasis, stem cells are maintained by self-renewal, which can involve two types of
cell division:
1. Asymmetric division, occurs when one daughter cell enters a differentiation pathway and gives rise to
mature cells, while the other retains its self-renewal capacity.

2. Symmetric division, occurs when both daughter cells retain self-renewal capacity.

Types of stem cells with importance: Fundamentally there are two varieties of stem cells:

1. Embroyonic stem cells (ES cells): The inner cell mass of the blastocysts in early embryonic
development contains pluripotent cells known as the embroyonic stem (ES) cells.

Ans.

Healing by primary union / first intention: When the injury involves only the epithelial layer, the principal
mechanism of repair is epithelial regeneration, also called primary union or healing by first intention.

Example: Healing of a clean, uninfected surgical incision approximated by surgical sutures.

Sequence of events in primary healing:
1. Immediately: Wounding causes the rapid activation of coagulation pathways, which results in the
formation of a blood clot on the wound surface. The clot serves to stop bleeding and supports
migrating cells, which are attracted by growth factors, cytokines, and chemokines released into the area.

2. Within 24 hours: Neutrophils are seen at the incision margin, migrating toward the fibrin clot.
They release proteolytic enzymes that begin to clear the debris.

3. Within 24-48 hours: Epithelial cells from both edges have begun to migrate and proliferate along the
dermis yielding a thin but continuous epithelial layer that closes the wound.

Ans.
Factors influencing wound healing: (****V.V./**** for VIVA)
A. Systemic factors:
1. Nutrition: Protein deficiency & particularly vitamin C deficiency inhibit collagen synthesis and retard
wound healing.
2. Metabolic status: Can change wound healing, e.g. diabetes mellitus is associated with delayed healing.
3. Circulatory status: Inadequate blood supply (poor perfusion), usually caused by arteriosclerosis and
diabetes or by obstructed venous drainage (e.g. in varicose veins), also impairs healing.

4. Hormones: Such as glucocorticoids (steroids), have well-documented anti-inflammatory effects that
influence various components of inflammation & fibroplasias. These agents also inhibit collagen synthesis.

B. Local factors:

1. Infection: Causes delay in healing because of persistent tissue injury and inflammation.

2. Mechanical factors: Such as early motion, or increased local pressure or torsion may cause wounds
to pull apart and ultimately delay healing.

3. Foreign bodies: Such as unnecessary sutures or fragments of steel, glass or even bone impede healing.

4. Size, location & type of wound: Influence healing. Wounds in richly vascularized areas, such as the
face, heal faster than those in poorly vascularized ones, such as foot. Small incisional injuries heal
faster and with less scar formation than large excisional wounds or wounds caused by blunt trauma.

(Ref: Robbins/9"/106)

Q. Short note: Complications of wound healing. (DU-14Ja, SUST-21Nv)

Ans.

Complications of wound healing: The complications of wound healing can be grouped into three general categories:
1. Deficient scar formation
2. Excessive formation of the repair components
3. Formation of contractures

A. Deficient scar formation (Defects in healing: Chronic wounds):
1. Wound dehiscence: When a surgical incision reopens internally or externally it is called wound
dehiscence. The risk factors for such an occurrence are obesity, malnutrition, infections, and vascular

insufficiency. In abdominal wounds it can be precipitated by vomiting and coughing.

2. Ulceration: Wounds can ulcerate because of inadequate vascularization during healing. The following
are some common examples-

= Venous leg ulcers develop most often in elderly people as a result of chronic venous hypertension,
which may be caused by severe varicose veins or congestive heart failure. These ulcers fail to heal
because of poor delivery of oxygen to the site of the ulcer.

# Arterial ulcers develop in individuals with atherosclerosis of peripheral arteries, especially
associated with diabetes.

" Diabetic ulcers affect the lower extremities, particularly the feet. There is tissue necrosis and
failure to heal as a result of vascular disease causing ischemia, neuropathy, systemic metabolic
abnormalities, and secondary infections.

« Pressure sores are areas of skin ulceration and necrosis of underlying tissues caused by prolonged
compression of tissues against a bone, e.g. in elderly patients with numerous morbidities lying in
bed without moving. The lesions are caused by mechanical pressure and local ischemia.

B. Excessive formation of the repair components (Excessive scarring):

1. Hypertrophic scar: Accumulation of excessive amounts of collagen may give rise to raised scar tha
remains within the boundaries of the original wound is known as hypertrophic scar. It regresse;
within 6 months.

2. Keloid: If the scar tissue grows beyond the boundaries of the original wound and does not regress,
it is called a keloid.

3. Exuberant granulation (proud flesh): Formation of excessive amounts of granulation tissue, which
protrudes above the level of the surrounding skin and blocks re-epithelialization.

4. Desmoids / aggressive fibromatoses: If the exuberant proliferation of fibroblast and other connective

tissue recur after excision, it is called desmoids. These lie in the interface between benign
proliferations and malignant tumours.

C. Contracture: Contraction in size of a wound is an important part in normal healing process. An
exaggeration of this process is called a contracture and results in deformities of the wound & the
surrounding tissues. Contractures are particularly prone to develop on palms, soles & anterior aspect of
the thorax. Contractures are commonly seen after various bums and can compromise the movement of joints.
