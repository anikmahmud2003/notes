Q. Short note: Fallot’s tetralogy / the tetralogy of Fallot. (RU-17Nv, CU-13Ju)
Ans.
Fallot’s tetralogy: The four cardinal features of the tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) are- (V.V./ for VIVA)

1) Ventricular septal defect (VSD)

2) Obstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract (subpulmonary stenosis)
3) An aorta that overrides the VSD

4) Right ventricular hypertrophy.

The first three features result embryologically from anterosuperior displacement of the infundibular septum,
and the right ventricular hypertrophy is a secondary consequence of the pressure overload.

Clinical features:
1, Even untreated, TOF can be tolerated into adulthood
2. As the child grows, the heart increases in size.
3. Most infants with TOF are cyanotic from birth or soon thereafter (classic TOF); some may not show
cyanosis (so-called “pink tetralogy”).

Morphology: The heart is typically enlarged and is classically “boot-shaped” due to marked right ventricular
hypertrophy.

Treatment: Complete surgical repair is possible for classic TOF but is more complicated for persons with
pulmonary atresia and dilated bronchial arteries.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/538)

Heart Failure

Q. What is heart failure?
Ans.
Heart failure: Heart failure is defined as the condition in which a heart cannot pump blood to adequately meet
the metabolic demands of peripheral tissues, or can do so only at elevated filling pressures.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/530)

Q. Discuss the pathogenesis of IHD. (RU-17Nv)

Ans,
Pathogenesis of IHD: The dominant cause of the [HD is partial or complete interruption of arterial blood
flow to the myocardium resulting insufficient coronary perfusion relative to myocardial demand.

It is convenient to consider the etiology of IHD under three broad headings:

i) Coronary atherosclerosis: Chronic, progressive atherosclerotic narrowing of the epicardial coronary
arteries.

ii) Variable degrees of superimposed acute plaque change: Rupture, superficial erosion, ulceration,
fissuring, or deep haemorrhage etc.

iii) Non-atherosclerotic causes:
Vasospasm
Stenosis of coronary ostia
Coronary arteries
Thrombotic diseases (e.g. polycythaemia vera, sickle cell anaemia)
Embolism
Trauma to coronary arteries
Aneurysms
Compression of a coronary from outside.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/542; BRS Pathology/5"/137; Harsh Mohan/7"/408)

Types of angina:

1. Stable (typical) angina: It is the most common form of angina. It is classically described as a
crushing or squeezing substernal sensation that can radiate down the left arm or to the left jaw
(referred pain). The pain is precipitated by exertion (i.c. physical activity, emotional excitement or
psychological stress) and is relieved by rest (decreasing demand) or administering vasodilators,
such as nitroglycerin and calcium channel blockers (thereby increasing perfusion).

It is caused by an imbalance in coronary perfusion (due to chronic stenosing coronary
atherosclerosis) relative to myocardial demand.

2. Unstable or crescendo angina: It is a pattern of increasingly frequent, prolonged (>20 min), or
severe angina, precipitated by progressively lower levels of physical activity or even occurring at
rest.

It is generally caused by disruption of an atherosclerotic plaque with superimposed thrombosis. It can
also be caused by distal embolization of the thrombus, and/or vasospasm.

Unstable angina serves as a warning for MI, that’s why it is referred to as ‘pre-infarction angina’ or
‘acute coronary insufficiency’.

3. Prinzmetal variant angina: It is an uncommon form of episodic myocardial ischemia caused by
coronary artery spasm.

(Ref: Robbins/10""/543; BRS Pathology/5"/137; Harsh Mohan/7"/409)

(N.B: Myocardial infarction differs from unstable angina, since there is evidence of myocardial necrosis.)
(Ref: Davidson’s/23"/493)

(N.B: In angina, there is no elevation of enzymes in the blood as there is no irreversible myocardial injury.)
(Ref: Harsh Mohan/7"/409)

Myocardial Infarction (MI)

Q. What is myocardial infarction (MI)? What are the types of MI?
Ans.

Myocardial infarction (MI): MI, also commonly referred to as “heart attack,” is the death of cardiac
muscle due to prolonged ischemia.

It is the most important and feared consequence of coronary artery disease.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/543; Harsh Mohan/7"/409)

Patterns of MI: There are two distinct patterns of myocardial ischemic necrosis:

1. Transmural (ful thickness) infarction: Ischemic necrosis involves the full or nearly full thickness
of the ventricular wall (from the endocardium to the epicardium) in the distribution of the affected
coronary artery.

2. Subendocardial (non-transmural) infarction: Ischemic necrosis involves the inner third of the
ventricular wall. It occurs as a result of a plaque disruption followed by a coronary thrombus that
becomes lysed (therapeutically or spontaneously) before myocardial necrosis extends across the full
thickness of the wall.

(Ref: Robbins/10"/546; BRS Pathology/5"/138; Harsh Mohan/7"/410)

Multifocal microinfarction is another pattern of myocardial ischemic necrosis which is seen when there is
pathology involving only smaller intramural vessels.
(Ref: Robbins/10'"/547)

Q. Discuss in brief about the pathogenesis of MI (myocardial infarction). (DU-21Ja,20NvM,
19Nv, CMU-22Nv, RMU-21M,08Ju, SU-15Ja)
Ans.
Pathogenesis of MI:
A) Coronary arterial occlusion:
a) Thrombotic occlusion of coronary artery: The following sequence of events likely underlies most MIs-
* An atheromatous plaque is eroded or suddenly disrupted by endothelial injury, intraplaque
hemorthage, or mechanical forces, exposing subendothelial collagen and necrotic plaque
contents to the blood.
* Platelets adhere, aggregate, and are activated, releasing thromboxane Az, adenosine
diphosphate (ADP), and serotonin—causing further platelet aggregation and vasospasm.
« Activation of coagulation by tissue factor and other mechanisms adds to the growing
thrombus.

* Within minutes, the thrombus can evolve to completely occlude the coronary artery lumen.

b) Non-thrombotic occlusion of coronary artery: The mechanisms include-
1. Vasospasm with or without coronary atherosclerosis, perhaps in association with platelet
aggregation or due to drug ingestion (e.g. cocaine or ephedrine).

2. Emboli from the left atrium in association with atrial fibrillation, a left-sided mural thrombus,
vegetations of infective endocarditis, intracardiac prosthetic material; or paradoxical emboli
from the right side of the heart or the peripheral veins, traversing a patent foramen ovale and
into the coronary arteries.

3. Uncommon causes of MI without atherothrombosis include disorders of small intramural
coronary vessels (e.g. vasculitis), hematologic abnormalities (e.g. sickle cell disease), amyloid
deposition in vascular walls, vascular dissection, marked hypertrophy (e.g. due to aortic
stenosis), lowered systemic blood pressure (e.g. shock), or inadequate myocardial “protection”
during cardiac surgery.

B) Myocardial response: Coronary arterial obstruction diminishes blood flow to a region of
myocardium, causing ischemia, rapid myocardial dysfunction, and eventually—with prolonged
vascular compromise—myocyte death. The anatomic region supplied by that artery is referred to as
the area at risk. The outcome depends predominantly on the severity and duration of flow

deprivation.

(Ref: Robbins/10"/544)

(N.B: Myocardial ischemia leads to loss of function within 1 to 2 minutes but causes necrosis only after 20 to

30 minutes.)

(Ref: Robbins/10'/553)

Q. Give an account of constrictive pericarditis. (SU-05Ja)
Ans.

Constrictive pericarditis: The heart may be encased in a dense, fibrous or fibrocalcific scar that limits
diastolic expansion and seriously restricts cardiac output, resembling restrictive cardiomyopathy.

A well-defined history of a previous pericarditis may or may not be present.

In constrictive pericarditis, the pericardial space is obliterated, and the heart is surrounded by a dense, adherent
layer of scar with or without calcification, often 0.5 - 1.0 cm thick, that can resemble a plaster mold in extreme
cases (concretio cordis).

(Ref: Robbins/10"/578)
