Q. Define meningitis and meningoencephalitis?

Ans.

Meningitis: Meningitis is an inflammatory process of the leptomeninges and CSF within the subarachnoid
space, usually caused by an infection.

It is usually caused by an infection, but may also occur in response to non-bacterial irritant (e.g. chemical
meningitis). Infectious meningitis is broadly classified into acute pyogenic (usually bacterial meningitis)
meningitis, aseptic (usually acute viral meningitis), and chronic (usually tuberculous, sphirochetal, or
cryptococcal) meningitis.

Although infections are the most common causes of meningitis and meningoencephalitis, this reaction may
also occur in response to a nonbacterial irritant introduced into the subarachnoid space (chemical meningitis)
or in the context of a systemic autoimmune disease. Based on the etiology and clinical evolution of the illness,
infectious meningitis is broadly classified into acute pyogenic (usually bacterial), aseptic (usually acute or
subacute viral), and chronic (usually tuberculous, spirochetal, or cryptococcal).

Meningoencephalitis: Meningoencephalitis refers to inflammation of the meninges and brain

parenchyma.
(Ref: Robbins/10'"/1261)

Q. Mention the differences between tumours of the nervous system and tumours of the other

parts of the body.
Ans

Differences between tumours of the nervous system and tumours of the other parts of the body: Tumours

of the nervous system have several unique characteristics that set them apart from neoplastic processes

elsewhere in the body.

First, the clinical course of disease is relatively unique in case of brain tumors. Thus, some glial tumors
with benign histologic features (low mitotic rate, cellular uniformity, and slow growth) may infiltrate large
regions of the brain and lead to serious clinical deficits and poor prognosis.

Second, because of their infiltrative behavior, it is often not feasible to resect glial neoplasms completely

without compromising neurologic function.

Third, any neoplasm can have lethal consequences if it is located in a critical region, as when a benign
meningioma, by compressing the medulla, causes cardiorespiratory arrest.

Finally, the pattern of the spread of primary CNS neoplasms differs from that of other tumours: even the
most highly malignant gliomas rarely metastasize outside the CNS.

(Ref: Robbins/10"/1293 + 8'°/1330)

Q. Short note: Glioma. (CU-19Nv)

Ans.

Glioma: The term glioma is used for all tumours arising from neuroglia, or more precisely, from
neuroectodermal epithelial tissue. Gliomas are the most common of the primary CNS tumours and
collectively account for 40% of all intracranial tumours.

They include tumours arising:
a. from astrocytes (astrocytomas and glioblastoma multiforme);
b. from oligodendrocytes (oligodendroglioma);
c. from ependyma (ependymoma); and
d. from choroid plexus (choroid plexus papilloma).

Gliomas are disseminated to other parts of the CNS by CSF but they rarely ever metastasise beyond the CNS.

Q. Short note: Meningioma. (DU-07Ja, SU-12Ju, CU-12Ja)

Ans.

Meningioma: This is the second most common primary intracranial neoplasm. Most cases are benign and
slow growing. The neoplasm originates in arachnoidal cells of the meninges; it is external to the brain and

Q. Short note: Peripheral nerve sheath tumours. (CU-16Ju)

Ans.

Peripheral nerve sheath tumours: A number of benign and malignant neoplasms are grouped together as
peripheral nerve sheath tumors—all likely arise from cells of Schwann cell lineage.

The three common peripheral nerve sheath tumors:
1. Schwannoma: This benign, slowly growing encapsulated tumor arises from Schwann cells.

2. Neurofibroma: This neoplasm occurs as solitary or multiple tumors of peripheral nerves derived from
Schwann cells that can be subtyped as localized cutaneous, diffuse, or plexiform.

3. Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (MPNST): Most (approximately 85%) are high-grade
tumors, but low-grade variants are recognized. About half arise in NF1 patients and are assumed to
result from malignant transformation of a plexiform neurofibroma.

Peripheral nerve sheath tumors have association with relatively common familial tumor syndromes,
including neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF 1), neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2), and schwannomatosis.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/1236; BRS Pathology/5'/385)
