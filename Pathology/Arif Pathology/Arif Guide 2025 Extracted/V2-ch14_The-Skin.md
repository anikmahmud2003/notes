Q. Short note: Psoriasis of skin. (CMU-24M)
Ans.
Psoriasis of skin: Psoriasis is a chronic inflammatory dermatosis that affects about 2% of the population.

Characteristics:

« It usually appears first between the age of 15 and 30 years.

" The lesions are characterised by brownish-red papules and plaques which are sharply demarcated and
are covered with fine, silvery white scales.

* As the scales are removed by gentle scrapping, fine bleeding points appear termed Auspitz sign.

* Commonly involved sites are the scalp, upper back, sacral region and extensor surfaces of the
extremities, especially the knees and elbows.

= In about 25% of cases, peculiar pitting of nails is seen.

* Psoriatic arthritis resembling rheumatoid arthritis is produced in about 5% of cases but rheumatoid
factor is absent.

Histopathology: Following features are observed in fully developed lesions:
* Acanthosis with regular downgrowth of rete ridges to almost the same dermal level with thickening of
their lower portion.
Elongation and oedema of the dermal papillae with broadening of their tips.
Suprapapillary thinning of stratum malpighii.
Absence of granular cell layer.
Prominent parakeratosis.
Presence of Munro microabscesses in the parakeratotic horny layer is diagnostic of psoriasis.

Q. Short note: Rodent ulcer. (RU-17Nv/Ja)

Ans.

Basal cell carcinoma (Rodent ulcer): Malignant proliferation of basal cell (stem cell) of epidermis.

It is the most common of all malignant skin tumors arising from basal layer of the epidermis & its
appendages. Typically, the basal cell carcinoma is a locally invasive, slow-growing tumour of middle-aged
that rarely metastasises.

Sites: (Important for VIVA)
* Chronic sun-exposed sites in lightly pigmented elderly adults.
* Most commonly on the face (90%), usually above a line from the angle of mouth upto the ear
lobule.

Rodent ulcer

Fig: Common location and macroscopic appearance of basal cell carcinoma (rodent ulcer).

Aetiology:
i) Age: Mostly occur after the age of 40 years.

ii) Race: Lightly skinned races (who have little melanin) are most susceptible than black.
iii) Precancerous conditions: Xeroderma pigmentosum, basal cell nevus syndrome.
iv) Environmental factor: Chronic sun exposure.

Morphology:
Macroscopic:
1, Ulcerated (most common pattern):
* A slow-growing small nodule undergoes central ulceration with pearly, raised rolled margins.
* The tumour enlarges in size by burrowing and by destroying the tissues locally like a rodent and
hence the name ‘rodent ulcer’.

2. Non-ulcerated (less frequent pattern): Pearly and shiny papules often containing dialated
(telangiectatic) vessel.

Fig: Basal cell carcinoma appears as a pearly papule with central ulceration.

Q. Give the morphology of squamous cell carcinoma.

Q. Short note: Squamous cell carcinoma.

Ans.

Squamous cell carcinoma (Marjolin ulcer): Squamous cell carcinoma is the second most common skin

cancer arising on sun exposed sites in older people.

Incidence: Higher incidence in men than in women.

Pathogenesis: The most important cause of cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma is DNA damage induced by
exposure to UV light. Tumor incidence is proportional to the degree of lifetime sun exposure.

Predisposing factors:
Prolonged sun exposure

Immunosupressive therapy

Industrial carcinogens (tars and oils)

Chronic ulcers and draining osteomyelitis

Old bum scars (Marjolin’s ulcers)

Arsenic exposure

Psoriasis

HIV infection

Clinical features:
1. Usually asymptomatic
2. Itching or pain may be early manifestations
3. Majority of the lesions are grater than 10 mm in diameter
4. Change in colour, size, or shape in a pigmented lesion are the most consistent clinical signs.

Clinical warning signs:
1. The most important warning signs, sometimes called the ABCDEs of melanoma, are-
Asymmetry
Irregular borders
Change in color
Increasing diameter
Evolution or change over time, especially if rapid.

2. Other features-
* Pigmented lesions > 6 mm in diameter
= Any change in appearance
= New onset of itching or pain.

Growth pattern:
* Radial growth (initial phase): Growth occurs in all directions but is predominantly lateral within the
epidermis and papillary zone of the dermis. During this stage of growth, melanomas do not
metastasize; clinical cure is frequent.

* Vertical growth (later phase): Growth extends into the reticular dermis or beyond. Prognosis varies
with the depth of the lesion. During this phase, lymphatics or hematogenous metastasis may occur.
