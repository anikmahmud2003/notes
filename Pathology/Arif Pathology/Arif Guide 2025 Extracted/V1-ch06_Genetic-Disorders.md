Q. What is mutation? (CU-19Nv,17M) Mention its types and consequences with example.
Q. Short note on: Mutation / Genetic mutation. (DU-19M, CU-15Ja)
Ans.
Mutation: A mutation is defined as a permanent change in the DNA.
(Ref: Robbins/10"/142)

Types of mutation: Based on the extent of genetic change, mutations may be classified into three categories:

1. Genome mutation: Loss or gain of whole chromosomes, giving rise to monosomy or trisomy.

2. Chromosomal mutation: Rearrangement of genetic material and give rise to visible structural
changes in the chromosome.

3. Gene mutation: Partial or complete deletion of a gene or, most often, affect a single base. It is of 2 types-

(a) Frame-shift mutation: This type of mutation occurs when one or two base pairs may be inserted
into or deleted from the DNA, leading to alterations in the leading frame of the DNA strand.

(b) Point mutation: In this type of mutation a single nucleotide base may be substituted by a
different base. Consequences of point mutation are-

i) Missense mutations: A point mutation (single base substitution) may alter the codon ina
triplet of bases and lead to the replacement of one amino acid by another. Because this
mutation alters the meaning of the genetic code, they are often termed missense mutations.

Example: Sickle cell anemia.

ji) Nonsense mutations: A point mutation may change an amino acid codon to a chain
terminator, or stop codon.

Example: B°-thalassemia.

iii) Silent mutations: Silent mutations are those that cause no change in the final protein product
and can only be detected by sequencing the gene. (This type not included in Robbin’s
Pathology)

(Ref: Robbins/10°/142 + 7°/138-140)

Features of autosomal dominant inheritance:

1. Autosomal dominant disorders are manifested in the heterozygous state, so at least one parent of an
index case is usually affected.

2. Both males and females are affected, and both can transmit the condition.

3. No carrier state.

4. When an affected person marries an unaffected one, every child has one chance in two of having the
disease (50% chance).

5. Risk remains the same for each successive pregnancy.

6. Clinical features can be modified by reduced penetrance and expressivity.

7. In many conditions, the age at onset is delayed; symptoms and signs do not appear until adulthood (as
in Huntington disease).

8. Rare and usually less severe than autosomal recessive disorder.

(Ref: Robbins/10"/144)

Q. Short note: X-linked dominant disorders. (DU-13/10Ju)

X-linked dominant disorders: There are only a few X-linked dominant conditions. They are caused by

Pathogenesis: Marfan syndrome results from an inherited defect in an extra-cellular glycoprotein calleg
fibrillin-1, which is the major component of microfibrils found in the extra-cellular matrix.

Skeletal abnormalities in Marfan syndrome:
1]. The patient is unusually tall with exceptionally long extremities and long, tapering fingers and toes.
2. The joint ligaments in the hands and feet are lax, suggesting that the patient is double-jointed; typically
the thumb can be hyperextended back to the wmist.
3. The head is commonly dolichocephalic (long-headed) with bossing of the frontal eminences and

Ocular changes in Marfan syndrome: Most characteristic is bilateral subluxation or dislocation (usually
outward and upward) of the lens referred to as ectopia lentis.

Cardiovascular lesions:
1. Mitral valve prolapse
2. Dilation of the ascending aorta owing to cystic medionecrosis
3. Loss of medial support results in progressive dilation of the aortic valve ring and the root of the aorta,
giving rise to severe aortic incompetence.

Q. Short note: Karyotyping. (DU-12Ju)

Q. Short note: Karyotype. (DU-17M)

Ans.

Karyotyping: The study of chromosomes is called karyotyping.
Or,

Karyotyping is the presentation of a cellular chromosome profile.

It is the basic tool for cytogeneticist.

Normal karyotype: Human somatic cells contain 46 chromosomes; these comprise 22 homologous pairs of
autosomes and two sex chromosomes, XX in the female and XY in the male.

Procedure of karyotyping: A karyotype is a standard arrangement of a photographed or imaged stained
metaphase spread in which chromosome pairs are arranged in order of decreasing length.

1. Mitosis in dividing cells is arrested in metaphase by use of mitotic spindle inhibitors (e.g. colcemid)

2. Then to stain the chromosomes (commonly Giemsa stain)

3. In a metaphasic spread, the individual chromosomes to take the form of two chromatids connected at
the centromere.

4. A karyotype is obtained by arranging each pair of autosomes according to length, followed by sex
chromosomes.

Way of karyotype presentation: Karyotypes are usually described using a shorthand system of notations in
the following order: total number of chromosomes is given first, followed by the sex chromosome
complement, and finally the description of abnormalities in ascending numerical order. For example, a male
with trisomy 21 is designated 47,XY,+21/.

(Ref: Robbins/10"/164)
