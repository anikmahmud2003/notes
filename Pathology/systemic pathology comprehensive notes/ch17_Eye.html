🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of the Eye (boss-level overview)

The eye is organised from anterior → posterior: conjunctiva → cornea → lens → retina → choroid. Disease can strike any layer. This chapter covers:

1. Conjunctival diseases — scarring, pinguecula, pterygium, neoplasms (squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), conjunctival intra-epithelial neoplasia (CIN), mucoepidermoid carcinoma, nevi, melanoma).
2. Corneal diseases — keratitis & ulcers, band keratopathy, keratoconus, Fuchs endothelial dystrophy, stromal dystrophies.
3. Orbital neoplasms — primary (capillary haemangioma, lymphangioma, cavernous haemangioma, pleomorphic adenoma of lacrimal gland, dermoid cyst, neurilemmoma, orbital lymphoma) and secondary/metastatic (prostatic carcinoma, neuroblastoma, Wilms tumour).
4. Eyelid malignancies — basal cell carcinoma (BCC), sebaceous carcinoma.
5. Retinal diseases — detachment, vascular disease (hypertensive, diabetic, retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), radiation, vasculitis, sickle-cell retinopathy, artery/vein occlusion), age-related macular degeneration (AMD), retinitis pigmentosa, retinitis due to Candida, neoplasms (retinoblastoma (Rb), retinal lymphoma).
6. Retinoblastoma — most common primary intraocular malignancy of children; RB gene on chromosome 13q14.
7. Cataract — congenital & acquired lenticular opacities (galactosemia, diabetes mellitus (DM), Wilson disease, drugs, radiation, trauma, age-related).
8. Glaucoma — elevated intra-ocular pressure (IOP) → visual field loss & optic-nerve cupping; classified as primary (open-angle = most common; angle-closure) and secondary.

1 · Conjunctival & Corneal Diseases

🔴 What are the conjunctival diseases? Describe the neoplasms of the conjunctiva.

Conjunctival diseases:

  1. Conjunctival scarring — due to chronic inflammation (trachoma, Stevens-Johnson syndrome), chemical burns, or surgical trauma.
  2. Pinguecula & pterygium — degenerative conjunctival lesions associated with UV (ultraviolet) light exposure and chronic irritation. Pinguecula = yellowish elevated plaque on the bulbar conjunctiva (nasal side). Pterygium = wing-shaped fibrovascular growth extending from the conjunctiva onto the cornea.
  3. Neoplasms (see below).

Conjunctival neoplasms

TumourKey Features
Conjunctival squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)Most common malignant conjunctival tumour. Arises from the conjunctival epithelium. Often at the limbus (corneo-conjunctival junction). Risk factors: UV exposure, HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) / immunosuppression.
Conjunctival intra-epithelial neoplasia (CIN)Precursor / pre-malignant lesion; graded I–III based on the thickness of epithelium involved. May progress to invasive SCC.
Mucoepidermoid carcinomaAggressive variant containing mucus-secreting (goblet) cells interspersed with squamous cells. Higher recurrence rate than pure SCC.
Conjunctival neviBenign melanocytic naevi; common in young adults. Usually pigmented, may show cystic changes.
Conjunctival melanomaMalignant melanoma of the conjunctiva. May arise from a pre-existing nevus, CIN, or de novo. Can metastasise haematogenously and via lymphatics.

Robbins 10e p.1310 · Arif 2025 p.373

🔴 What are the diseases of the cornea? Classify corneal degenerations and dystrophies.

Corneal diseases:

  1. Keratitis and ulcers — infectious (bacterial, fungal, viral — especially herpes simplex virus (HSV)) or non-infectious. Corneal ulceration can be sight-threatening if it perforates.
  2. Corneal degenerations and dystrophies (see classification below).

Classification of corneal degenerations & dystrophies

TypeDescription
Band keratopathyCalcium phosphate deposition in Bowman layer of the cornea, forming a horizontal "band" across the inter-palpebral zone. Associated with chronic eye disease, hypercalcaemia, and renal failure.
KeratoconusNon-inflammatory, progressive thinning and cone-shaped protrusion of the cornea. Usually bilateral. Often presents in adolescence. Leads to irregular astigmatism.
Fuchs endothelial dystrophyProgressive loss of corneal endothelial cells → stromal oedema → epithelial bullae ("bullous keratopathy"). Usually bilateral; more common in women. May require corneal transplant (penetrating keratoplasty) in advanced stages.
Stromal dystrophiesGroup of inherited corneal opacities affecting the stroma. Includes granular, lattice, and macular dystrophies. Typically bilateral, symmetric, and slowly progressive.

3 · Orbital Neoplasms

🔴 What are the neoplasms of the orbit? (Important for VIVA) Distinguish primary from secondary (metastatic).

Neoplasms of the orbit — VIVA favourite. Classified as primary (arising within the orbit) and secondary (metastatic to the orbit).

1. Primary orbital neoplasms

TumourAge / Key Features
Capillary haemangiomaMost common orbital tumour of infancy and early childhood. Benign vascular proliferation of capillary-sized vessels. Usually involutes spontaneously by age 5–7 yr.
LymphangiomaBenign lymphatic-channel tumour. May enlarge and cause proptosis. Can undergo spontaneous haemorrhage (chocolate cyst).
Cavernous haemangiomaMost common orbital tumour in adults. Slowly progressive proptosis. Well-encapsulated; surgical excision is curative.
Pleomorphic adenoma of the lacrimal glandBenign mixed tumour of the lacrimal gland (analogous to salivary-gland pleomorphic adenoma). Slowly growing, painless superolateral proptosis. Risk of malignant transformation if incompletely excised.
Dermoid cystCongenital choristoma containing ectodermal derivatives (skin, hair follicles, sebaceous glands). Most common congenital orbital mass in children.
Neurilemmoma (Schwannoma)Benign nerve-sheath tumour arising from Schwann cells. Well-encapsulated, slow-growing.
Orbital lymphomaTypically Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), most commonly MALT (mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue) lymphoma. Painless, gradually progressive proptosis. Bilateral involvement possible.

2. Secondary (metastatic) orbital neoplasms

  • Metastatic prostatic carcinoma — in older males; often bilateral.
  • Metastatic neuroblastoma — in children; may cause "raccoon eyes" (periorbital ecchymosis).
  • Metastatic Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma) — paediatric renal malignancy that can metastasise to the orbit.

4 · Eyelid Malignancies

🔴 What are the common malignancies of the eyelid? (Important for VIVA)

Common malignancies of the eyelid (Ref: Robbins 10e p.1308):

  1. Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) — most common eyelid malignancy. Slow-growing, locally invasive but rarely metastasises. Typically occurs on the lower eyelid. Pearly, translucent nodule with telangiectasia and central ulceration ("rodent ulcer"). Risk factors: UV exposure, fair skin, chronic sun damage.
  2. Sebaceous carcinoma — second most common eyelid malignancy. Arises from Meibomian glands (tarsal plate) or sebaceous glands of the eyelid. More aggressive than BCC; may metastasise. Can mimic chalazion or chronic blepharitis → high index of suspicion needed. Must be differentiated from BCC because management differs.
🎯 Bottom eyelid = BCC (most common) · Upper eyelid = Sebaceous carcinoma (think Meibomian gland origin).

Robbins 10e p.1308 · Arif 2025 p.374

5 · Retinal Diseases

🔴 What are the common retinal diseases? Classify them systematically.

Common retinal diseases (Ref: Robbins 10e p.1319, Arif p.374):

CategoryConditions
Retinal detachmentSeparation of the neurosensory retina from the underlying retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Two types: rhegmatogenous (due to a retinal break allowing vitreous humour to seep under the retina) and exudative/tractional (secondary to inflammation, tumour, or proliferative membranes).
Retinal vascular disease
  • Hypertensive retinopathy — arteriolar narrowing, flame-shaped haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, hard exudates, papilloedema (grade IV).
  • Diabetic retinopathy — non-proliferative (microaneurysms, dot-and-blot haemorrhages, hard exudates, cotton-wool spots) and proliferative (neovascularisation → vitreous haemorrhage → tractional detachment).
  • Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) — vasoproliferative disorder of immature retinal vasculature in premature infants. May lead to retinal detachment and blindness.
  • Radiation retinopathy — damage to retinal vasculature following ionising radiation therapy.
  • Retinal vasculitis — inflammation of retinal vessels; may be idiopathic or secondary to systemic disease.
  • Sickle-cell retinopathy — proliferative retinal neovascularisation in sickle-cell disease (HbS) due to retinal ischaemia from vascular occlusion.
  • Retinal artery and vein occlusion — central or branch retinal artery occlusion (CRAO/BRAO) → painless sudden vision loss; central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO) → "blood and thunder" fundus.
Age-related macular degeneration (AMD)Leading cause of irreversible vision loss in the elderly (>50 yr). Two forms: dry (atrophic) — drusen accumulation, RPE atrophy; wet (neovascular/exudative) — choroidal neovascularisation (CNV) → subretinal haemorrhage & scarring.
Retinitis pigmentosaGroup of inherited (usually autosomal dominant) retinal dystrophies characterised by progressive night blindness, visual field constriction ("tunnel vision"), and eventual blindness. Photoreceptor (rod > cone) degeneration.
Retinitis due to Candida infectionEndogenous Candida endophthalmitis — white, fluffy chorioretinal lesions. Seen in immunocompromised patients (IV drug users, ICU patients, neutropenic patients).
Retinal neoplasms
  • Retinoblastoma (Rb) — most common primary intraocular malignancy of children (see Section 6).
  • Retinal lymphoma — primary intraocular lymphoma, usually diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL); typically in elderly, immunocompromised patients.

6 · Retinoblastoma (Rb)

🔴 Short note: Retinoblastoma. (SU-17M) — Cover pathogenesis, morphology, and clinical features.

Retinoblastoma (Rb): the most common primary intraocular malignancy of children.

Age

Diagnosed usually prior to 4 years of age. Bilateral in ~30% of cases (hereditary form); unilateral in ~70% (sporadic form).

Pathogenesis (Important for VIVA)

Two mutations of the RB gene, located on chromosome 13q14, lead to neoplastic proliferation of the retinal cells.

  • Hereditary form: first mutation inherited (germline); second mutation acquired (somatic). Bilateral, earlier onset, higher risk of second primary tumours (e.g., osteosarcoma).
  • Sporadic form: both mutations acquired (somatic, "two-hit" in the same cell). Unilateral, later onset.
🎯 RB gene = chromosome 13q14 · "13" → unlucky number for Rb patients. Two-hit hypothesis (Knudson).

Morphology

  1. The pathology of retinoblastoma of both hereditary and sporadic type is identical.
  2. Tumours may contain both undifferentiated and differentiated elements.
  3. Undifferentiated elements: collections of small round cells with hyperchromatic nuclei ("small blue round cell tumour" of the eye).
  4. Viable tumour cells are found encircling tumour blood vessels, with zones of necrosis typically found in relatively avascular areas.
  5. Focal zones of dystrophic calcification are characteristic of retinoblastoma — visible on CT (computed tomography) scans.

Clinical features

  • Leukocoria ("white pupillary reflex") — most common presenting sign. A white mass reflex seen through the pupil.
  • Strabismus (squint) — misalignment of the eyes; second most common presentation.
  • May present with painful glaucoma, proptosis, or systemic metastases (optic nerve → CNS; bone; liver).
  • Extension: along the optic nerve into the CNS; haematogenous spread to bone, liver, lungs.

7 · Cataract

🔴 Short note: Cataract. Describe the causes (congenital and acquired).

Cataract: the term describes lenticular opacities (opacification of the crystalline lens) that may be congenital or acquired. Most common cause of preventable blindness worldwide.

Causes of cataract

CategorySpecific Causes
CongenitalGenetic syndromes (Down syndrome, Turner syndrome), intra-uterine infections (rubella, toxoplasmosis, CMV (cytomegalovirus)), metabolic disorders (galactosemia), trauma.
Acquired
  • Galactosemia — galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase deficiency → galactitol accumulation in the lens → osmotic swelling → cataract. Classically "oil-droplet" cataract in infants.
  • Diabetes mellitus (DM) — sorbitol accumulation (via aldose reductase) in the lens. Snowflake cataract.
  • Wilson disease — copper deposition in the lens → "sunflower cataract" (pathognomonic).
  • Atopic dermatitis — anterior subcapsular cataract.
  • Drugs — especially corticosteroids (posterior subcapsular cataract). Other drugs: chlorpromazine, amiodarone.
  • Radiation — ionising radiation or UV exposure.
  • Trauma — blunt or penetrating injury → traumatic cataract.
  • Age-related nuclear sclerosis — the most common cause overall. Progressive hardening and yellowing of the lens nucleus.

Morphology (gross)

The lens becomes white and opaque (mature cataract). Histologically: lens fibres swell, lose their nuclei, and form eosinophilic globules (Morgagnian globules) in the cortex.

Robbins 10e p.1313 · Arif 2025 p.375

8 · Glaucoma

🔴 Short note: Glaucoma. Classify glaucoma and describe its pathogenesis.

Glaucoma: the term refers to a collection of diseases characterised by distinctive changes in the visual field and in the cup of the optic nerve. Most glaucomas are associated with elevated intra-ocular pressure (IOP).

Pathogenesis

↑IOP → compression of retinal ganglion cell axons at the optic disc → ischaemic damage → progressive optic neuropathy → characteristic visual field loss (arcuate scotoma, nasal constriction → eventually "tunnel vision" and blindness).

IOP is determined by the balance between aqueous humour production (by the ciliary body) and drainage (through the trabecular meshwork into Schlemm canal).

Classification of glaucoma

TypeDescription
1. Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG)Most common type overall. Gradual, painless obstruction of the trabecular meshwork → slow ↑IOP. Peripheral visual field loss first; central vision preserved until late. "Silent thief of sight."
2. Primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG)Acute or chronic obstruction of aqueous outflow by the iris physically covering the trabecular meshwork. Predisposed by shallow anterior chamber, hypermetropia. Acute episode: sudden severe eye pain, headache, nausea, blurred vision, halos around lights, ↑↑IOP, fixed dilated pupil, red eye — ophthalmic emergency.
3. Secondary open-angle glaucomaObstruction of the trabecular meshwork by exfoliation material, pigment dispersion, traumatic hyphaema, neovascularisation, or corticosteroid use.
4. Secondary angle-closure glaucomaAngle closure due to lens swelling (intumescent cataract), iris neovascularisation (rubeosis iridis), synechiae, or tumours pushing the iris forward.
🎯 Open-angle = painless, chronic, "silent". Angle-closure = painful, acute, "emergency". Primary = no identifiable cause; Secondary = identifiable cause.

🔁 Rapid-Fire MCQ & Viva Sweep (incl. folded Systemic Pathology MCQs)

The Eye — True/False & one-liners (test yourself, then reveal)
Retinoblastoma is the most common primary intraocular malignancy of children (T/F).
True. It is diagnosed usually before the age of 4 years.
The RB gene is located on chromosome 13q14. Two mutations are required for retinoblastoma to develop (T/F).
True. Knudson's "two-hit" hypothesis. Hereditary form: one germline + one somatic; sporadic: two somatic.
Leukocoria (white pupillary reflex) is the most common presenting sign of retinoblastoma (T/F).
True. Strabismus (squint) is the second most common presentation.
Focal zones of dystrophic calcification are NOT characteristic of retinoblastoma (T/F).
False. Focal dystrophic calcification IS characteristic and visible on CT scans.
Capillary haemangioma of the orbit is the most common orbital tumour in adults (T/F).
False. Capillary haemangioma is the most common in infancy and early childhood. Cavernous haemangioma is most common in adults.
Cavernous haemangioma is the most common orbital tumour in adults (T/F).
True. Slowly progressive, well-encapsulated; surgical excision is curative.
Pleomorphic adenoma of the lacrimal gland is analogous to pleomorphic adenoma of the salivary gland (T/F).
True. Both are benign mixed tumours with risk of malignant transformation if incompletely excised.
Neuroblastoma and Wilms tumour can metastasise to the orbit (T/F).
True. Both are common paediatric malignancies that can metastasise to the orbit. Neuroblastoma may cause "raccoon eyes."
Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is the most common eyelid malignancy, while sebaceous carcinoma is the most common benign eyelid tumour (T/F).
False. BCC is the most common malignancy; sebaceous carcinoma is the second most common malignancy, not benign.
Sebaceous carcinoma of the eyelid arises from Meibomian glands (T/F).
True. Meibomian glands are modified sebaceous glands within the tarsal plate of the eyelid.
Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) affects premature infants and can lead to retinal detachment and blindness (T/F).
True. Vasoproliferative disorder of immature retinal vasculature in premature infants.
Diabetic retinopathy is classified as non-proliferative and proliferative. Neovascularisation characterises the proliferative stage (T/F).
True. Proliferative diabetic retinopathy → neovascularisation → vitreous haemorrhage → tractional retinal detachment.
Cataract is a lenticular opacity that can be congenital or acquired. The most common acquired cause is age-related nuclear sclerosis (T/F).
True. Age-related nuclear sclerosis is the most common cause of acquired cataract worldwide.
"Sunflower cataract" is associated with which metabolic disease?
Wilson disease (hepatolenticular degeneration) — copper deposition in the lens forms a sunflower-shaped opacity.
"Oil-droplet" cataract is characteristic of which metabolic disorder?
Galactosemia — galactitol accumulation in the lens → osmotic swelling → characteristic oil-droplet appearance on slit-lamp examination.
Posterior subcapsular cataract is associated with corticosteroid use (T/F).
True. Corticosteroids are the most common drug cause of posterior subcapsular cataract.
Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) is the most common type of glaucoma (T/F).
True. POAG is painless, chronic, and often asymptomatic until advanced — "silent thief of sight."
Acute angle-closure glaucoma is an ophthalmic emergency characterised by sudden severe eye pain, a fixed dilated pupil, and markedly elevated intra-ocular pressure (T/F).
True. Requires urgent treatment to prevent permanent optic nerve damage and blindness.
🎯 One-liner: most common primary intraocular malignancy of children? Most common orbital tumour of infancy? Most common orbital tumour in adults? Most common eyelid malignancy?
Retinoblastoma · Capillary haemangioma · Cavernous haemangioma · Basal cell carcinoma (BCC).
🎯 One-liner: what connects "raccoon eyes" to an orbital tumour?
Metastatic neuroblastoma → periorbital ecchymosis ("raccoon eyes") in children.
🎯 One-liner: which corneal dystrophy classically affects women and may require corneal transplant?
Fuchs endothelial dystrophy — progressive loss of endothelial cells → stromal oedema → bullous keratopathy.
🎯 One-liner: what is the mechanism of glaucoma-induced optic nerve damage?
Elevated IOP → compression of retinal ganglion cell axons at the optic disc → ischaemic damage → progressive optic neuropathy.
