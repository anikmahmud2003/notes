🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of the Eye (boss-level overview)

The eye is organised from anterior → posterior: conjunctiva → cornea → lens → retina → choroid. Disease can strike any layer. This chapter covers:

1. Conjunctival diseases — scarring, pinguecula, pterygium, neoplasms (squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), conjunctival intra-epithelial neoplasia (CIN), mucoepidermoid carcinoma, nevi, melanoma).
2. Corneal diseases — keratitis & ulcers, band keratopathy, keratoconus, Fuchs endothelial dystrophy, stromal dystrophies.
3. Orbital neoplasms — primary (capillary haemangioma, lymphangioma, cavernous haemangioma, pleomorphic adenoma of lacrimal gland, dermoid cyst, neurilemmoma, orbital lymphoma) and secondary/metastatic (prostatic carcinoma, neuroblastoma, Wilms tumour).
4. Eyelid malignancies — basal cell carcinoma (BCC), sebaceous carcinoma.
5. Retinal diseases — detachment, vascular disease (hypertensive, diabetic, retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), radiation, vasculitis, sickle-cell retinopathy, artery/vein occlusion), age-related macular degeneration (AMD), retinitis pigmentosa, retinitis due to Candida, neoplasms (retinoblastoma (Rb), retinal lymphoma).
6. Retinoblastoma — most common primary intraocular malignancy of children; RB gene on chromosome 13q14.
7. Cataract — congenital & acquired lenticular opacities (galactosemia, diabetes mellitus (DM), Wilson disease, drugs, radiation, trauma, age-related).
8. Glaucoma — elevated intra-ocular pressure (IOP) → visual field loss & optic-nerve cupping; classified as primary (open-angle = most common; angle-closure) and secondary.

1 · Conjunctival & Corneal Diseases

What are the conjunctival diseases?
  1. Conjunctival scarring — due to chronic inflammation (trachoma, Stevens-Johnson syndrome), chemical burns, or surgical trauma.
  2. Pinguecula — yellowish elevated plaque on the bulbar conjunctiva (nasal side); associated with UV exposure.
  3. Pterygium — wing-shaped fibrovascular growth extending from conjunctiva onto the cornea; associated with UV light and chronic irritation.
  4. Neoplasms — SCC, CIN, mucoepidermoid carcinoma, nevi, melanoma (see next cards).

Robbins 10e p.1310

What is the most common malignant conjunctival tumour?

Conjunctival squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) — arises from the conjunctival epithelium, often at the limbus. Risk factors: UV exposure, HIV/immunosuppression.

Robbins 10e p.1310

What is conjunctival intra-epithelial neoplasia (CIN)?

A precursor / pre-malignant lesion; graded I–III based on thickness of epithelium involved. May progress to invasive SCC.

Robbins 10e p.1310

Distinguish mucoepidermoid carcinoma from pure SCC of the conjunctiva.

Mucoepidermoid carcinoma contains mucus-secreting (goblet) cells interspersed with squamous cells. It is more aggressive with a higher recurrence rate than pure SCC.

Robbins 10e p.1310

What are conjunctival nevi and conjunctival melanoma?

Conjunctival nevi: benign melanocytic naevi; common in young adults; usually pigmented, may show cystic changes.

Conjunctival melanoma: malignant melanoma that may arise from a pre-existing nevus, CIN, or de novo. Can metastasise haematogenously and via lymphatics.

Robbins 10e p.1310

2 · Corneal Diseases (detail)

What are the diseases of the cornea?
  1. Keratitis and ulcers — infectious (bacterial, fungal, viral — especially HSV) or non-infectious. Corneal ulceration can be sight-threatening if it perforates.
  2. Corneal degenerations and dystrophies — band keratopathy, keratoconus, Fuchs endothelial dystrophy, stromal dystrophies.

Robbins 10e p.1311

What is band keratopathy?

Calcium phosphate deposition in Bowman layer of the cornea, forming a horizontal "band" across the inter-palpebral zone. Associated with chronic eye disease, hypercalcaemia, and renal failure.

Robbins 10e p.1311

What is keratoconus?

Non-inflammatory, progressive thinning and cone-shaped protrusion of the cornea. Usually bilateral; often presents in adolescence. Leads to irregular astigmatism.

Robbins 10e p.1311

What is Fuchs endothelial dystrophy?

Progressive loss of corneal endothelial cells → stromal oedema → epithelial bullae ("bullous keratopathy"). Usually bilateral; more common in women. May require corneal transplant (penetrating keratoplasty) in advanced stages.

Robbins 10e p.1311

What are corneal stromal dystrophies?

Group of inherited corneal opacities affecting the stroma. Includes granular, lattice, and macular dystrophies. Typically bilateral, symmetric, and slowly progressive.

Robbins 10e p.1311

3 · Orbital Neoplasms

What is the most common orbital tumour of infancy?

Capillary haemangioma — benign vascular proliferation of capillary-sized vessels. Usually involutes spontaneously by age 5–7 yr.

Robbins 10e p.1307

What is the most common orbital tumour in adults?

Cavernous haemangioma — slowly progressive proptosis. Well-encapsulated; surgical excision is curative.

Robbins 10e p.1307

What is a lymphangioma of the orbit?

Benign lymphatic-channel tumour. May enlarge and cause proptosis. Can undergo spontaneous haemorrhage → chocolate cyst.

Robbins 10e p.1307

What is pleomorphic adenoma of the lacrimal gland?

Benign mixed tumour of the lacrimal gland (analogous to salivary-gland pleomorphic adenoma). Slowly growing, painless superolateral proptosis. Risk of malignant transformation if incompletely excised.

Robbins 10e p.1307

What is the most common congenital orbital mass in children?

Dermoid cyst — congenital choristoma containing ectodermal derivatives (skin, hair follicles, sebaceous glands).

Robbins 10e p.1307

What is a neurilemmoma (schwannoma) of the orbit?

Benign nerve-sheath tumour arising from Schwann cells. Well-encapsulated, slow-growing.

Robbins 10e p.1307

What is orbital lymphoma?

Typically Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), most commonly MALT lymphoma. Painless, gradually progressive proptosis. Bilateral involvement possible.

Robbins 10e p.1307

Name the secondary (metastatic) orbital neoplasms.
  • Metastatic prostatic carcinoma — in older males; often bilateral.
  • Metastatic neuroblastoma — in children; may cause "raccoon eyes" (periorbital ecchymosis).
  • Metastatic Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma) — paediatric renal malignancy that can metastasise to the orbit.

Robbins 10e p.1307

4 · Eyelid Malignancies

What is the most common eyelid malignancy?

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) — slow-growing, locally invasive but rarely metastasises. Typically on the lower eyelid. Pearly, translucent nodule with telangiectasia and central ulceration ("rodent ulcer"). Risk factors: UV exposure, fair skin, chronic sun damage.

Robbins 10e p.1308

What is sebaceous carcinoma of the eyelid?

Second most common eyelid malignancy. Arises from Meibomian glands (tarsal plate) or sebaceous glands. More aggressive than BCC; may metastasise. Can mimic chalazion or chronic blepharitis → high index of suspicion needed.

🎯 Bottom eyelid = BCC (most common) · Upper eyelid = Sebaceous carcinoma (think Meibomian gland origin).

Robbins 10e p.1308

5 · Retinal Diseases

What is retinal detachment?

Separation of the neurosensory retina from the underlying retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Two types: rhegmatogenous (retinal break → vitreous seeps under retina) and exudative/tractional (secondary to inflammation, tumour, or proliferative membranes).

Robbins 10e p.1319

What are the retinal vascular diseases?
  • Hypertensive retinopathy — arteriolar narrowing, flame-shaped haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, hard exudates, papilloedema (grade IV).
  • Diabetic retinopathy — non-proliferative (microaneurysms, dot-and-blot haemorrhages, hard exudates, cotton-wool spots) and proliferative (neovascularisation → vitreous haemorrhage → tractional detachment).
  • Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) — vasoproliferative disorder of immature retinal vasculature in premature infants. May lead to retinal detachment and blindness.
  • Radiation retinopathy — damage to retinal vasculature following ionising radiation.
  • Retinal vasculitis — inflammation of retinal vessels; idiopathic or secondary to systemic disease.
  • Sickle-cell retinopathy — proliferative retinal neovascularisation in sickle-cell disease due to retinal ischaemia from vascular occlusion.
  • Retinal artery/vein occlusion — CRAO/BRAO → painless sudden vision loss; CRVO → "blood and thunder" fundus.

Robbins 10e p.1319

What is age-related macular degeneration (AMD)?

Leading cause of irreversible vision loss in the elderly (>50 yr). Two forms: dry (atrophic) — drusen accumulation, RPE atrophy; wet (neovascular/exudative) — choroidal neovascularisation (CNV) → subretinal haemorrhage & scarring.

Robbins 10e p.1319

What is retinitis pigmentosa?

Group of inherited (usually autosomal dominant) retinal dystrophies characterised by progressive night blindness, visual field constriction ("tunnel vision"), and eventual blindness. Photoreceptor (rod > cone) degeneration.

Robbins 10e p.1319

What causes Candida retinitis?

Endogenous Candida endophthalmitis — white, fluffy chorioretinal lesions. Seen in immunocompromised patients (IV drug users, ICU patients, neutropenic patients).

Robbins 10e p.1319

What is retinal lymphoma?

Primary intraocular lymphoma, usually diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL); typically in elderly, immunocompromised patients.

Robbins 10e p.1319

6 · Retinoblastoma (Rb)

What is retinoblastoma? Which chromosome is involved?

The most common primary intraocular malignancy of children. Diagnosed usually prior to 4 years of age. Bilateral in ~30% (hereditary); unilateral in ~70% (sporadic). Gene: RB gene on chromosome 13q14.

🎯 "13" → unlucky number for Rb patients. Two-hit hypothesis (Knudson).

Robbins 10e p.1324

Explain the two-hit hypothesis in retinoblastoma.

Two mutations of the RB gene on chromosome 13q14 lead to neoplastic proliferation.

  • Hereditary form: first mutation inherited (germline); second acquired (somatic). Bilateral, earlier onset, higher risk of second primary tumours (e.g., osteosarcoma).
  • Sporadic form: both mutations acquired (somatic, "two-hit" in same cell). Unilateral, later onset.

Robbins 10e p.1324

Describe the morphology of retinoblastoma.
  1. Pathology of both hereditary and sporadic type is identical.
  2. Tumours may contain both undifferentiated and differentiated elements.
  3. Undifferentiated elements: collections of small round cells with hyperchromatic nuclei ("small blue round cell tumour" of the eye).
  4. Viable tumour cells encircle tumour blood vessels; zones of necrosis in avascular areas.
  5. Focal zones of dystrophic calcification are characteristic — visible on CT scans.

Robbins 10e p.1324

What are the clinical features of retinoblastoma?
  • Leukocoria ("white pupillary reflex") — most common presenting sign.
  • Strabismus (squint) — second most common presentation.
  • May present with painful glaucoma, proptosis, or systemic metastases (optic nerve → CNS; bone; liver).
  • Extension: along the optic nerve into CNS; haematogenous spread to bone, liver, lungs.

Robbins 10e p.1324

7 · Cataract

Define cataract. What are the congenital causes?

Cataract: opacification of the crystalline lens — may be congenital or acquired. Most common cause of preventable blindness worldwide.

Congenital causes: Genetic syndromes (Down, Turner), intra-uterine infections (rubella, toxoplasmosis, CMV), metabolic disorders (galactosemia), trauma.

Robbins 10e p.1313

What metabolic diseases cause cataracts?
  • Galactosemia — galactitol accumulation → osmotic swelling → "oil-droplet" cataract in infants.
  • Diabetes mellitus — sorbitol accumulation (via aldose reductase) → snowflake cataract.
  • Wilson disease — copper deposition → "sunflower cataract" (pathognomonic).

Robbins 10e p.1313

What is the most common cause of acquired cataract?

Age-related nuclear sclerosis — progressive hardening and yellowing of the lens nucleus. The most common cause overall.

Robbins 10e p.1313

Which drugs cause cataracts? Describe the morphology.

Corticosteroids → posterior subcapsular cataract (most common drug cause). Other drugs: chlorpromazine, amiodarone. Radiation (ionising or UV) and trauma also cause cataracts.

Morphology: lens becomes white and opaque (mature cataract). Histologically: lens fibres swell, lose nuclei, form eosinophilic Morgagnian globules in the cortex.

Robbins 10e p.1313

8 · Glaucoma

Define glaucoma. What determines IOP?

Glaucoma: a collection of diseases characterised by distinctive changes in the visual field and in the cup of the optic nerve, most associated with elevated intra-ocular pressure (IOP).

IOP is determined by the balance between aqueous humour production (ciliary body) and drainage (trabecular meshwork → Schlemm canal).

Robbins 10e p.1314

Describe the pathogenesis of glaucomatous optic nerve damage.

↑IOP → compression of retinal ganglion cell axons at the optic disc → ischaemic damage → progressive optic neuropathy → visual field loss (arcuate scotoma, nasal constriction → eventually "tunnel vision" and blindness).

Robbins 10e p.1314

What is primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG)?

Most common type overall. Gradual, painless obstruction of the trabecular meshwork → slow ↑IOP. Peripheral visual field loss first; central vision preserved until late. "Silent thief of sight."

Robbins 10e p.1314

What is primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG)?

Acute or chronic obstruction by the iris physically covering the trabecular meshwork. Predisposed by shallow anterior chamber, hypermetropia. Acute episode: sudden severe eye pain, headache, nausea, blurred vision, halos, ↑↑IOP, fixed dilated pupil, red eye — ophthalmic emergency.

Robbins 10e p.1314

What causes secondary glaucoma?

Secondary open-angle: exfoliation material, pigment dispersion, traumatic hyphaema, neovascularisation, corticosteroid use.

Secondary angle-closure: lens swelling (intumescent cataract), iris neovascularisation (rubeosis iridis), synechiae, tumours pushing iris forward.

🎯 Open-angle = painless, chronic, "silent". Angle-closure = painful, acute, "emergency".

Robbins 10e p.1314
