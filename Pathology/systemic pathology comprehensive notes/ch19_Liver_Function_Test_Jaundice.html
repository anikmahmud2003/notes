🧠 Big Picture — Liver Function Tests & Jaundice

The liver performs 6 major functions: secretory, metabolic, synthetic, excretory, storage, and detoxification. Liver Function Tests (LFTs) are biochemical investigations that assess these capacities — grouped as excretory (serum bilirubin, urine bilirubin, urobilinogen, stercobilinogen), enzymatic (ALT/AST for hepatocellular damage; ALP/GGT for biliary obstruction), synthetic (serum albumin, albumin/globulin ratio, prothrombin time, total protein), detoxification (serum ammonia), and other tests (electrolytes, iron, copper, AFP, viral markers, immunological tests).

Bilirubin metabolism: Haem → biliverdin → unconjugated bilirubin (indirect, lipid-soluble, albumin-bound) → liver conjugation → conjugated bilirubin (direct, water-soluble) → bile → intestine → stercobilinogen (dark stool) / urobilinogen (some reabsorbed, excreted in urine).

Jaundice = yellow discolouration when serum bilirubin >3 mg/dL. Three clinico-pathological types: Pre-hepatic (haemolytic) — excess unconjugated bilirubin from RBC destruction; Hepatic (hepatocellular) — liver cell damage → mixed; Post-hepatic (obstructive) — bile duct blockage → conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia. The van den Bergh reaction distinguishes direct (conjugated) from indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin.

Key clinical correlations: physiological jaundice of newborn (immature liver), kernicterus (bilirubin brain toxicity in neonates), obstructive jaundice triad (conjugated bilirubin ↑, pruritus, pale stool), Crigler-Najjar (absent conjugation enzyme), Dubin-Johnson (excretion defect), and cholestasis workup (ALP & GGT together raised).

1 · Functions of the Liver

🔴 Enumerate the functions of the liver.

The liver performs six major groups of functions:

  1. Secretory function: formation and secretion of bile.
  2. Metabolic function (nutrient & vitamin metabolism): main organ for metabolism of glucose and other sugars, amino acids, lipids (fatty acids, cholesterol, lipoproteins), fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and water-soluble vitamins (B₁₂, folic acid). Also the main site for alcohol metabolism.
  3. Synthetic function: synthesis of plasma proteins (except gamma-globulins, which are made by plasma cells), vitamin-K-dependent clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X), steroid-binding and other hormone-binding proteins.
  4. Excretory function: excretes drugs, alcohol, bilirubin, hormones, cholesterol, etc.
  5. Storage function: stores glycogen, vitamin A, D, B₁₂, iron, folic acid.
  6. Detoxification function: detoxifies and inactivates toxins, steroids, other hormones, ammonia (NH₃), and drugs such as penicillin, ampicillin, erythromycin.
  7. Haemopoietic function: in the first trimester of foetal life, the liver is the main site of red blood cell (RBC) production.

2 · What Are Liver Function Tests (LFTs)?

What do you mean by Liver Function Tests (LFTs)? Give their indications.

Definition: Liver Function Tests (LFTs) are biochemical investigations to assess the capacity of the liver to carry out any of its functions.

Indications / significance of LFTs

  1. Differential diagnosis and progress of jaundice
  2. To assess the extent of liver damage
  3. To follow the progress of liver damage
  4. To identify the specific cause of liver damage
  5. To investigate possible complications

Prof. Khaleque/196; Davidson's 20e/940

Enumerate the different liver function tests grouped by function, with normal values.

1. Tests for excretory function

  • In blood — Serum bilirubin: Normal = 0.3–1.0 mg/dL (5–17 μmol/L). In serum, bilirubin is normally almost all unconjugated. Subdivided into:
    • Direct (conjugated) bilirubin = 0.25 mg% (6 μmol/L)
    • Indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin = 0.75 mg% (11 μmol/L)
  • In urine: urinary bilirubin, urobilinogen
  • In stool: stercobilinogen

2. Tests for enzymatic function

  • a. Evidence of hepatocellular damage (e.g. hepatitis):
    • Alanine aminotransferase (ALT / SGPT) — Normal: 10–40 U/L
    • Aspartate aminotransferase (AST / SGOT) — Normal: 10–35 U/L
  • b. Evidence of biliary obstruction (e.g. obstructive jaundice):
    • Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) — Normal: 40–125 U/L
    • γ-Glutamyl transferase (GGT) — Male: 10–55 U/L; Female: 5–35 U/L

3. Tests for synthetic function

  • Serum albumin: Normal ≈ 3.5–5.0 g/dL (mean 4.8 g/dL). A falling albumin indicates severity of chronic liver disease (CLD) — bad prognostic sign.
  • Albumin/Globulin (A/G) ratio: Normal = 1.7 : 1
  • Prothrombin time (PT): Normal = 12–16 seconds. Sensitive indicator of both acute and chronic liver disease.
  • Serum total protein: Normal = 6–8 g/dL

4. Tests for detoxification function

  • Serum ammonia concentration

5. Other biochemical tests

  • Serum electrolytes (hyponatraemia may occur in severe liver disease)
  • Blood urea level
  • Serum iron, transferrin saturation, serum ferritin (hereditary haemochromatosis)
  • Serum & urinary copper, serum ceruloplasmin (Wilson's disease)

6. Tumour marker

  • α-Fetoprotein (AFP) for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)

7. Viral markers

  • Different viral markers for hepatitis A, B, C, D & E (HBsAg, HBeAg, anti-HCV, etc.)

8. Immunological tests

  • Anti-mitochondrial antibody (AMA) — primary biliary cirrhosis (PBC)
  • Antinuclear antibody (ANA) — autoimmune hepatitis (AIH)
  • Anti-smooth muscle antibody (ASMA) — autoimmune hepatitis
List the investigations for diagnosis of liver disease beyond basic LFTs.

A. Liver function tests

(As enumerated above — serum bilirubin, ALT, AST, GGT, ALP, PT, total protein, albumin, A/G ratio, urinary bilirubin, urobilinogen, CBC)

B. Specific aetiological investigations

  • Viral markers in hepatitis A, B, C, D and E
  • α-Fetoprotein (AFP) in hepatocellular carcinoma
  • Immunological tests (auto-antibodies) for autoimmune liver disease (e.g. primary biliary cirrhosis)
  • Serum iron, transferrin saturation, serum ferritin in hereditary haemochromatosis
  • Serum & urinary copper, and serum ceruloplasmin in Wilson's disease

C. Imaging of liver

CT scan or MRI

D. Liver biopsy

🎯 Memory aid: ALT = viral hepatitis (most specific for liver); AST = MI + non-viral hepatitis; ALP = cholestasis + bone disease + pregnancy; GGT = alcoholic liver disease + cholestasis.

Enzymes elevated in hepatocellular jaundice: ALT (SGPT) and AST (SGOT).

Enzymes elevated in obstructive jaundice: ALP and GGT.

Which one is more liver-specific: ALT or AST? Why?

ALT is more specific for liver damage than AST, because AST is also present in heart, brain, kidney, and muscle. High levels of AST are seen in:

  • Hepatic necrosis
  • Myocardial infarction (MI)
  • Congestive cardiac failure (CCF)
  • Muscle injury

Therefore, an elevated AST alone does not confirm liver-specific damage.

🔴 What are the best markers of chronic liver disease (CLD)?

The best markers of chronic liver disease (CLD) are:

  1. Total protein and albumin/globulin (A/G) ratio
  2. Prothrombin time (PT) — PT is better than total protein as a CLD marker

🎯 Always remember: PT is better than total protein for assessing CLD severity.

Mention the sites where ALP is found.
  1. Liver (bile duct epithelium)
  2. Bone (osteoblasts)
  3. Intestine
  4. Placenta
When may ALP be increased? How to differentiate cholestasis from bone disease?
  1. Cholestatic jaundice — 4–6× normal limit
  2. Bone disease (e.g. Paget disease, osteomalacia, fractures, rickets, bone tumours)
  3. Pregnancy (placental ALP)

How to differentiate cholestasis from bone disease? If cholestasis — both ALP and GGT are increased. If bone disease or pregnancy — only ALP is increased, GGT is normal.

What are the markers of alcoholic liver disease?

The markers of alcoholic liver disease are:

  1. GGT (γ-glutamyl transferase) — most sensitive marker; elevated due to enzyme induction by alcohol
  2. AST (aspartate aminotransferase) — AST/ALT ratio >2:1 is suggestive of alcoholic liver disease
🎯 GGT is the best screening marker for alcohol abuse.

4 · Synthetic Function Tests — Albumin, PT, Total Protein

Why is prothrombin time (PT) important?

Prothrombin time (PT) is a coagulation screening test that measures how long it takes blood to clot. It is the most sensitive and most specific indicator of both acute and chronic liver disease.

🎯 PT test may also be called an INR (International Normalized Ratio) test — a way of standardizing PT results across laboratories.
What are the causes of raised (prolonged) PT?
  1. Liver disease — deficiency of factors I, II, V, VII, X (factors II, VII, IX, X are vitamin-K-dependent)
  2. Vitamin K deficiency — impaired lipid absorption (bile duct obstruction)
  3. Warfarin therapy (anticoagulant that blocks vitamin-K-dependent factor synthesis)
  4. DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation) — consumption of clotting factors
Why is prothrombin time more specific than total protein estimation?

Prothrombin is produced only by the liver. In contrast, some plasma proteins such as immunoglobulins are produced outside the liver (by plasma cells). Therefore, total protein concentration in blood does not directly reflect the status of liver function.

That is why PT is more specific than total protein estimation for assessing liver synthetic capacity.

Why is prothrombin time prolonged in obstructive jaundice?

In obstructive jaundice, obstruction of the bile duct and intrahepatic cholestasis result in:

  1. Impaired bile flow → impaired lipid (fat) absorption in the gut
  2. Resultant vitamin K deficiency (vitamin K is fat-soluble and requires bile salts for absorption)
  3. Impaired synthesis of vitamin-K-dependent clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X)
  4. Prolonged prothrombin time

5 · Bilirubin Metabolism & Hyperbilirubinaemia

What is hyperbilirubinaemia? When does clinical jaundice appear?

Hyperbilirubinaemia: serum bilirubin >1 mg/dL (1 mg%). Normal total bilirubin = 0.3–1.0 mg/dL (5–17 μmol/L). Clinical jaundice appears when bilirubin exceeds 3 mg/dL (50 μmol/L). The sub-clinical range (1–3 mg/dL) is detectable by lab but no visible jaundice.

Lippincott's 3e/283; Prof. Khaleque/194

Define direct and indirect bilirubin.

Direct (conjugated) bilirubin

Conjugated bilirubin is called direct bilirubin. In aqueous solution, it is water-soluble and reacts rapidly (within 1 minute) with the reagent diazotized sulfanilic acid in the van den Bergh reaction — that is why it is called "direct reacting" or direct bilirubin. Present in urine (bilirubinuria) when raised.

Indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin

Unconjugated bilirubin is called indirect bilirubin. It is not soluble in water; in plasma it is bound to albumin. It is obtained by subtracting the direct-reacting bilirubin from the total bilirubin. Cannot pass through urine (bound to albumin, too large for glomerular filtration). Lipid-soluble → can cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) → toxic to brain (kernicterus).

Prof. Khaleque/194–195

🔴 State the causes of unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia.

Causes:

  1. Excess production of bilirubin — e.g. haemolytic anaemia (sickle cell anaemia, thalassaemia, autoimmune haemolytic disease, G6PD deficiency)
  2. Reduced hepatic uptake — e.g. Gilbert's syndrome (autosomal dominant)
  3. Impaired bilirubin conjugation — e.g. physiological jaundice of the newborn, Crigler-Najjar syndrome

Clinical importance: even a small fraction of unbound unconjugated bilirubin may diffuse into tissues — particularly the brain of infants → toxic injury (kernicterus).

Prof. Khaleque/194

State the causes of conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia.

Causes of conjugated (direct) hyperbilirubinaemia:

  1. Cirrhosis of liver
  2. Crigler-Najjar syndrome (some types)
  3. Dubin-Johnson syndrome — decreased hepatic excretion of conjugated bilirubin (autosomal recessive)
  4. Rotor syndrome — decreased hepatic excretion of conjugated bilirubin (autosomal dominant)
  5. Intrahepatic cholestasis (viral hepatitis, drugs, primary biliary cirrhosis, alcoholic hepatitis)
  6. Extrahepatic cholestasis (CBD stones, pancreatic head carcinoma, bile duct stricture)

6 · Jaundice — Definition & Classification

Define jaundice. At what bilirubin level does it become visible?

Definition: Jaundice (icterus) is a clinical condition characterised by yellow discolouration of the skin, sclera, and mucous membranes due to increased bilirubin concentration in body fluids above normal.

  • Normal serum bilirubin = 0.3–1.0 mg/dL (5–17 μmol/L)
  • Clinical jaundice appears when bilirubin > 3 mg/dL (50 μmol/L)
  • Sub-clinical jaundice: bilirubin >1 mg/dL but <3 mg/dL (no visible jaundice)
Give the pathophysiological classification of jaundice.
  1. Predominantly unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia:
    • Excess production (e.g. haemolytic anaemia)
    • Reduced hepatic uptake (e.g. Gilbert's syndrome)
    • Impaired bilirubin conjugation (e.g. physiological jaundice of newborn, Crigler-Najjar)
  2. Predominantly conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia: intrahepatic & extrahepatic cholestasis
Give the clinico-pathological classification of jaundice with causes.

1. Haemolytic / Pre-hepatic jaundice: results from excessive breakdown of RBCs → excessive production of unconjugated bilirubin.

  • Congenital RBC defects: sickle cell anaemia, thalassaemia, haemoglobinopathies, hereditary spherocytosis
  • Incompatible blood transfusion
  • Malaria
  • Pyruvate kinase deficiency
  • Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency
  • Autoimmune haemolytic disease
  • Certain drugs (e.g. quinine)
  • Snake venom
  • Erythroblastosis foetalis

2. Hepatocellular / Hepatic jaundice: liver cell damage → decreased conjugation → unconjugated bilirubin rises; if excretion also impaired → conjugated rises too (mixed picture).

  • Viral hepatitis (A, B, C, D, E)
  • Chronic alcoholic hepatitis
  • Liver cirrhosis
  • Infections: yellow fever, septicaemia
  • Poisons: arsenic, CCl₄, phosphorus, paracetamol poisoning
  • Cardiac failure

3. Obstructive / Post-hepatic jaundice: obstruction of bile duct prevents bile passage into intestine → cholestasis → bilirubin in serum is conjugated.

Obstruction may be intrahepatic or extrahepatic:

  • Intrahepatic cholestasis: viral hepatitis, drugs (methyltestosterone, oestrogen, oral contraceptives), primary biliary cirrhosis, alcoholic hepatitis, pregnancy, post-operative
  • Extrahepatic cholestasis: stone in common bile duct (CBD), carcinoma of head of pancreas/ampulla of Vater, metastatic carcinoma of CBD, stricture of CBD, cysts in CBD, Ascaris impaction in CBD, pressure from enlarged coeliac lymph nodes, traumatic biliary stricture
🎯 Pre-hepatic = RBC breakdown; Hepatic = liver damage; Post-hepatic = bile duct blockage.
What is choluric jaundice? What is acholuric jaundice?

Choluric jaundice: jaundice with presence of bilirubin in urine. Occurs in hepatocellular jaundice and obstructive jaundice (both have conjugated bilirubin which is water-soluble → passes through urine).

Acholuric jaundice: jaundice without presence of bilirubin in urine. Occurs in haemolytic jaundice — unconjugated bilirubin is water-insoluble and tightly albumin-bound → cannot be excreted in urine.

8 · Obstructive (Post-hepatic) Jaundice

Give the pathogenesis and etiology of obstructive jaundice.

Pathogenesis

Intrahepatic & extrahepatic bile flow obstruction → bile cannot drain into intestine → absorption of conjugated bilirubin into blood → conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia → obstructive jaundice.

Etiology / Causes (VIVA must-know)

  • Intrahepatic cholestasis: viral hepatitis, drugs (methyltestosterone, oestrogen, oral contraceptives), primary biliary cirrhosis, alcoholic hepatitis, pregnancy, post-operative
  • Extrahepatic cholestasis: CBD stone, carcinoma of head of pancreas, carcinoma of ampulla of Vater, metastatic carcinoma of CBD, stricture of CBD, cysts in CBD, Ascaris impaction, pressure from enlarged coeliac lymph nodes, traumatic biliary stricture

Prof. Khaleque/195

Write the LFT findings in obstructive jaundice.
  1. Serum bilirubin: conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia
  2. Serum ALP: markedly raised (4–6× normal)
  3. PT: prolonged (vitamin K deficiency due to impaired fat absorption)
  4. Urine urobilinogen: reduced or absent
  5. Stool stercobilinogen: reduced or absent
What are the complications and 3 cardinal features of obstructive jaundice?

Complications of obstructive jaundice (VIVA important)

  1. Pruritus — deposition of bile salts in skin → degranulation of mast cells → histamine release → itching
  2. Indigestion and malabsorption (steatorrhoea) — absence of bile salts in gut → fat not digested
  3. Dark urine — bilirubin (water-soluble conjugated) present in urine
  4. Prolonged PT → bleeding tendency

3 cardinal features of obstructive jaundice

  1. Raised conjugated bilirubin
  2. Generalised itching (pruritus)
  3. Pale stool + urinary bilirubin present
A 30-year-old female presented with jaundice, pruritus, bulky pale stool, and raised ALP. What is your clinical diagnosis? List the extrahepatic causes.

Clinical diagnosis: Obstructive jaundice.

Extrahepatic causes:

  • Stone in common bile duct (CBD)
  • Carcinoma of head of pancreas / ampulla of Vater
  • Metastatic carcinoma of CBD
  • Stricture of CBD (traumatic or inflammatory)
  • Cysts in CBD
  • Ascaris impaction in CBD
  • Pressure from enlarged coeliac lymph nodes
A 60-year-old male presented with severe jaundice with history of carcinoma of head of pancreas. What type of jaundice? What will be the LFT and urine findings?

Type of jaundice: Obstructive (post-hepatic) jaundice.

LFT findings:

  • Serum bilirubin: raised (conjugated)
  • ALP: very high
  • PT: prolonged
  • AST/ALT: slightly raised

Urine findings:

  • Bilirubin: present (bilirubinuria)
  • Urobilinogen: decreased or absent
  • Bile salts: present
A known haemolytic anaemia patient presents with jaundice and itching; USG shows gall stone. What type of jaundice? What LFT changes?

Type of jaundice: Obstructive jaundice (gall stone causing extrahepatic obstruction in addition to underlying haemolysis).

LFT changes: conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, markedly raised ALP, prolonged PT, reduced urinary urobilinogen, pale stool.

Why is there generalised itching (pruritus) in obstructive jaundice?

Due to cholestasis, bile salts cannot be excreted → accumulation occurs in skin → degranulation of mast cells → increased histamine release → generalised itching.

Why is there pale stool in obstructive jaundice?

No bile in intestine → no digestion of fat → steatorrhoea → pale, bulky, frothy stool. Also, no stercobilinogen (which normally gives stool its brown colour).

Why is bilirubin present in urine in obstructive but not haemolytic jaundice?
  • Obstructive jaundice: conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble → excreted through urine (bilirubinuria)
  • Haemolytic jaundice: unconjugated bilirubin is water-insoluble and tightly albumin-bound → NOT excreted through urine

9 · Physiological Jaundice of Newborn

What is physiological jaundice?

Definition: Most infants develop visible jaundice due to elevation of unconjugated bilirubin during the first week of life. This condition is called physiological jaundice. It generally appears at 2–4 days of age and disappears by 1–2 weeks. It is harmless and doesn't usually cause a problem.

What is the mechanism of physiological jaundice?
  1. Newborns make more bilirubin than adults — more RBC turnover
  2. A newborn's still-developing liver may not yet be able to remove adequate bilirubin from the blood (immature glucuronyl transferase)
  3. Increased enterohepatic recirculation — too much bilirubin reabsorbed from intestines before the baby eliminates it in stool
What is the treatment and complications of physiological jaundice?

Treatment

Usually not needed. Jaundice usually resolves within 1–2 weeks. Sometimes phototherapy is needed for very high bilirubin levels.

Possible complications (rare but serious)

  • Cerebral palsy
  • Deafness
  • Kernicterus (brain damage from very high bilirubin levels)
Why does jaundice appear in premature babies?

In premature babies, the liver cannot properly conjugate bilirubin (immature glucuronyl transferase enzyme). As a result, jaundice occurs due to unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia.

  • Jaundice develops on or after 2nd or 3rd day of life and resolves by 14 days
  • Peak unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia may reach 10–12 mg/dL on the 5th day, up to maximum 15 mg/dL, then falls
  • Direct fraction is <15% of the total

10 · Kernicterus

What is kernicterus?

Definition: Kernicterus is a neurological syndrome resulting from bilirubin toxicity within the brain — staining and necrosis of neurons in the basal ganglia, hippocampal cortex, subthalamic nuclei, and cerebellum, followed by gliosis of those areas. The cerebral cortex is usually spared.

M R Khan 3e/69–70

What are the causes of kernicterus?
  1. Prematurity
  2. Haemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN)
  3. Congenital familial non-hemolytic jaundice (Crigler-Najjar syndrome)
  4. Neonatal hepatitis
  5. Congenital spherocytosis
What is the treatment of kernicterus?
  1. Appearance of clinical signs suggesting bilirubin toxicity or encephalopathy is an indication for exchange transfusion at any level of serum bilirubin
  2. Once established, kernicterus is irreversible — management is only symptomatic

Liver Function Test & Jaundice — One-Liners & Mnemonics (test yourself, then reveal)
🎯 One-liner: Which enzymes are markers of hepatocellular damage? Which of biliary obstruction?
Hepatocellular damage = ALT & AST; Biliary obstruction = ALP & GGT.
🎯 One-liner: Best markers of chronic liver disease (CLD)?
PT (prothrombin time) and serum albumin / A:G ratio. PT is better than total protein.
🎯 One-liner: Why is PT more specific than total protein for liver disease?
PT is produced only by liver; total protein includes immunoglobulins made by plasma cells outside the liver.
🎯 One-liner: What is van den Bergh reaction used for?
To distinguish direct (conjugated) from indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin using diazotized sulfanilic acid.
🎯 One-liner: Hallmark of haemolytic jaundice in urine?
No bilirubin in urine (acholuric) + increased urobilinogen.
🎯 One-liner: Why does obstructive jaundice cause pruritus?
Bile salt accumulation in skin → mast cell degranulation → histamine release → itching.
🎯 One-liner: Why is stool pale in obstructive jaundice?
No bile in gut → no fat digestion → steatorrhoea + no stercobilinogen → pale, bulky, frothy stool.
🎯 One-liner: Kernicterus affects which brain structures? Which area is spared?
Affected: basal ganglia, hippocampal cortex, subthalamic nuclei, cerebellum. Spared: cerebral cortex.
🎯 One-liner: Difference between choluric and acholuric jaundice?
Choluric = bilirubin present in urine (hepatic + obstructive); Acholuric = no bilirubin in urine (haemolytic).
🎯 One-liner: ALT is found in which tissues? AST in which?
ALT = mainly liver (most liver-specific); AST = liver + heart + brain + kidney + muscle.
🎯 One-liner: Where is ALP found?
Liver (bile duct), bone, intestine, placenta.
🎯 One-liner: How to differentiate raised ALP due to cholestasis vs bone disease?
Cholestasis: both ALP & GGT raised. Bone disease: only ALP raised, GGT normal.
🎯 One-liner: GGT is a marker of which liver disease?
Alcoholic liver disease (most sensitive screening marker) + cholestasis.
🎯 One-liner: Normal serum bilirubin? At what level does clinical jaundice appear?
Normal = 0.3–1.0 mg/dL. Clinical jaundice = bilirubin >3 mg/dL.
🎯 One-liner: In physiological jaundice of newborn, what fraction of bilirubin is direct?
Direct fraction is <15% of the total (mostly unconjugated).
🎯 One-liner: Treatment for kernicterus once established?
Irreversible — only symptomatic management. Prevention via exchange transfusion if bilirubin toxicity signs appear.
🎯 One-liner: Haemolytic jaundice — what happens to stool stercobilinogen and urinary urobilinogen?
Stool stercobilinogen: increased (dark stool). Urinary urobilinogen: increased.
🎯 One-liner: Three cardinal features of obstructive jaundice?
(1) Conjugated bilirubin raised, (2) Generalised itching, (3) Pale stool + urinary bilirubin present.
