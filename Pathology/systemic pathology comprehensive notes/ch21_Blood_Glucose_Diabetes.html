🧠 Big Picture — Blood Glucose & Diabetes Mellitus (boss-level overview)

Physiology first: glucose is the body's energy currency. Its level is kept within a tight range by insulin (the only hormone that lowers blood glucose) and counter-regulatory hormones that raise it. Hyperglycaemia = blood glucose above normal; diabetes mellitus (DM) is a clinical syndrome of persistent hyperglycaemia from absolute or relative insulin deficiency.

2 · Diabetes Mellitus — Definition & Classification

🔴 What is diabetes mellitus? (DU-22M,17Ja,08Ju, BUP-22Nv/M,21Nv/M, CMU-22Nv,05Ja, RU-18Ja) Classify diabetes mellitus. (DU-22M,17Ja,14Ju, CMU-22Nv)

Diabetes mellitus (DM): a clinical syndrome characterised by an increase in plasma blood glucose (hyperglycaemia) due to absolute or relative deficiency of insulin. (Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed. p.722)

Or, diabetes mellitus is a group of metabolic disorders sharing the common feature of hyperglycaemia caused by defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or, most commonly, both. (Ref: Robbins 10e p.1097)

Classification of DM

A. Type 1 diabetes (T1D) — β-cell destruction, usually leading to absolute insulin deficiency:

  • Immune-mediated
  • Idiopathic (autoantibody-negative)

B. Type 2 diabetes (T2D) — combination of insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction.

C. Other specific types:

  1. Genetic defects of β-cell function: maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY), neonatal diabetes, maternally inherited diabetes and deafness (MIDD), defects in proinsulin conversion, insulin gene mutations.
  2. Genetic defects in insulin action: type A insulin resistance, lipoatrophic diabetes.
  3. Exocrine pancreatic defects ("pancreatogenic" or type 3C diabetes): pancreatitis, pancreatectomy/trauma, pancreatic cancer, cystic fibrosis, haemochromatosis, fibrocalculous pancreatopathy.
  4. Endocrinopathies: acromegaly, Cushing syndrome, hyperthyroidism, pheochromocytoma, glucagonoma.
  5. Infections: cytomegalovirus, Coxsackie B virus, congenital rubella.
  6. Drugs: glucocorticoids, thyroid hormone, interferon-α, protease inhibitors, β-adrenergic agonists, thiazides, nicotinic acid, phenytoin (dilantin), Vacor.
  7. Genetic syndromes associated with diabetes: Down syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome, Turner syndrome, Prader-Willi syndrome.

D. Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM).

Ref: Robbins 10e p.1099; Davidson's 23rd ed. p.733

Mention the risk factors of diabetes mellitus. (BUP-21Nv/M)

Key risk factors for developing diabetes mellitus:

  • Obesity (especially central/abdominal obesity)
  • Family history of diabetes (first-degree relatives)
  • Increasing age
  • Sedentary lifestyle / physical inactivity
  • Hypertension (>140/90 mmHg)
  • Dyslipidaemia (low HDL cholesterol ≤35 mg/dl, or high triglycerides ≥250 mg/dl)
  • History of gestational diabetes or delivery of a macrosomic baby
  • Previous impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) or impaired fasting glucose (IFG)
  • Ethnicity / genetic susceptibility
  • Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
🎯 "ROAD-FIT" — Risk factors: Relative (family history) · Obesity · Age · Dyslipidaemia · Family/ethnicity · Inactivity · This hypertension & prior GDM.

Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed.; ADA criteria (cross-referenced)

3 · Clinical Features & Diagnosis of DM

🔴 What are the clinical features of a diabetes patient? (DU-03Ja)

Common symptoms:

  • Polyuria (frequent micturition)
  • Polydipsia (increased thirst)
  • Polyphagia (increased appetite)
  • Tachycardia
  • Weakness
  • Weight loss

Other features (severe/presenting late):

  • Dehydration
  • Hypotension
  • Hypothermia
  • Confusion
  • Drowsiness
  • Hyperventilation
  • Muscle wasting
  • Acidosis & coma
🎯 Classic triad "3-P": Polyuria · Polydipsia · Polyphagia (+ weight loss) = the hallmark symptoms of diabetes.

Ref: Lippincott's 5th ed. p.337; Ganong 24th ed. p.449

🔴 How will you diagnose / confirm a case of diabetes mellitus in a pathology laboratory? (CMU-23/22Nv,18M, SU-17M,04Ja) How will you confirm a case of diabetes mellitus? (RU-17Ja) Write down the methods you will use in the diagnosis of diabetes mellitus. (RU-14/13/11Ja) Write down the laboratory diagnosis of diabetes mellitus. (CU-20Ju, RU-17Nv/Ju,16Ja) Give your approach to diagnose a case of diabetes mellitus. (RU-18Nv)

Diagnosis of DM: the term diagnosis refers to confirmation of diabetes in people who have symptoms, or who have had a positive screening test. The approach is two-part: (A) elicit symptoms, (B) laboratory investigations.

A. Symptoms suggesting diabetes

Common: polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia, tachycardia, weakness, weight loss. Others: dehydration, hypotension, hypothermia, confusion, drowsiness, hyperventilation, muscle wasting, acidosis & coma.

B. Laboratory investigations in DM

  1. Fasting, random and post-prandial blood glucose concentration — diagnosis is confirmed by:
    • Fasting blood glucose >7.0 mmol/L (≥126 mg/dl)
    • Random blood glucose >11.1 mmol/L (>200 mg/dl)
    • Post-prandial blood glucose (2 hrs after meal) >11.1 mmol/L (>200 mg/dl)
    In asymptomatic patients, two samples are required to confirm diabetes.
  2. Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT):
    • Fasting plasma glucose >7.0 mmol/L (>126 mg/dl)
    • 2 hours after 75 gm glucose load >11.1 mmol/L (>200 mg/dl)
  3. Urine testing for glucose, ketone bodies, and proteins
  4. Glycosylated Hb (HbA1c):
    • Normally 4–6% of total Hb is glycosylated
    • Diagnosis of diabetes is confirmed by HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol (>6.5%)
  5. Other investigations: full blood count, fasting lipid profile, blood urea and serum creatinine, serum electrolytes.

Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed. p.726

🎯 Nice to Know (definitions): Random means without regard to time since last meal · Fasting means no calorie intake for at least 8 hours (but not more than 16 hours). (Ref: Long Cases in Clinical Medicine 1st ed. p.610)
Write down the confirmatory biochemical tests for diabetes mellitus.

Confirmatory biochemical tests for DM:

  • Either fasting plasma glucose (FPG) or random plasma glucose (RPG) can be used for the confirmation of diagnosis.
  • In symptomatic individuals, one abnormal plasma glucose measurement in the diabetic range confirms the diagnosis.
  • Asymptomatic individuals with a positive screening test (FPG or RPG) need another abnormal plasma glucose measurement on another day for confirmation.
  • OGTT is not recommended for routine use as a confirmatory test.
📌 Definitions: Fasting = no caloric intake for at least 8 h. Random = any time of day without regard to time since last meal. (Ref: Manual of diabetes mellitus)

Nice to Know — Screening tests for DM

  • Screening is recommended for all individuals with one or more major risk factors for developing DM.
  • FPG (fasting plasma glucose) is the laboratory test recommended for screening.
  • RPG (random plasma glucose) is an alternative test for screening.
  • When facilities for FPG or RPG are not available, CBG (capillary blood glucose) and UG (urinary glucose) are alternative screening tests for patients with classical symptoms of DM.

Ref: Manual of diabetes mellitus

4 · Diagnostic Criteria for DM (ADA & WHO)

🔴 Write the diagnostic criteria for diabetes mellitus (as per ADA & WHO). (CMU-22M,21/20Nv) Give the WHO diagnostic criteria for diagnosis of diabetes mellitus. (DU-23Nv,21Ja,20Nv/M,19Nv,14/07Ju, BUP-22Nv,21Nv/M, CU-20M, RU-12Ja)

Diagnostic criteria for DM (ADA & WHO) — Very Important for VIVA:

  1. A single fasting plasma glucose >7.0 mmol/L (≥126 mg/dl) in symptomatic patients and on two separate occasions in asymptomatic patients.
  2. A random plasma glucose >11.1 mmol/L (≥200 mg/dl) in an individual with classic signs/symptoms of diabetes (polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia & weight loss) and on two separate occasions in asymptomatic patients.
  3. A 2-hour OGTT >11.1 mmol/L (>200 mg/dl) after a 75 gm load of glucose.
  4. An HbA1c of ≥48 mmol/mol (>6.5%) is also diagnostic of diabetes.
🎯 N.B: As HbA1c reflects the last 2–3 months of glycaemia, it should not be used to diagnose diabetes where the duration of onset is short, i.e. in someone with suspected type 1 diabetes or severe symptomatic hyperglycaemia. (Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed. p.727)

Ref: Lecture SSMC; Davidson's 23rd ed. p.727

Mention the criteria of the patients who should be considered for diabetic testing.

Criteria for patients who should be considered for diabetic testing:

  • All individuals 45 years of age or older (repeat at 3-year interval)
  • Persons with classic signs & symptoms of diabetes (polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia & weight loss)
  • Obesity
  • History of first-degree relations with diabetes
  • Women with gestational diabetes
  • Individuals with hypertension (>140/90 mmHg)
  • An HDL (high-density lipoprotein) cholesterol level ≤35 mg/dl or triglyceride (TG) level ≥250 mg/dl
  • Presence of impaired glucose tolerance on previous testing

Ref: Lecture SSMC

What are the metabolic goals of management of diabetes mellitus? (CU-11Ju)

Metabolic goals of management of DM:

  1. Target blood glucose level: fasting blood glucose <6 mmol/L
  2. Target HbA1c: <7%
  3. Target levels of blood lipids:
    • Total cholesterol: <5.0 mmol/L (approx. 190 mg/dl)
    • LDL (low-density lipoprotein) cholesterol: <3.0 mmol/L (approx. 115 mg/dl)

Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed.

5 · Hyperglycaemia

What is hyperglycaemia? (DU-12Ju) What are the causes of hyperglycaemia? (SUST-21/19Nv, RU-20Nv) Mention the important causes (any 4) of hyperglycaemia. (DU-05M, RU-17Ja, CU-18M,16/14Ja,11/10Ju,11/09Ja, SU-17M,16Ja,15/13Ja,09Ju) Mention the hyperglycaemic conditions.

Hyperglycaemia: a condition when blood sugar level is raised above normal.

Causes of hyperglycaemia:

  1. Diabetes mellitus
  2. Impaired glucose tolerance
  3. Pregnancy
  4. Pancreatic diseases — e.g. pancreatitis, haemochromatosis, pancreatectomy
  5. Liver disease
  6. Endocrine disorders — e.g. thyrotoxicosis, Cushing's syndrome, acromegaly, pheochromocytoma
  7. Drugs — e.g. corticosteroids, thiazide diuretics, phenytoin

Ref: Prof. Khaleque p.191

🔴 What is hyperglycaemia sine glycosuria? (DU-08Ju, RU-07Ja) Why is sugar absent in urine during hyperglycaemia sine glycosuria? (DU-22Nv,21Oct/M) Write about hyperglycaemia sine glycosuria. (RU-19M) Short note: Hyperglycaemia sine glycosuria. (DU-09Ju, CU-11Ju, RU-16Ja,14Ju,12Ja,10Ju)

Hyperglycaemia sine glycosuria: a condition in which the plasma glucose is above the normal renal threshold for glucose (180 mg/dl), but the urine is free from glucose.

Cause: this is due to a raised renal threshold above normal. This may occur in prolonged uncontrolled diabetes mellitus.

Ref: Prof. Khaleque p.191

🎯 "sine" = without. Hyperglycaemia sine glycosuria = high blood sugar without sugar in urine (raised renal threshold).
🔴 In which disease is blood glucose normal but there is glucose in urine? (Very Important for VIVA)

Renal glycosuria — the reverse of hyperglycaemia sine glycosuria.

Ref: Prof. Khaleque

🔴 A diabetic patient has blood glucose level more than 200 mg/dl, but he has no glycosuria. How can it be possible? (Very Important for VIVA)

Hyperglycaemia sine glycosuria — the plasma glucose is above the renal threshold, but the urine is free from glucose because the renal threshold is raised above normal (e.g., in prolonged uncontrolled diabetes mellitus).

Ref: Prof. Khaleque p.191

Enumerate the causes of hypoglycaemia. (RU-17/15Ja, CU-13Ju/Ja) Short note: Hypoglycaemia. (CU-16Ju)

Hypoglycaemia: the condition when blood glucose level is reduced below normal (<2.2 mmol/L).

Causes of hypoglycaemia (Very Important for VIVA):

  1. Diabetic patients taking anti-diabetic drugs with delayed, inadequate or missed meal, heavy exercise or alcohol
  2. Overdose of hypoglycaemic drugs such as insulin or sulphonylurea drugs
  3. Addison's disease
  4. Insulin-secreting tumour, e.g. insulinoma, retroperitoneal fibrosarcoma etc.
  5. Malnutrition
  6. Severe liver disease
  7. Malabsorption, e.g. coeliac disease
  8. Gastroparesis due to autonomic neuropathy
  9. Breast-feeding by diabetic mother
🎯 Remember AMI: A — Anti-diabetic drugs · M — Malabsorption · I — Insulinoma.

Symptoms of hypoglycaemia

  • 1. Neuroglycopenic: confusion, drowsiness, speech difficulty, inability to concentrate, irritability, incoordination
  • 2. Autonomic: sweating, trembling, hunger, anxiety
  • 3. Non-specific: nausea, headache, tiredness

Ref: Davidson's 21st ed. pp.812–813

6 · Glycosuria

🔴 What is glycosuria? (CH-?Ja) Enumerate the causes of glycosuria. (BSFMMUS-23Nv, RU-MM/Ju, JU,14,13,11,07Ja, CU-14Ju/07Ja, SU-11Ju)

Glycosuria: presence of glucose in urine which can be detected by conventional method (Benedict test). (Ref: Prof. Khaleque p.202)

Causes:

  1. Untreated diabetes mellitus (DM)
  2. Renal glycosuria: blood glucose values are normal but sugar is present in urine due to low renal threshold value for glucose. There is reduced rate of reabsorption of glucose by kidney tubules, as occurs in serious tubular damage or inherited defect in tubular absorption.
  3. Alimentary (lag storage) glycosuria: glycosuria developing after ingestion of a moderate amount of sugar or starch (which normally is dissipated without appearing in the urine) because the rate of intestinal absorption exceeds the capacity of the liver and other tissues to remove the glucose, thus allowing blood glucose levels to become high enough for renal excretion (above renal threshold, 10 mmol or 180 mg/dl). This may occur in:
    • Normal people
    • Liver disease
    • Hyperthyroidism
    • After gastric surgery (gastrectomy & vagotomy)
  4. Fanconi syndrome: glucose, amino acid, and phosphate are excreted due to impaired absorption
  5. Sometimes during pregnancy

Ref: Lecture SSMC; Prof. Khaleque p.192

What is renal glycosuria? (DU-24Nv,11Ja) Short note: Renal glycosuria. (BUP-22M, RU-14Ju, SU-12a) Why is sugar present in renal glycosuria? (DU-23Nv)

Renal glycosuria: blood glucose values are normal, but sugar is present in urine due to a low renal threshold value for glucose (reduced rate of reabsorption of glucose by kidney tubules — as occurs in serious tubular damage or inherited defect in tubular absorption). It is the reverse of hyperglycaemia sine glycosuria.

Raised renal threshold for glucose occurs in:

  • Heart failure
  • Shock

7 · Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT / GTT)

🔴 What do you mean by OGTT? (DU-22Nv,21Oct/M,11Ja, BUP-22Nv, SU-20M) Discuss the interpretation of OGTT. (DU-11Ja, BSFMMUS-23Nv, SUST-21Nv,20M,19Nv,15/13Ja,11Ju, RU-18M,16Ju, CU-14Ja,10Ju/Ja) How can you confirm OGTT? (DU-15Ja) Short note: GTT / OGTT. (DU-05S/Ja,04Ja, SU-22/19M,14Ju,06Ja,05M, RU-17M) Short note: OGTT and its interpretation. (DU-13Ju)

Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT / GTT): a laboratory test to diagnose diabetes mellitus. When random blood glucose values are elevated but are not diagnostic of diabetes, glucose tolerance is usually assessed by the glycaemic response to oral ingestion of a glucose load. It measures the ability of the body to tolerate, or cope with, a standard dose of glucose. Tolerance is reduced when insulin is insufficient or absent.

Indication of OGTT — patients with:

  • Fasting plasma glucose: 6.1–6.9 mmol/L (110–125 mg/dl)
  • Random plasma glucose: 7.8–11.0 mmol/L (140–199 mg/dl)

Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed. p.726

Preparation of the patient

  1. Unrestricted CHO (carbohydrate) diet (minimum 150 gm/day) for 3 days before test
  2. Avoidance of drugs affecting blood glucose level (e.g. corticosteroid, oral contraceptives etc.)
  3. Treatment of any infection (if present)
  4. Fasted overnight (for at least 8 hours) prior to the test
  5. Avoidance of smoking & exercise prior to the test
  6. Rest before test (for at least 30 minutes)
  7. The person should sit throughout the procedure
  8. No smoking is permitted during test interval
  9. Caffeinated beverages should not be consumed
  10. The person should not be bed-ridden or under stress

Procedure

  1. Fasting venous blood for plasma and corresponding urine sample is collected.
  2. 75 gm glucose dissolved in 250–300 ml of water is given by mouth within 5 minutes of fasting sample collection.
  3. Venous blood and urine samples are collected 2 hours after the 75 gm glucose load — or at half-hourly intervals for at least two hours.
  4. Plasma glucose on each blood sample and sugar on each urine sample is estimated.

Interpretation of OGTT curve

CategoryFasting2 hrs after 75 gm glucose load
Healthy3.6–6.1 mmol/L (65–110 mg/dl)<7.8 mmol/L (<140 mg/dl)
Diabetic>7.0 mmol/L (≥126 mg/dl)>11.1 mmol/L (≥200 mg/dl)
Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT)6.1–6.9 mmol/L (110–125 mg/dl)7.8–11.0 mmol/L (140–199 mg/dl)
📌 N.B: For asymptomatic patients, 2 abnormal values greater than 11.1 mmol/L are needed to establish the diagnosis of DM. (Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed. p.726)

Nice to Know

  • In OGTT, testing of urine for glucose in all the samples is the usual procedure for detecting DM, but it has no diagnostic value because DM may exist without glycosuria if blood glucose level does not exceed the renal threshold.
  • Sometimes there may be glycosuria in the absence of DM, e.g. renal glycosuria, alimentary glycosuria.
  • Glycosuria frequently coexists with DM, but for diagnosis of DM glycosuria is not mandatory.

Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed. p.726

8 · Pre-diabetes (IFG & IGT)

🔴 Short note: Pre diabetes. (Very Important for VIVA)

Pre-diabetes: the intermediary metabolic stage of glucose between normal glucose homeostasis and diabetes mellitus.

Components:

  1. IFG (impaired fasting glucose)
  2. IGT (impaired glucose tolerance)

Laboratory diagnosis (by OGTT, WHO criteria):

Fasting glucose (mmol/L)2 hrs after OGTT (mmol/L)Diagnosis
6.1 – 6.9<7.8IFG
6.1 – 6.97.8 – 11.0IGT

Consequence / importance:

  1. Pre-diabetics are at risk of developing diabetes mellitus (DM) later
  2. Pre-diabetics are at increased risk of vascular complications
  3. It is a useful cautionary clinical message to decide on preventive measures through dietary and lifestyle modification to avoid development of DM

Ref: Lecture SSMC; WHO/NICE criteria

🔴 What is IFG? (DU-15Ja,13Ju) Short note: Impaired fasting glucose (IFG). (Very Important for VIVA)

Impaired fasting glucose (IFG): refers to a condition in which the fasting blood glucose level is consistently elevated above what is considered normal levels; however, it is not high enough to be diagnosed as diabetes mellitus.

Or, IFG is defined as the fasting glucose level between 6.1 to 6.9 mmol/L (110 to 125 mg/dL) according to WHO.

Diagnostic criteria — WHO criteria for IFG

  • Fasting plasma glucose = 6.1 mmol/L (110 mg/dL) to 6.9 mmol/L (125 mg/dL)
  • 2 hrs after OGTT glucose is normal, i.e. <7.8 mmol/L (<140 mg/dl)

Importance

  1. These patients are prone to develop frank diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease.
  2. There is a 50% risk over 10 years of progressing to overt diabetes.
  3. IFG is a risk factor for mortality due to the serious complications of type-2 diabetes mellitus.

Advice to patients with IFG

Patients are advised for weight reduction of about 5–10% of their body weight, regular exercise and follow-up. Usually no drug therapy is recommended.

Ref: Lecture SSMC; Long Cases in Clinical Medicine 1st ed. p.610

🔴 What is IGT? (DU-15Ja,13Ju) Mention the clinical importance of IGT. (CU-11Ju) What will be the OGTT findings in IGT? (SU-14Ju) Write the diagnostic criteria for defining impaired glucose tolerance test. (CMU-21M) Short note: Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT). (DU-15Ju,12Ja) (Very Important for VIVA)

Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT): when a random or fasting blood glucose is suggestive but not diagnostic of DM — that is, when the values are above the upper limits of normal but below the values diagnostic of diabetes — it is called impaired glucose tolerance (IGT).

Or, when fasting glucose is less than 7 mmol/L, but during OGTT, 2 hours after the glucose load is 7.8 to 11.0 mmol/L, it is called IGT.

Diagnostic criteria — WHO criteria for IGT

ParameterValue
Fasting plasma glucose6.1–6.9 mmol/L (110–125 mg/dl)
2 hrs after OGTT7.8–11.0 mmol/L (140–199 mg/dl)

Clinical importance of IGT

The patient who has IGT is at increased risk of developing frank DM type 2 with time, and also macrovascular complications are more (mainly cardiovascular). Lifestyle modification for type 2 DM and annual check-up for glucose are recommended. Cardiovascular risk factors should be treated aggressively.

🔴 An obese male of 58 years complaining of loss of weight and mild generalized weakness. Laboratory investigations reveal fasting blood sugar 6.6 mmol/L and random level 10.0 mmol/L. Serum lipid level is also raised. What is the diagnosis? How will you investigate the case? (DU-11Ju)

Diagnosis: Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT).

Fasting blood sugar 6.6 mmol/L falls in the IGT range (6.1–6.9 mmol/L); random level 10.0 mmol/L falls in the IGT range (7.8–11.0 mmol/L) — suggestive but not diagnostic of DM.

Investigation: by OGTT (oral glucose tolerance test).

Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed.

🔴 An obese female aged 45 years with glucosuria, found during routine medical check-up. OGTT done and the results: Fasting 5.4 mmol/L (-), 1 hour 10.7 mmol/L (+), 2 hours 9.9 mmol/L (+). How will you interpret this OGTT result? What is the underlying cause for this? (DU-08Ja)

Interpretation of OGTT result: Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) with renal glycosuria.

  • Fasting 5.4 mmol/L → normal (<6.1)
  • 1 hour 10.7 mmol/L → high (transient, above renal threshold)
  • 2 hours 9.9 mmol/L → in IGT range (7.8–11.0 mmol/L) — suggestive but not diagnostic of DM
  • Urine sugar positive even though fasting blood glucose is normal → renal glycosuria (low renal threshold for glucose)

Underlying cause of renal glycosuria: in renal glycosuria, blood glucose values are normal but sugar is present in urine due to a low renal threshold value for glucose (reduced rate of reabsorption of glucose by kidney tubules — as occurs in serious tubular damage or inherited defect in tubular absorption).

Ref: Lecture SSMC; Prof. Khaleque p.192

  2. Colorimeter
  3. Spectrophotometer
  4. Automated biochemistry analyzer
  5. Test strips
  6. Chemical reduction method

9 · Glycosylated Haemoglobin (HbA1c)

🔴 What is the significance of HbA1c? (CU-18Nv) Short note: Glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c). (DU-21Ja,20Nv/M,16Ju, CMU-21Nv,20Ju,16Ja,13Ju,11/10/09Ja) (Must be Asked in VIVA)

Glycosylated haemoglobin (HbA1c): the most widely used measure of long-term glycaemic control in diabetes mellitus. It is produced by the glycosylation of haemoglobin at a rate proportional to the glucose concentration.

📌 N.B: Slow non-enzymatic covalent attachment of glucose to haemoglobin is called glycosylation of haemoglobin.

Normal value: 4.0 – 6.0% of total Hb (haemoglobin).

Importance:

  1. Glycosylated haemoglobin provides an accurate and objective measure of glycaemic control over a period of weeks to months.
  2. The measurement of HbA1c level in the blood is a useful adjunct in monitoring the management of DM.
  3. The rate of formation of HbA1c is directly proportional to the ambient blood glucose concentration.
  4. A rise of 1% in HbA1c corresponds to an approximate average increase of 2 mmol/L blood glucose.
  5. A large shift in blood glucose control is rapidly accompanied by a change in HbA1c, detectable within 2–3 weeks.
  6. It also provides an index of risk for developing diabetic complications.

Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed. p.726; Lecture SSMC; Prof. Khaleque p.193

10 · Complications of Diabetes Mellitus

🔴 What are the complications of diabetes mellitus? (DU-22M,21Ja,20Nv/M,19Nv,17/15Ja,13/12Ju, RMU-22Nv,18Ja,17Nv,11Ja,09Ju, BUP-22M, SU-20M, CU-19M) Mention the acute and chronic complications of diabetes mellitus. (CMU-22Nv) Short note: Complications of diabetes mellitus. (RU-15/14Ju)

Acute complications

  1. Diabetes ketoacidosis
  2. Hypoglycaemic coma
  3. Hyperglycaemic hyperosmolar state (HHS)
  4. Lactic acidosis

Chronic complications

A) Microvascular / neuropathic:

  1. Diabetic retinopathy — impaired vision
  2. Diabetic nephropathy — renal failure
  3. Diabetic neuropathy — sensory loss, motor weakness, pain
  4. Autonomic neuropathy — gastrointestinal problems (gastroparesis; altered bowel habit), postural hypotension
  5. Diabetic foot disease — ulceration, arthropathy

B) Macrovascular:

  1. Coronary circulation — myocardial ischaemia / infarction
  2. Cerebral circulation — transient ischaemic attack (TIA), stroke
  3. Peripheral circulation — claudication, ischaemia (e.g. gangrene of the lower extremities)

Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed. pp.734,756; Robbins 10e pp.1106–1107

What are the ketone bodies? (DU-11Ju,10Ja, RU-17/16Ja) Define ketonuria. How is it formed?

Ketone bodies / ketones: acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, and acetone collectively called ketone bodies. Excretion of more than a trace of these substances in the urine is called ketonuria.

Mechanism of formation of ketonuria: glucose provides energy to our body. If intake is insufficient or metabolism defective due to lack of insulin (as in untreated or uncontrolled diabetes), the body obtains energy by breaking down fats & producing ketones. Ketones are toxic to the brain and can cause coma (found in diabetic ketoacidosis). In ketoacidosis, ketonuria is always present. Urine ketone test detects acetoacetate and acetone only.

Ref: Lecture SSMC

11 · Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

🔴 How does ketoacidosis develop in cases of uncontrolled diabetes mellitus? (DU-24Nv,11Ju,10Ja,08Ju) Explain the mechanism of developing metabolic acidosis in diabetes mellitus. (DU-03M, RU-08Ju) Discuss in short about the pathogenesis of diabetic ketoacidosis. (DU-23Nv) Short note: Diabetic ketoacidosis. (DU-19M,18Ja) Short note on: Ketoacidosis. (DU-17Ja)

Ketoacidosis / diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA): over-production of ketone bodies in long-standing uncontrolled or poorly controlled diabetes mellitus exceeds the buffering capacity of blood. As a result, blood pH is decreased and acidosis is produced — this is called ketoacidosis.

Mechanism of development of ketoacidosis in uncontrolled DM

  1. Diabetes mellitus → ↓ plasma insulin level
  2. Loss of the inhibitory effect of insulin on lipolysis → ↑ lipolysis
  3. ↑ Free fatty acid in plasma → ↑ β-oxidation
  4. ↑ Acetyl CoA (coenzyme A)
  5. All the acetyl CoA cannot be utilised by the TCA (tricarboxylic acid / Krebs) cycle in the liver
  6. Excess acetyl CoA is converted to acetoacetic acid & 3-hydroxybutyric acids
  7. ↑ Ketone bodies in blood
  8. ↑ Excretion of ketone body (and glucose) in urine → dehydration
  9. ↑ Ketone bodies in decreased volume of plasma → ↑ plasma ketone body concentration
  10. → Diabetic ketoacidosis

Ref: Lippincott's 3rd ed. p.195

Cardinal biochemical features

  1. Hyperglycaemia
  2. Hyperketonaemia
  3. Metabolic acidosis

Clinical features

A. Symptoms:

  • Polyuria, thirst
  • Weight loss
  • Weakness
  • Nausea, vomiting
  • Leg cramps
  • Blurred vision
  • Abdominal pain

B. Signs:

  • Dehydration
  • Hypotension
  • Cold extremities / peripheral cyanosis
  • Tachycardia
  • Air hunger (Kussmaul breathing)
  • Smell of acetone
  • Hypothermia
  • Confusion, drowsiness, coma (10%)

Management

The principle components of treatment are:

  • The administration of short-acting (soluble) insulin
  • Fluid replacement
  • Potassium replacement
  • The administration of antibiotics if infection is present

Complications of diabetic ketoacidosis

  • Cerebral oedema
  • Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)
  • Thromboembolism
  • Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) (rare)
  • Acute circulatory failure

Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed. pp.735–736

🔴 A 48-year-old diabetic patient became unconscious following a chest infection. He did not take any anti-diabetic drugs. He also developed a musty odour in breath. What is the diagnosis? What may be the likely findings in urine analysis?

Diagnosis: Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

📌 Any form of stress, particularly that produced by infection, may precipitate severe ketoacidosis. (Ref: Davidson's 21st ed. p.809)

Likely findings in urine analysis:

  • pH of urine: ↓ (low / acidic)
  • Urinary glucose: ↑↑ (markedly increased)
  • Urinary ketone body: ↑↑ (markedly increased)

Ref: Davidson's 21st ed. p.809

🔴 A 26-year-old female was brought to the hospital with a recent history of polyuria, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain. Blood parameters on admission: glucose 19 mmol/L, serum electrolytes — Na 120 meq/L, Cl 85 meq/L, K 2.5 meq/L, HCO₃ 10 mmol/L. What is the likely diagnosis?

Diagnosis: Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

Glucose 19 mmol/L is markedly elevated (>11.1 mmol/L, diabetic range). The low HCO₃ (10 mmol/L) confirms metabolic acidosis. Polyuria, nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain are classic presenting symptoms of DKA.

Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed.

12 · Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM)

🔴 Define gestational diabetes mellitus. (DU-09Ja, RU-20Nv,18Ja) Short note: Gestational diabetes mellitus. (Important for VIVA)

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM): glucose metabolism changes during normal pregnancy. Gestational diabetes is defined as diabetes with first onset or recognition during pregnancy.

WHO suggests that all women meeting criteria for impaired glucose tolerance or diabetes after a 75 gm OGTT should be classified as having gestational diabetes.

Risk factors for gestational diabetes

  1. Obesity
  2. Family history of type 2 diabetes
  3. Previous glucose abnormality in pregnancy
  4. Previous macrosomia (large baby)

Complications of gestational diabetes

  • Gestational diabetes is associated with an increased risk of later development of type 2 diabetes.
  • Maternal hyperglycaemia is associated with increased fetal size (fetal macrosomia) & obstructed labour.

Diagnosis of GDM — European criteria

  • Venous plasma glucose in the fasting state: >5.5 mmol/L (99 mg/dl)
  • 2 hours after a glucose load: >9 mmol/L (162 mg/dl)

Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed. p.752

🔴 A 32-year-old pregnant woman case with high blood sugar level who had no diabetes mellitus before pregnancy. What is your diagnosis and how will you investigate & confirm the condition of the case? (RMU-24M)

Diagnosis: Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) — diabetes with first onset or recognition during pregnancy.

Investigations to confirm the case:

  • OGTT (oral glucose tolerance test) with 75 gm glucose — the diagnostic test. WHO suggests all women meeting criteria for impaired glucose tolerance or diabetes after a 75 gm OGTT should be classified as having gestational diabetes.
  • European diagnostic criteria:
    • Venous plasma glucose in the fasting state: >5.5 mmol/L (99 mg/dl)
    • 2 hours after a glucose load: >9 mmol/L (162 mg/dl)
  • Also: fasting blood glucose, random blood glucose, and urine testing for glucose / ketones as supportive tests.

Ref: Davidson's 23rd ed. p.752

Blood Glucose & Diabetes — One-Liners & Mnemonics (test yourself, then reveal)
🎯 One-liners: What is the renal threshold for glucose? What is the normal HbA1c?
Renal threshold for glucose ≈ 180 mg/dl (10 mmol/L). Normal HbA1c = 4–6% of total haemoglobin; DM diagnosed at HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol (>6.5%); target <7% in management.
🎯 One-liner: Normal fasting / random blood glucose (mmol/L and mg/dl)?
Fasting 3.6–6.1 mmol/L (65–110 mg/dl); random & 2-hr postprandial <7.8 mmol/L (<140 mg/dl). DM: fasting >7.0 (≥126 mg/dl), random/2-hr >11.1 (≥200 mg/dl).
🎯 One-liner: "High blood sugar, no sugar in urine" = ? And "normal blood sugar, sugar in urine" = ?
High sugar, no urinary sugar = Hyperglycaemia sine glycosuria (raised renal threshold). Normal blood sugar with urinary sugar = Renal glycosuria (low renal threshold).
🎯 One-liner: Classic "3-P" symptoms of diabetes?
Polyuria · Polydipsia · Polyphagia (plus weight loss). These classic symptoms + random glucose >11.1 mmol/L confirm diagnosis.
🎯 One-liner: Which insulin-secreting tumour causes hypoglycaemia?
Insulinoma (pancreatic β-cell tumour).
🎯 One-liner: A 1% rise in HbA1c corresponds to how much average blood glucose rise?
≈ 2 mmol/L average increase in blood glucose.
