🧠 Big Picture — Head, Neck & Oral Cavity (boss-level overview)

This chapter is organised in four spheres: (1) Neck swellings — midline vs unilateral vs cystic causes + how to investigate; (2) Oral cavity — diseases of teeth/gingiva, reactive lesions, infections, oral manifestations of systemic disease, precancerous & cancerous lesions, odontogenic cysts/tumours; (3) Salivary glands — xerostomia, sialadenitis, Sjögren syndrome & the big tumour classification (pleomorphic adenoma = most common benign; adenoid cystic carcinoma = classic malignant); (4) Nose & nasopharynx — tumours (angiofibroma, SCC-related nasopharyngeal carcinoma etc.).

The precancerous → cancer axis (VIVA gold): Leukoplakia (white patch, tobacco-related, lower malignant potential) → Erythroplakia (red, velvety, ~90% show severe dysplasia/carcinoma in-situ, HIGHER malignant risk) → SCC (squamous cell carcinoma).
📌 Neck swelling quintet: midline = thyroglossal cyst / goitre / sublingual dermoid / ranula / sebaceous cyst; unilateral = lymph node / thyroid / submandibular gland / cystic hygroma / branchial cyst / sternomastoid tumour.
🔴 Salivary tumour distribution rule: parotid = 65–80% of all (majority benign); malignancy rises as gland size falls — submandibular 40%, minor 50%, sublingual 70–90%.
🎯 Mnemonics (Banglish): "Neck-er Swelling — Ghaat-bangla koro!" Midline = Thyroglossal, Sublingual dermoid, Ranula, Subhyoid bursa, Ca of larynx/trachea/oesophagus, Thyroid, Sebaceous/SCC skin.

1 · Diseases of Head & Neck — Neck Swellings

🔴 What are the causes of midline neck swelling?

Causes of midline neck swelling:

  1. Submental lymph node
  2. Sublingual dermoid cyst
  3. Ranula (a mucous cyst/retention cyst of the sublingual salivary gland)
  4. Subhyoid bursa (subhyoid bursitis)
  5. Thyroglossal cyst
  6. Carcinoma of the larynx / trachea / oesophagus (Ca larynx/trachea/oesophagus)
  7. Thyroid swelling
  8. Goitre of the thyroid isthmus
  9. Lymph node in the suprasternal "space of Burns" (suprasternal space of Burns = the interval between the superficial and deep layers of the superficial cervical fascia)
  10. Any skin or subcutaneous swelling in the neck occupying the midline — e.g. sebaceous cyst, skin wart, skin lesion such as SCC (squamous cell carcinoma) or basal cell carcinoma
🎯 Midline neck swelling = "This area runs down the middle" → Thyroglossal, Sublingual dermoid, Ranula, Thyroid/goitre, Subhyoid bursa, Ca larynx/trachea/oesophagus, Sebaceous cyst/SCC/BCC skin.
🔴 Mention 5 causes of unilateral neck swelling.

5 (actually up to 7) causes of unilateral neck swelling:

  1. Enlarged lymph node
  2. Thyroid swelling
  3. Enlargement of the submandibular salivary gland
  4. Cystic hygroma (cavernous lymphangioma)
  5. Branchial cyst
  6. Sternomastoid tumour
  7. Cervical rib (a congenital bony outgrowth from the 7th cervical vertebra)

Name some cystic swellings in the neck.

Cystic swellings in the neck (8):

  1. Thyroglossal cyst
  2. Sublingual dermoid cyst
  3. Ranula
  4. Subhyoid bursa
  5. Branchial cyst
  6. Laryngocele (air-containing dilatation of the laryngeal saccule)
  7. Cystic hygroma

🎯 Cystic neck swellings = "T-R-S-S-B-L-C-P": Thyroglossal, Ranula, Sublingual dermoid, Subhyoid bursa, Branchial cyst, Laryngocele, Cystic hygroma, Pharyngeal pouch.

A 12-year-old child has a localized swelling in the cervical region of the neck. Give the possible causes and how you will investigate for diagnosis. (CU-16Ja,13Ju)

Possible causes of neck swelling (in a child)

  • Lymph node enlargement
  • Thyroid swelling
  • Sub-mandibular salivary gland tumour
  • Branchial cyst
  • Carotid artery aneurysm

Relevant investigations

  • Blood for TC (total count), DC (differential count), ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) & Hb% (haemoglobin percentage)
  • If lymph node enlargement → FNAC (fine-needle aspiration cytology) & biopsy of lymph node
  • If thyroid swelling → thyroid function test, USG (ultrasonography), FNAC & biopsy
  • If salivary gland tumour → FNAC & biopsy
  • If branchial cyst → Ultrasonogram
  • Colour Doppler study of carotid vessels
🎯 Lymph node → FNAC + biopsy; Thyroid → TFT + USG + FNAC; Salivary → FNAC + biopsy; Branchial cyst → USG; Carotid aneurysm → colour Doppler.

Arif 15e (2025) p.137

A female aged 20 years came to you with neck swelling that moves with deglutition (swallowing). What are the causes and how will you investigate the case? (RMU-21Nv,13Ju)

Causes of neck swelling that moves with deglutition

  • Goitre
  • Thyroglossal cyst
  • Sublingual dermoid
  • Thyroid malignancy

Investigations

  • Thyroid function tests (e.g. TSH, T3, T4)
  • Ultrasonogram (USG) of the thyroid including the neck
  • Thyroid scan (thyroid scintigraphy)
  • FNAC (fine-needle aspiration cytology) or biopsy from the swelling
  • Imaging — CT/MRI scan of the neck (computed tomography / magnetic resonance imaging)

2 · Diseases of the Oral Cavity

🔴 What are the diseases of oral cavity? What are the common infections of oral cavity?

Diseases of the oral cavity (full classification)

  1. Diseases of teeth and supporting structures:
    • Caries (tooth decay)
    • Gingivitis
    • Periodontitis
  2. Inflammatory / reactive lesions:
    • Aphthous ulcer (canker sores)
    • Fibrous proliferative lesions:
      • Irritation fibroma / traumatic fibroma / focal fibrous hyperplasia
      • Pyogenic granuloma
      • Peripheral ossifying fibroma
      • Peripheral giant cell granuloma
  3. Infections:
    • Herpes simplex virus (HSV) infections
    • Other viral infections: Herpes zoster (varicella-zoster virus), EBV (Epstein–Barr virus), cytomegalovirus (CMV), enterovirus, and rubeola (measles)
    • Oral candidiasis (thrush)
    • Deep fungal infections: Histoplasmosis, blastomycosis, coccidioidomycosis, cryptococcosis, zygomycosis, and aspergillosis
  4. Oral manifestations of systemic disease (e.g. Hairy leukoplakia)
  5. Precancerous and cancerous lesions: (For VIVA)
    • Leukoplakia
    • Erythroplakia
    • Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)
  6. Odontogenic cysts and tumors
🎯 6 categories = "Teeth · Reactive · Infection · Systemic · Precancer/Cancer · Odontogenic." Favourite short-list = Leukoplakia, Erythroplakia, SCC.

Robbins 10e pp.731–737

3 · Glossitis

What is glossitis? Where does it occur?

Glossitis implies inflammation of the tongue. It is sometimes applied to the beefy-red tongue encountered in certain deficiency states.

Causes (deficiency states that produce the beefy-red tongue)

  • Deficiency of vitamin B₁₂ (pernicious anaemia)
  • Deficiency of riboflavin, niacin, or pyridoxine (B-vitamin complex deficiencies)
  • Deficiency of iron (iron deficiency anaemia)
🎯 Glossitis triad = vitamin B₁₂ / B-complex (riboflavin·niacin·pyridoxine) / iron deficiency → "beefy-red tongue."

Robbins 8e p.742

4 · Aphthous Ulcer

🔴 What is aphthous ulcer? Describe its characteristics. (Important for VIVA)

Aphthous ulcer (canker sores): common, often recurrent & painful, superficial oral mucosal ulcerations of unknown aetiology.

Characteristics

  • Affect up to 40% of the population, most common in the first two decades of life.
  • Extremely painful and often recurrent.
  • May be associated with immunologic disorders including celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and Behçet disease.
  • Lesions appear as single or multiple, shallow, hyperaemic, mucosal ulcerations covered by a thin exudate and rimmed by a narrow zone of erythema.
  • Lesions typically resolve spontaneously in 7–10 days but sometimes persist for weeks, particularly in immunocompromised patients.
🎯 Aphthous = "patch-che": painful + recurrent + shallow ulcer with erythematous rim + 40% population + healed 7–10 days. Associates: Celiac · IBD · Behçet.

Robbins 10e p.732

6 · Leukoplakia

🔴 What is leukoplakia? State its common sites, pathology and clinical importance. Short note. (CU-19/18Nv, DU-03M, For VIVA)

Leukoplakia — defined by the WHO (World Health Organization) as "a white patch or plaque that cannot be scraped off and cannot be characterized clinically or pathologically as any other disease."

📌 Until proven otherwise by means of histologic evaluation, all leukoplakias must be considered precancerous.

Age at incidence

Adults at any age, but usually found in persons 40 to 70 years of age, with a 2:1 male preponderance.

Cause

Multifactorial origin, but most important cause is the use of tobacco (cigarettes, pipes, cigars and certain forms of smokeless tobacco).

Sites

May occur anywhere in the oral cavity, but the favoured sites are:

  • Buccal mucosa
  • Floor of the mouth
  • Ventral surface of the tongue, palate and gingiva

Morphology — Macroscopic (gross)

  • Solitary or multiple white patches or plaques.
  • Sharply demarcated borders.
  • May be slightly thickened and smooth or wrinkled and fissured, or they may appear as raised, sometimes corrugated, verrucous plaques.

Morphology — Microscopic (histology)

A spectrum of epithelial changes ranging from:

  • Hyperkeratosis overlying a thickened, acanthotic but orderly mucosal epithelium, to
  • Lesions with markedly dysplastic changes sometimes merging into carcinoma in situ.

Clinical importance

It is a precancerous state — so it may turn into malignancy {squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)}.

🎯 Leukoplakia = "Laal na, shada patch" (a WHITE patch, unscrapable, tobacco-related, 40–70 yrs, 2:1 M, buccal mucosa/floor of mouth/ventral tongue — all precancerous).

Robbins 10e p.734

7 · Erythroplakia

🔴 What is erythroplakia? What is its histology, cause and clinical importance? Short note. (CU-18Nv, For VIVA)

Erythroplakia is a red, velvety, possibly eroded area within the oral cavity that usually remains level with or may be slightly depressed in relation to the surrounding mucosa.

The epithelium in such lesions tends to be markedly atypical, incurring a much higher risk of malignant transformation (pre-malignant).

Age at incidence

Adults at any age, but usually found in persons 40 to 70 years of age, with a 2:1 male preponderance.

Cause

Multifactorial origin, but the most important cause is the use of tobacco (cigarettes, pipes, cigars and certain forms of smokeless tobacco).

Histology of erythroplakia

  • Severe dysplasia / carcinoma in situ / minimally invasive carcinoma (approx. 90% of cases)
  • An intense subepithelial inflammatory reaction with vascular dilatation accounts for the reddish clinical appearance of the lesion.

Clinical importance

It is a precancerous state. Risk of malignant transformation (SCC — squamous cell carcinoma) is much higher than in leukoplakia.

Nice to Know (NB)

Intermediate forms that have the characteristics of both leukoplakia and erythroplakia are termed speckled leukoerythroplakia.

🎯 Erythroplakia = "Erythro = red; danger = higher!" Red/velvety, 90% show severe dysplasia/CIS/invasive SCC → MORE dangerous than leukoplakia.

Robbins 10e pp.734–735

8 · Salivary Gland — Xerostomia, Sialadenitis & Sjögren Syndrome

Define xerostomia and sialadenitis. (For VIVA)

Xerostomia is defined as a dry mouth resulting from a decrease in the production of saliva.

Sialadenitis refers to the inflammation of the salivary gland, which may be of traumatic, viral, bacterial, or autoimmune origin.

Nice to Know (NB)

The most common viral cause of sialadenitis is mumps, which affects the major salivary glands (particularly the parotids) and frequently involves other glandular organs such as the pancreas and testes.

🎯 Xerostomia = dry mouth (↓ saliva production); Sialadenitis = inflamed salivary gland; mumps (paramyxovirus) → parotid + pancreas + testes.

Robbins 10e pp.747, 751

What is Sjögren syndrome? Describe primary and secondary forms and associated disorders.

Sjögren syndrome is a chronic disease characterized by dry eyes (keratoconjunctivitis sicca) and dry mouth (xerostomia) resulting from immunologically mediated destruction of the lacrimal and salivary glands.

Nice to Know (NB)

  • Occurs as an isolated disorder (primary form), also known as the sicca syndrome, or
  • More often in association with another autoimmune disease (secondary form).

Associated disorders: rheumatoid arthritis is the most common, but some patients have SLE (systemic lupus erythematosus), polymyositis, scleroderma, vasculitis, mixed connective tissue disease, or thyroiditis.

🎯 Sjögren = "dry-eyed + dry-mouth" autoimmune destruction of lacrimal + salivary glands; secondary form → RA most common.

Robbins 10e p.229

9 · Tumours of the Salivary Glands — Classification

🔴 Classify salivary gland tumours/neoplasms. Enumerate the malignant neoplasms. Short note. (RMU-24/23/22M, CMU-23Nv, RU-19/18M, CU-19M/18Nv, For VIVA)

The majority of salivary gland tumours occur in the parotid gland (65%–80%). The majority of parotid tumours are benign, but 40% of submandibular, 50% of minor salivary gland, and 70%–90% of sublingual tumours are malignant.

Benign tumoursMalignant tumours
  • Pleomorphic adenoma (mixed tumour) (50%)
  • Warthin tumour
  • Oncocytoma
  • Canalicular adenoma
  • Basal cell adenoma
  • Other adenomas
  • Ductal papillomas
  • Mucoepidermoid carcinoma
  • Acinic cell carcinoma
  • Adenoid cystic carcinoma
  • Adenocarcinoma, not otherwise specified (NOS)
  • Carcinoma ex pleomorphic adenoma
  • Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)
  • Other carcinomas
🎯 Distribution rule: 65–80% in parotid (mostly BENIGN); as gland gets smaller, malignancy ↑ — submandibular 40%, minor 50%, sublingual 70–90%.

Robbins 10e p.748; BRS Pathology 5e p.226

10 · Pleomorphic Adenoma (Mixed Tumour)

🔴 What is pleomorphic adenoma? Give its characteristics, clinical features and morphology (gross + histologic). Short note. (RMU-23/22/21M, CMU-23M/19Nv, RU-18/13/10, CU-09, DU-04Ja)

Pleomorphic adenoma (mixed tumour): a benign, slow-growing neoplasm composed of a heterogenous mixture of epithelial and mesenchymal cells.

Pleomorphic adenomas are the most common salivary gland neoplasms. They represent about 60% of tumours in the parotid, are less common in the submandibular glands, and are relatively rare in the minor salivary glands.

Cause

Little is known, but radiation exposure increases the risk.

Characteristics

  • Benign tumours consisting of a mixture of ductal (epithelial), myoepithelial, and mesenchymal cells — which explains why they are also termed "mixed tumours".
  • Malignant transformation is rare. When they appear, these cancers are among the most aggressive of all salivary gland neoplasms, with mortality rates of 30%–50% at 5 years.
  • Such cancers are usually adenocarcinomas or undifferentiated carcinomas.

Clinical features

  1. Painless, slow growing, mobile, discrete masses within the parotid or submandibular areas or in the buccal cavity.
  2. Often, the tumour is difficult to remove completely because of its proximity to the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII), and it is likely to recur after resection.
  3. The rate of recurrence following parotidectomy is about 4%.

Morphology — Macroscopic (gross)

  • Rounded, well demarcated masses that rarely exceed 6 cm in greatest dimension.
  • Encapsulated with some exceptions — e.g. in the palate, where the capsule is not fully developed.
  • Cut surface is gray-white with myxoid and blue translucent areas of chondroid (cartilage-like) stroma.

Morphology — Microscopic / Histologic

  • Great heterogeneity.
  • The epithelial elements resembling ductal cells or myoepithelial cells are arranged as ducts, acini, irregular tubules, strands, or sheets of cells.
  • These elements are typically dispersed within a background of loose myxoid and hyaline tissue containing islands of cartilage and, rarely, foci of bone.
🎯 Pleomorphic adenoma = benign, most common salivary tumour, parotid 60%, painless mobile mass near facial nerve, 4% recurrence after parotidectomy; gross 6 cm grey-white with myxoid/chondroid areas; histo = ductal + myoepithelial + mesenchymal (cartilage/bone islands).

Robbins 10e pp.748, 751; BRS Pathology 5e p.226

🔴 Why is it called pleomorphic adenoma? (RU-18M)

Pleomorphism is a term used in histology and cytopathology to describe variability in the size, shape and staining of cells and/or their nuclei. An adenoma is a benign tumour originating in glandular tissue.

Pleomorphic adenoma consists of a mixture of ductal (epithelial), myoepithelial and mesenchymal cells, and therefore it shows both epithelial and mesenchymal (myxoid & chondroid/cartilage-like elements) differentiation. Because of this remarkable histologic diversity, this adenoma is called "Pleomorphic adenoma".

🎯 Pleomorphic = variable size/shape/staining of cells & nuclei; adenoma = benign glandular tumour. Mixed epithelial + myoepithelial + mesenchymal differentiation → hence "pleomorphic".

Robbins 10e p.748; BRS Pathology 5e p.226; Lecture DMC

11 · Adenoid Cystic Carcinoma

🔴 Give the morphologic features of adenoid cystic carcinoma. (RU-03Ja)

Adenoid cystic carcinoma: a relatively uncommon tumour, which in approximately 50% of cases is found in the minor salivary glands (in particular the palatine glands). Among the major salivary glands, the parotid and submandibular glands are the most common locations.

Gross appearance

  1. Generally small, poorly encapsulated, infiltrative, gray-pink lesions.

Microscopic

  1. Composed of small cells having dark, compact nuclei and scant cytoplasm.
  2. Turnour cells are organized in a cribriform growth pattern that resembles Swiss cheese.
  3. The spaces between the tumour cells are often filled with hyaline material thought to represent excess basement membrane.
  4. Less common histologic patterns are tubular and solid.
🎯 Adenoid cystic carcinoma = minor salivary (50%, palatine) > parotid/submandibular; small dark cells; "Swiss-cheese" cribriform pattern; hyaline = excess basement membrane; patterns: cribriform, tubular, solid.

Robbins 10e p.751

12 · Tumours of the Nose, Sinuses & Nasopharynx

Name the tumours of nose, sinuses & nasopharynx.

Tumours of the nose, sinuses & nasopharynx:

  1. Nasopharyngeal angiofibroma (juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma — a vascular tumour of adolescent males)
  2. Sinonasal (Schneiderian) papilloma
  3. Olfactory neuroblastoma (Esthesioneuroblastoma)
  4. NUT midline carcinoma (Nuclear protein in testis / NUT carcinoma — driven by NUTM1 gene rearrangements)
  5. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (associated with EBV — Epstein–Barr virus infection)
🎯 Naso-tumour list = Angiofibroma · Schneiderian papilloma · Olfactory (esthesio) neuroblastoma · NUT midline carcinoma · Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (EBV+).

Robbins 10e p.741
