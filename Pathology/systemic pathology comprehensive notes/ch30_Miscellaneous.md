## 1 · VDRL Test (Syphilis Screening)
<b>🔴 Write a short note on the VDRL (Venereal Disease Research Laboratory) test. What are its advantages and disadvantages?</b>

<b>VDRL (Venereal Disease Research Laboratory) test:</b> the most commonly used simple and rapid screening test for <b>syphilis</b>, caused by the spirochaete <i>Treponema pallidum</i>.

It is non-specific (non-treponemal) but a useful screening test.
Becomes positive within 3–4 weeks of primary infection (after the primary chancre appears).
Generally becomes negative by 6 months after successful treatment — hence used to monitor treatment response.

Advantages

Simple and easy to perform (flocculation test on a slide).
Inexpensive — suitable for resource-limited settings.
Suitable as a screening test: positive in approximately 70% of primary syphilis and 98% of secondary syphilis.

Disadvantages

Non-specific — false-positive results occur in: malaria, measles, infectious mononucleosis (Epstein–Barr virus), leprosy, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), rheumatic disorders, pregnancy, advanced age, and other chronic infections.
May become false-negative in late latent syphilis (prozone phenomenon — very high antibody titres that saturate the antigen).
The VDRL antigen must be prepared fresh daily (commercially available antigen is a cardiolipin–lecithin–cholesterol mixture that loses potency rapidly).

## 2 · Causes of Hypoalbuminaemia
<b>🔴 Mention and classify the causes of hypoalbuminaemia.</b>

<b>Hypoalbuminaemia:</b> serum albumin concentration below the normal range (normal ≈ 3.5–5.0 g/dL). Albumin is synthesised exclusively by the liver and accounts for ~60% of total serum protein — its level reflects the balance between <b>synthesis, intake, absorption, and loss</b>.

Classification of causes (four-pillar framework)

<table>
<tr><th>Mechanism</th><th>Examples</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>1. Decreased protein intake</strong></td><td>Protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) — kwashiorkor (protein-deficient diet), marasmus (total calorie deficiency), prolonged fasting, anorexia nervosa</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>2. Decreased protein absorption</strong></td><td>Protein-losing enteropathy (PLE) — intestinal lymphangiectasia, coeliac disease, Crohn disease, Whipple disease; malabsorption syndromes</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>3. Decreased albumin synthesis</strong></td><td>Chronic liver disease (CLD) &amp; cirrhosis of the liver (the most common cause globally); acute liver failure; hepatocellular carcinoma; protein-calorie malnutrition impairs hepatic synthetic function</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>4. Increased protein loss</strong></td><td><strong>Renal:</strong> nephrotic syndrome (massive proteinuria &gt;3.5 g/day), acute glomerulonephritis (AGN)<br/><strong>GI:</strong> protein-losing enteropathy, burns (protein loss through denuded skin), protein-losing dermatoses, severe exudative conditions</td></tr>
</table>

Clinical significance of hypoalbuminaemia

Decreased oncotic pressure → oedema (peripheral, ascites, pleural effusion).
Altered drug binding — increased free fraction of highly protein-bound drugs (warfarin, phenytoin) → enhanced pharmacological effect and toxicity.
Decreased synthesis of clotting factors (some are albumin-associated) → prolonged prothrombin time (PT).
A reliable marker of chronic liver disease severity — the albumin level is part of the Child–Pugh and MELD (Model for End-Stage Liver Disease) scoring systems.

🎯 <b>"4 I's of hypoalbuminaemia":</b> Intake ↓ · Intestinal absorption ↓ · (hepatic) Insufficient synthesis ↑ · Increased loss (renal/GI/skin).

Arif 15e (2025) Vol-2, p.499 · Robbins 10e, Ch 18 (Liver, pp. 835–838)

## 3 · Triple Antigen Test (Widal, Weil-Felix, Brucella Agglutination)
<b>🔴 By which triple antigen test, which organisms can be detected? Describe each component.</b>

The <b>Triple Antigen test</b> is a multiplex serological screening assay used to simultaneously detect agglutinating antibodies against three major febrile-illness pathogens. It is performed by mixing patient serum with a cocktail of three standardised antigens on a single slide or test card.

<table>
<tr><th>Antigen / Test</th><th>Organism Detected</th><th>Key Details</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Widal test</strong></td><td><em>Salmonella</em> typhi &amp; paratyphi (enteric fever)</td><td>Detects O (somatic) and H (flagellar) agglutinating antibodies. O antigen = current/recent infection; H antigen = past infection or immunisation. A four-fold rise in titre between acute and convalescent sera = diagnostic. Cross-reactions with other Salmonella species, malaria, and chronic liver disease may cause false-positives.</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Weil-Felix test</strong></td><td><em>Rickettsia</em> species (spotted fever group &amp; typhus group)</td><td>Based on cross-reactivity between rickettsial antigens and <em>Proteus</em> OX-19, OX-2, OX-K antigens. OX-19 / OX-2 = epidemic / endemic typhus (R. prowazekii / R. typhi); OX-K = scrub typhus (R. tsutsugamuchi). Low sensitivity — largely historical but still asked in exams.</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Brucella agglutination test</strong></td><td><em>Brucella</em> species (brucellosis / undulant fever)</td><td>Standard tube agglutination test (STAT) or Rose Bengal test. Titre ≥1:160 significant in endemic areas. Brucella melitensis, B. abortus, B. suis are the main human pathogens. False-positives: cholera vaccination (cross-reacts with H antigen of Brucella).</td></tr>
</table>

Advantages of the Triple Antigen approach

Single serum sample screens for three common causes of fever in one step.
Rapid, inexpensive, and suitable for primary-care / field-laboratory settings.
Useful when the clinical differential is broad (enteric fever vs rickettsial fever vs brucellosis).

Limitations

Non-treponemal and non-specific nature leads to cross-reactions (Widal may cross-react with other Salmonellae, malaria, TB; Weil-Felix has low sensitivity and specificity).
Titre interpretation requires clinical context and serial (acute + convalescent) sampling for Widal.
Brucella agglutination test may be falsely positive after cholera vaccination.

🎯 <b>Triple Antigen = "W-W-B":</b> <b>W</b>idal (Salmonella) · <b>W</b>eil-Felix (Rickettsia) · <b>B</b>rucella (Brucella).

Arif 15e (2025) Vol-2, p.499 · Ananthanarayan & Paniker's Textbook of Microbiology, 10th Ed

## 🔁 Rapid-Fire MCQ & Viva Sweep (incl. folded General Clinical Pathology MCQs from ch31)
<b>Miscellaneous — True/False & one-liners (test yourself, then reveal)</b>

Following are correctly matched: Ketonuria — old diabetes mellitus (T/F)? Increased lymphocytes — pyogenic meningitis (T/F)? Increased PT — overdose of anticoagulant (T/F)? Pseudocyesis — positive pregnancy test (T/F)? Albumin-globulin ratio altered — Crohn's disease (T/F)?

Ketonuria in old DM = <b>True</b> · increased lymphocytes in pyogenic meningitis = <b>False</b> (pyogenic = neutrophils, not lymphocytes) · increased PT in anticoagulant overdose = <b>True</b> · pseudocyesis (false pregnancy) = <b>True</b> (urinary hCG can be positive in pseudocyesis) · A:G ratio altered in Crohn's = <b>False</b>.

Which of the following values are within normal limits: FBS 5.5 mmol/L (T/F)? Serum creatinine 3.4 mg/dL (T/F)? Blood urea 120 mg/dL (T/F)? Triglycerides 180 mg/dL (T/F)? Serum PSA 0.4 U/L (T/F)?

FBS 5.5 mmol/L = <b>True</b> (normal fasting glucose <5.6 mmol/L) · S. creatinine 3.4 mg/dL = <b>False</b> (normal 0.6–1.2 mg/dL; 3.4 = renal failure) · Blood urea 120 mg/dL = <b>False</b> (normal 15–40 mg/dL) · Triglycerides 180 mg/dL = <b>False</b> (normal <150 mg/dL) · PSA 0.4 U/L = <b>True</b> (normal <4.0 ng/mL).

VDRL test becomes positive within how many weeks of primary syphilis? When does it generally become negative after treatment?

Positive within <b>3–4 weeks</b> of primary infection. Becomes negative by <b>6 months after treatment</b>.

VDRL test is positive in approximately what percentage of primary and secondary syphilis?

<b>~70% of primary</b> and <b>~98% of secondary</b> syphilis.

Give 5 diseases that cause false-positive VDRL results.

Malaria, measles, infectious mononucleosis, leprosy, SLE, rheumatic disorders, pregnancy.

In the Triple Antigen test, which organism is detected by the Widal component?

<i>Salmonella</i> typhi & paratyphi (enteric fever).

Weil-Felix test detects antibodies against which group of organisms?

<i>Rickettsia</i> species — uses cross-reactive <i>Proteus</i> OX-19, OX-2, OX-K antigens.

The 4-pillar classification of hypoalbuminaemia causes: name the four mechanisms.

<b>(1)</b> Decreased intake (PEM) · <b>(2)</b> Decreased absorption (protein-losing enteropathy) · <b>(3)</b> Decreased synthesis (chronic liver disease / cirrhosis) · <b>(4)</b> Increased loss (nephrotic syndrome, AGN, burns).

🎯 One-liner: Triple Antigen = W-W-B. What does each letter stand for?

<b>W</b>idal (Salmonella typhi) · <b>W</b>eil-Felix (Rickettsia) · <b>B</b>rucella (Brucella species).

🎯 One-liner: Why must VDRL antigen be prepared fresh daily?

The cardiolipin–lecithin–cholesterol antigen mixture <b>loses potency rapidly</b> at room temperature and cannot be stored.

🎯 One-liner: VDRL is used to monitor treatment response in syphilis because…

Titres generally <b>fall and become negative within 6 months</b> of successful treatment — a persistently positive VDRL suggests treatment failure or reinfection.

🎯 One-liner: Most common cause of hypoalbuminaemia globally?

<b>Chronic liver disease / cirrhosis</b> — the liver is the sole site of albumin synthesis.

🎯 One-liner: What is the prozone phenomenon in VDRL?

In very high antibody titres (secondary syphilis), <b>excess antibody saturates all antigen sites</b>, preventing visible flocculation → false-negative result. Diluting the serum (quantitative VDRL) resolves this.
