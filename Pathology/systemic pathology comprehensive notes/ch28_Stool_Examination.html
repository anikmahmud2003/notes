🧠 Big Picture — Stool Examination (boss-level overview)

Stool (faecal) examination is a cornerstone of clinical pathology for evaluating gastrointestinal (GI) diseases. It follows a five-part workflow:

1. Physical examination → amount, consistency (semisolid), odour, colour (brown; pale in obstructive jaundice), presence of mucus and/or blood.

2. Chemical examination → occult blood test (OBT) to detect hidden GI bleeding; faecal fat test to assess malabsorption/pancreatic insufficiency; reducing substances to screen for carbohydrate malabsorption; bilirubin (present in haemolytic jaundice).

3. Microscopic examination → trophozoites and cysts of protozoa (e.g., Entamoeba histolytica); larvae and ova of helminths (worms); epithelial cells, pus cells (inflammatory lesions), red blood cells (RBCs), macrophages (bacillary dysentery), and muscle fibres (improper digestion).

4. Culture of the specimen → identification of pathogenic bacteria.

5. Culture sensitivity test → guides appropriate antibiotic therapy.

Clinically, stool examination answers five critical questions: (a) Is there parasitic infestation? (b) What type of dysentery? (c) What type of jaundice? (d) Is there fat malabsorption (pancreatic disease/obstructive jaundice)? (e) Is there occult GI bleeding?

1 · Laboratory Examinations Done with Stool

What are the laboratory examinations done with stool? Describe each briefly.

Laboratory examinations done with stool are divided into five categories:

1. Physical Examination

  • Amount — estimated grossly.
  • Consistency — normally semisolid.
  • Odour — characteristic; may change in malabsorption or infection.
  • Colour — normally brown; pale/clay-coloured in obstructive jaundice (absence of stercobilinogen).
  • Mucus — presence suggests inflammation (e.g., dysentery, irritable bowel disease).
  • Blood-stained — visible (fresh) or occult (hidden) blood.

2. Chemical Examination

  • Occult blood test (OBT) — detects trace/altered blood not visible to the naked eye.
  • Test for faecal fat — assesses fat malabsorption (steatorrhoea); elevated in cystic fibrosis of pancreas and obstructive jaundice.
  • Reducing substances — screens for unabsorbed sugars (e.g., lactose intolerance).
  • Bilirubin — normally absent from adult stool; present in haemolytic jaundice and diarrhoeal conditions.

3. Microscopic Examination

  • Trophozoite of protozoa — e.g., Entamoeba histolytica (motile, invasive form).
  • Cyst of protozoa — dormant/transmissible form of intestinal protozoa.
  • Larvae of helminths (parasitic worms).
  • Ova (eggs) of helminths — diagnostic for specific worm infestations.
  • Other cellular elements:
ElementSignificance
Epithelial cellsGI mucosal irritation/damage
Pus cells (white blood cells/WBCs)Inflammatory/infectious lesions (e.g., bacillary dysentery, ulcerative colitis)
Red blood cells (RBCs)Lower GI bleeding (haematochezia)
MacrophagesBacillary dysentery (pathognomonic finding)
Muscle fibres (undigested)Improper digestion / exocrine pancreatic insufficiency
Vegetable matter, hair, starch cells, fat globulesDietary; excessive fat globules → steatorrhoea
Air bubblesNormal finding

4. Culture of the Specimen

Grows pathogenic bacteria (e.g., Salmonella, Shigella, Vibrio cholerae) to identify the causative organism.

5. Culture Sensitivity Test

Determines which antibiotics the identified pathogen is susceptible to → guides targeted therapy and helps prevent antimicrobial resistance.

🎯 Microscopy mnemonic: "POMM" — Pus cells (inflammation), Ova (worms), Macrophages (bacillary dysentery), Muscle fibres (malabsorption).

2 · Indications of Routine Stool Examination

What are the indications of routine examination of stool?

Routine stool examination is indicated in the following clinical scenarios:

  1. Parasitic infestation — detection of ova, cysts, larvae, and trophozoites of different parasites (e.g., Ascaris lumbricoides, Entamoeba histolytica, hookworms).
  2. Type of dysentery — to differentiate bacillary dysentery (pus cells + macrophages + no parasites) from amoebic dysentery (trophozoites/cysts of E. histolytica).
  3. Type of jaundice — pale/clay-coloured stool → obstructive jaundice; normal or dark stool → haemolytic or hepatocellular jaundice; bilirubin present → haemolytic jaundice.
  4. Fat in stool (steatorrhoea) — to assess cystic fibrosis of pancreas (pancreatic insufficiency) and obstructive jaundice (bile salt deficiency).
  5. Occult blood test (OBT) — to detect any (upper or lower) gastrointestinal tract (GIT) bleeding that is not visible to the naked eye.
🎯 5 Indications mnemonic: "P-D-J-F-O" — Parasites, Dysentery, Jaundice, Fat, Occult blood.

3 · Occult Blood Test (OBT)

🔴 Write in short about the occult blood test (OBT). Describe its procedure, interpretation, and clinical significance. (DU-06S, BUP-22Nv, RU-19/18/17Nv, 16/14Ju, 13/12Ja, SU-17M, 11Ja, 09Ju, CU-16Ju, 07Ja)

Definition: The occult blood test (OBT) is done to detect altered blood or trace amounts of blood in stool that are not visible to the naked eye. It is one of the most frequently examined investigations in clinical pathology.

Methods

Two commonly used methods:

  1. Orthotoluidine test (benzidine-based peroxidase reaction)
  2. Hema-Check slide test (rapid immunochromatographic card test)

A. Orthotoluidine Test — Detailed Procedure

  1. Make an emulsion of the stool (a pea-sized amount) in distilled water in a test tube.
  2. Boil for 5 minutes and cool. (This destroys bacterial peroxidases that could give a false positive.)
  3. In a clean test tube, place 4 drops of orthotoluidine reagent and 1 drop of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂); leave for 1 minute.
  4. Add 1 drop of the stool suspension to the mixture.

Interpretation

A green colour developing within 1 minute = positive result → indicates the presence of occult (hidden) blood in the stool.

The reaction is based on the peroxidase-like activity of haemoglobin, which catalyses the oxidation of orthotoluidine by H₂O₂ to produce a green colour.

Precautions / Sources of Error

  • False positive may occur due to the presence of meat (red meat) or green vegetable products in the stool sample (both contain haemoglobin or plant peroxidases).
  • Therefore, the individual must not take meat or green vegetables for 3 days prior to the test to make the result significant.
  • Boiling the stool emulsion destroys bacterial peroxidases (reduces false positives from gut flora).

B. Hema-Check Slide Test

A rapid, immunochromatographic test paper (slide) is used. It detects human haemoglobin specifically (more specific than orthotoluidine). The slide is dipped in stool suspension and results are read within minutes.

Conditions Where Occult Blood Test May Be Positive

#Condition
1Bleeding peptic ulcer disease (PUD) — due to Helicobacter pylori infection or NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs)
2Carcinoma (Ca.) of stomach
3Carcinoma (Ca.) of colon (colorectal cancer screening)
4Ulcerative colitis — chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)

Additional causes (from clinical context): oesophageal varices, gastric erosions (NSAIDs/alcohol), angiodysplasia (vascular malformations), Meckel's diverticulum, colorectal polyps, and other GI malignancies.

Clinical significance: OBT is used as a screening test for colorectal cancer (especially in individuals >50 years), for evaluating iron-deficiency anaemia of unknown origin, and for monitoring GI bleeding.

🎯 OBT Positive mnemonic: "PUC-C" — Peptic ulcer, Ulcerative colitis, Ca. stomach, Ca. colon.

4 · Common Ova Found in Stool

What are the common ova found in stool?

The four most common ova (eggs) of helminths (parasitic worms) found in human stool:

#ParasiteCommon Name / Notes
1Ascaris lumbricoidesRoundworm — largest intestinal nematode; transmitted via ingestion of embryonated eggs from contaminated food/water. May cause intestinal obstruction, biliary/pancreatic duct obstruction, Löffler syndrome (pulmonary eosinophilia).
2Ankylostoma duodenaleHookworm (Old World) — attachment to duodenal/intestinal mucosa → chronic blood loss → iron-deficiency anaemia; larvae penetrate skin (e.g., bare feet).
3Trichuris trichiuraWhipworm — anterior end embeds in colonic mucosa → mild to moderate colitis; heavy infections may cause rectal prolapse in children.
4Enterobius vermicularisPinworm/threadworm — most common helminth in temperate climates; perianal itching (especially at night due to gravid female migration); diagnosed by the Scotch tape (cellophane tape) test rather than routine stool O&P (ova & parasite).
🎯 Ova mnemonic: "ATEE" — Ascaris · Trichuris · Enterobius · Encylostoma (hookworm).

5 · Melaena — Definition & Causes

🔴 What is melaena? What are the causes of melaena? (Ref: Davidson's 23rd/781)

Definition: Melaena is the passage of black, tarry, foul-smelling stools containing altered (digested) blood, usually caused by bleeding from the upper gastrointestinal tract (GIT) — typically proximal to the ligament of Treitz (i.e., oesophagus, stomach, duodenum).

Why black and tarry? When blood passes through the upper GIT, haemoglobin is degraded by gastric acid and bacterial action → converted to acid haematin (a black pigment) → gives the characteristic tarry appearance and characteristic smell.

Causes of Bleeding from Upper GIT (Melaena)

#CauseKey Details
1Peptic ulcer disease (PUD)The most common cause; risk factors: Helicobacter pylori infection, NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs)
2Gastric erosionsCaused by NSAIDs, alcohol, stress (Curling ulcer in burns; Cushing ulcer in CNS injury)
3Oesophageal varicesDue to liver diseases (cirrhosis) with portal hypertension ± portal vein thrombosis → dilated submucosal oesophageal veins rupture
4Cancer of stomach and oesophagusGastric carcinoma (most common in males); oesophageal carcinoma
5Oesophagitis with hiatus herniaAcid reflux → oesophageal mucosal inflammation/ulceration
6Vascular malformationsAngiodysplasia (common in elderly); hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (Osler-Weber-Rendu syndrome)
7Mallory-Weiss tearLongitudinal mucosal tear at the gastro-oesophageal junction following forceful vomiting/retching

6 · Fresh Blood in Stool (Haematochezia)

🔴 Write four causes of fresh blood in stool (haematochezia). (Ref: Davidson's 23rd/782)

Definition: Haematochezia = the passage of fresh (bright red) blood per rectum, typically indicating bleeding from the lower gastrointestinal tract (GIT) (distal to the ligament of Treitz — i.e., small intestine distal portion, colon, rectum, anal canal).

Causes of Fresh Blood in Stool (Haematochezia)

#CauseKey Details
1DysenteryAmoebic (trophozoites of E. histolytica) or bacillary (Shigella spp.) dysentery → bloody diarrhoea with mucus
2Bleeding piles (haemorrhoids)Dilated rectal/anal venous plexuses → painless bright red bleeding, often during defecation
3Malignant diseaseCa. rectum, Ca. colon — a change in bowel habit with rectal bleeding in a patient >40 yr = colorectal cancer until proven otherwise
4Ulcerative colitisChronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) — bloody diarrhoea, tenesmus, abdominal pain
5Crohn's diseaseTransmural granulomatous inflammation — less commonly bloody stool than ulcerative colitis, but can cause fistulae, strictures
6Anorectal fissure and haemorrhoidsFissure = painful linear tear of the anal mucosa → bright red bleeding on defecation
7Meckel's diverticulumCongenital remnant of the vitellointestinal (omphalomesenteric) duct; may contain ectopic gastric mucosa → acid secretion → ulceration of adjacent ileal mucosa → painless rectal bleeding (especially in children); follows the "Rule of 2s": 2 feet from ileocaecal valve, 2 inches long, 2% of population, symptomatic before age 2.

Melaena vs Haematochezia — Key Distinction

FeatureMelaenaHaematochezia
AppearanceBlack, tarry, foul-smellingBright red / fresh blood
SourceUpper GIT (proximal to ligament of Treitz)Lower GIT (distal to ligament of Treitz)
MechanismBlood degraded by acid + bacteria → acid haematinBlood passes rapidly → not altered
🎯 Haematochezia mnemonic: "D-D-M-U-C-F-M" — Dysentery, Diverticula (Meckel's), Malignancy, Ulcerative colitis, Crohn's, Fissure/Haemorrhoids, Meckel's.

🔁 Rapid-Fire MCQ & Viva Sweep (incl. Folded Clinical Pathology MCQs)

Stool Examination — True/False & One-Liners (test yourself, then reveal)
Colour of normal stool is brown. (T/F)
True — due to stercobilinogen (urobilinogen derivative of bilirubin).
Stool is pale/clay-coloured in haemolytic jaundice. (T/F)
False — pale/clay-coloured stool occurs in obstructive jaundice (absence of bile salts reaching the gut → no stercobilinogen). In haemolytic jaundice, stool is characteristically dark due to excess stercobilinogen.
Bilirubin is present in stool of a patient with obstructive jaundice. (T/F)
False — bilirubin is present in stool in haemolytic jaundice (excess unconjugated bilirubin reaches gut). In obstructive jaundice, bilirubin cannot reach the gut → absent from stool.
Macrophages in stool are seen in amoebic dysentery. (T/F)
False — macrophages in stool are characteristically seen in bacillary dysentery. In amoebic dysentery, one finds E. histolytica trophozoites.
Undigested muscle fibres in stool suggest pancreatic insufficiency. (T/F)
True — failure of proteolytic enzyme secretion → improper digestion of protein (meat) fibres.
The orthotoluidine test for occult blood uses H₂O₂. (T/F)
True — orthotoluidine + H₂O₂ is oxidised by the peroxidase-like activity of haemoglobin → green colour (positive).
Eating green vegetables before an orthotoluidine test may cause a false positive. (T/F)
True — plant peroxidases in green vegetables catalyse the same reaction → false positive. Patient should avoid meat and green vegetables for 3 days.
Boiling the stool emulsion in the orthotoluidine test is done to destroy bacterial peroxidases. (T/F)
True — eliminates false positives from bacterial enzymes present in normal gut flora.
Hema-Check slide test uses immunochromatography to detect human haemoglobin specifically. (T/F)
True — it is more specific than the orthotoluidine test (detects human-specific haemoglobin, not plant or animal peroxidases).
🎯 One-liner: A positive occult blood test with a green colour within 1 minute in the orthotoluidine test indicates?
Presence of occult (hidden) blood in stool — peroxidase-like activity of haemoglobin oxidises orthotoluidine in the presence of H₂O₂.
🎯 One-liner: Give 4 causes of melaena (black tarry stool).
(1) Peptic ulcer disease, (2) Gastric erosions (NSAIDs), (3) Oesophageal varices (liver cirrhosis), (4) Carcinoma of stomach.
🎯 One-liner: Give 4 causes of fresh blood in stool (haematochezia).
(1) Dysentery, (2) Bleeding piles (haemorrhoids), (3) Malignancy (Ca. rectum/colon), (4) Ulcerative colitis / Meckel's diverticulum.
🎯 One-liner: Common ova found in stool — name four parasites.
Ascaris lumbricoides (roundworm), Ankylostoma duodenale (hookworm), Trichuris trichiura (whipworm), Enterobius vermicularis (pinworm).
🎯 One-liner: What is the most common cause of melaena?
Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) — caused by H. pylori infection or NSAID use.
🎯 One-liner: What does the presence of pus cells and macrophages in stool suggest?
Bacillary dysentery (shigellosis) — an invasive bacterial infection of the colon causing inflammatory/dysenteric stool.
Melaena indicates bleeding from the upper GIT. (T/F)
True — blood is black/tarry because haemoglobin is degraded by gastric acid and bacteria into acid haematin.
Haematochezia indicates bleeding from the upper GIT. (T/F)
False — haematochezia (fresh bright red blood) indicates bleeding from the lower GIT.
Meckel's diverticulum follows the rule of 2s. (T/F)
True — 2 feet from ileocaecal valve, 2 inches long, 2% of population, symptomatic before age 2.
🎯 One-liner: Most common site of carcinoma of stomach causing occult blood in stool?
Pyloric antrum — the most common site for gastric carcinoma overall.
Fatty stool (steatorrhoea) in obstructive jaundice is due to bile salt deficiency. (T/F)
True — bile salts are required for emulsification and absorption of dietary fats; obstruction prevents bile salts from reaching the intestine → fat malabsorption → pale, bulky, greasy/frothy stool.
Folded Clinical Pathology MCQs — Stool Examination (from Ch 31)
Q. Occult bleed test is positive in: (DU-18Ja)
a) Obstructive jaundice — FALSE · b) Bleeding peptic ulcer disease (PUD) — TRUE · c) Carcinoma (Ca.) of stomach — TRUE · d) Ca. gall bladder — FALSE · e) Ulcerative colitis — TRUE.
Note: PUD, Ca. stomach, and ulcerative colitis are the classic causes of a positive occult blood test. Obstructive jaundice and Ca. gall bladder do not typically cause occult GI bleeding.
Q. Features of obstructive jaundice include: (DU-18M)
a) Unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia — FALSE · b) Pale, bulky and frothy stool — TRUE (steatorrhoea from bile salt deficiency) · c) Increased prothrombin time (PT) — TRUE (decreased vitamin K absorption → impaired clotting factor synthesis) · d) Increased alkaline phosphatase (ALP) — TRUE (cholestatic marker) · e) Increased urinary urobilinogen — FALSE (urobilinogen is absent from urine in obstructive jaundice because bilirubin cannot reach the gut to be converted).
