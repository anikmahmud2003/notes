# Ch 06 — The Gastrointestinal System     <i>Full-detail flashcards (Q→A) · All abbreviations expanded · Robbins 4-pillar answers</i>
### 🧠 Big Picture — The Gastrointestinal System (boss-level overview)
The GI tract runs mouth → anus; each segment has a signature disease set. 🔴 <b>Oesophagus:</b> motility disorders (achalasia), reflux oesophagitis (gastro-oesophageal reflux disease = GERD), Barrett oesophagus (intestinal metaplasia), varices (portal hypertension), squamous cell carcinoma (upper 2/3) & adenocarcinoma (lower 1/3).
🔴 <b>Stomach:</b> gastritis (acute & chronic — <i>Helicobacter pylori</i> vs autoimmune), peptic ulcer disease (PUD), gastric adenocarcinoma (intestinal & diffuse types), gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST — <code>c-kit</code> / CD117).
🔴 <b>Small & large intestine:</b> inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) = Crohn disease (transmural, skip lesions, non-caseating granuloma) vs ulcerative colitis (mucosal, continuous, crypt abscesses); congenital anomalies (Meckel diverticulum, Hirschsprung disease); malabsorption (coeliac disease, tropical sprue, Whipple disease); colorectal carcinoma (adenocarcinoma — 2nd most common cancer cause of death); carcinoid tumours & carcinoid syndrome; polyps (adenomatous → adenoma–carcinoma sequence); acute appendicitis.
📌 <b>Key integrative links:</b> <i>H. pylori</i> → chronic gastritis → PUD → gastric carcinoma / mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma (MALToma). Chronic IBD (especially ulcerative colitis) → increased risk of colorectal carcinoma. Portal hypertension → oesophageal varices. Adenoma–carcinoma sequence → most colorectal cancers.
Robbins 10e, Ch 17 (GI Tract), pp. 753–819 · Arif 15e (2025) Vol-2, pp.144–191

## 1 · Oesophagus — Diseases Overview
<b>Name the pathological conditions / diseases of the oesophagus. What are the congenital anomalies?</b>

<b>Classification of oesophageal diseases:</b>

Congenital anomalies (VIVA favourite): agenesis (congenital absence), stenosis (fibrous thickening → narrowed lumen), atresia (segment non-canalised), tracheo-oesophageal fistula, oesophageal mucosal webs, oesophageal rings (Schatzki rings).
Motor dysfunction: achalasia, diverticula, hiatal hernia.
Oesophagitis: Mallory-Weiss tears (lacerations), chemical/infectious oesophagitis (alcohol, corrosive acids/alkalis), reflux oesophagitis / gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD), eosinophilic oesophagitis (atopic).
Oesophageal varices (important cause of oesophageal bleeding — portal hypertension).
Barrett oesophagus (complication of chronic GERD; intestinal metaplasia → increased risk of adenocarcinoma).
Tumours:
Benign: squamous cell papilloma, condyloma, leiomyoma, lipoma, fibroma, neurofibroma, rhabdomyoma, haemangioma, lymphangioma.
Malignant (VIVA): squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), adenocarcinoma, undifferentiated carcinoma, carcinoid tumour, melanoma, lymphoma, sarcoma.

## 1a · Achalasia
<b>🔴 Short note: Achalasia. (CMU-23M)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Disordered oesophageal motility with inability to relax the lower oesophageal sphincter (LES), leading to progressive dysphagia and dilation of the oesophagus (mega-oesophagus).

<b>Triad of achalasia:</b>

Incomplete LES relaxation
Increased LES tone
Aperistalsis of the oesophagus

Types & causes

Primary achalasia (rare): degeneration of nitric oxide (NO)-producing neurons that normally induce LES relaxation. Degenerative changes in the extraoesophageal vagus nerve or dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus may also occur.
Secondary achalasia: may arise in Chagas disease (Trypanosoma cruzi infection → destruction of myenteric plexus → failure of peristalsis → oesophageal dilation).

Clinical features

Dysphagia for solids and liquids
Putrid breath
High LES pressure on oesophageal manometry
"Bird-beak" sign on barium swallow study
Increased risk of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma

Treatment

Laparoscopic myotomy; pneumatic balloon dilatation; botulinum neurotoxin (Botox) injection (inhibits cholinergic neurons that promote contraction).

Robbins 10e p.757 · BRS Pathology 5e p.228 · Pathoma 2014 p.102

## 1b · Reflux Oesophagitis / GERD
<b>🔴 Short note: Reflux oesophagitis / GERD (gastro-oesophageal reflux disease). (CU-19M)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Reflux of gastric acid into the lower oesophagus due to reduced lower oesophageal sphincter (LES) tone. <i>(N.B: It is the opposite of achalasia, where LES tone is increased.)</i>

Causes / risk factors (conditions that decrease LES tone or increase abdominal pressure)

Transient LES relaxation (vagal pathways)
Abrupt increases in intra-abdominal pressure (coughing, straining, bending)
Alcohol & tobacco use, obesity, fat-rich diet, caffeine
CNS depressants, pregnancy, hiatal hernia
Delayed gastric emptying, increased gastric volume

Clinical features

Heartburn (retrosternal burning — may mimic cardiac chest pain)
Dysphagia
Regurgitation of sour-tasting gastric contents (postprandial)
Adult-onset asthma and cough
Dental enamel damage

Treatment

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs).

Complications

Oesophagitis
Oesophageal ulceration with stricture
Barrett oesophagus (columnar metaplasia of oesophageal squamous epithelium)

Morphology

Mild GERD: simple hyperaemia (redness).
More significant reflux: erosions, influx of eosinophils into squamous mucosa, basal zone hyperplasia, elongation of lamina propria papillae. Neutrophil infiltration less frequent (usually in setting of bacterial/fungal infection or chemical damage).

Robbins 10e p.759 · BRS Pathology 5e p.228

<b>Short note: Hiatal hernia.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Herniation or protrusion of part of the stomach through the oesophageal hiatus of the diaphragm. May cause LES incompetence; symptoms similar to GERD but symptomatic in <10% of adults.

Anatomical types (3 patterns)

Sliding / oesophago-gastric hernia (85%): herniated stomach appears as supra-diaphragmatic bell, sliding up on both sides of the oesophagus.
Rolling / para-oesophageal hernia (10%): true hernia — cardiac end of stomach rolls up para-oesophageally, producing an intrathoracic sac.
Mixed / transitional hernia (5%): combination of sliding and rolling.

Features & complications

Heartburn (retrosternal burning), regurgitation of gastric juice — worsened by heavy work, lifting, bending.
Complications: ulceration, bleeding, perforation, reflux oesophagitis (more with sliding type).

Robbins 10e p.760 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.521

## 1c · Oesophageal Varices
<b>🔴 What are oesophageal varices? Mention its mechanism. (CMU-23M)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Dilated, tortuous veins lying primarily within the mucosa & submucosa of the distal oesophagus and proximal stomach, occurring secondary to <b>portal hypertension</b>. Develops in 90% of cirrhotic patients (most commonly in alcoholic liver disease).

Clinical features

Mostly asymptomatic, but risk of rupture exists. Variceal rupture → haemorrhage into lumen or oesophageal wall → painless <b>haematemesis</b>. <b>Most common cause of death in cirrhosis.</b>

Mechanism

Liver cirrhosis / fibrosis → obstruction of portal blood flow → portal hypertension → porto-systemic shunting → anastomotic channels connecting portal and systemic venous systems open and become distended → oesophageal varices.

Robbins 10e p.760 · BRS Pathology 5e p.228

## 1d · Barrett Oesophagus
<b>🔴 Short note: Barrett oesophagus. (CU-18Nv, DU-06S/Ja, CMU-21Nv,20M)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Long-standing complication of chronic GERD characterised by <b>intestinal metaplasia</b> within the oesophageal squamous mucosa — associated with increased risk of cancer. It is the <b>single most important risk factor for oesophageal adenocarcinoma</b>.

<b>Incidence:</b> occurs in up to 10% of individuals with symptomatic GERD; peak age 40–60 years.

Pathogenesis

Distal squamous epithelium replaced by metaplastic columnar epithelium as response to prolonged injury. Sequential evolution: Barrett epithelium (columnar metaplasia with goblet cells) → dysplasia → carcinoma in situ → oesophageal adenocarcinoma.

Morphology

Gross: red, velvety mucosa between smooth pale-pink squamous mucosa and light-brown gastric mucosa; may exist as tongues or patches (islands).
Microscopic: replacement of squamous epithelium by metaplastic columnar cells with goblet cells (definitive diagnosis). Intestinal metaplasia may be accompanied by dysplastic changes (low → high grade). Inflammatory changes (acute or chronic) commonly accompany.

Diagnostic criteria (2 required)

Endoscopic evidence of metaplastic columnar mucosa above the gastro-oesophageal junction.
Histologic evidence of intestinal-type metaplasia showing goblet cells replacing squamous epithelium.

Treatment

Surgical resection / oesophagectomy; photodynamic therapy, laser ablation, endoscopic mucosectomy; regular follow-up with endoscopy and biopsy.

Complications

Local ulceration with bleeding and stricture; oesophageal adenocarcinoma.

Robbins 10e p.761 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.523

## 1e · Oesophageal Carcinoma
<b>🔴 Mention the risk factors of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Write the etiopathogenesis.</b>

SCC occurs in adults >45 years; males affected 4× more than females.

Risk factors

Dietary: deficiency of vitamins (A, C, B₂, thiamin, B₆), trace elements (Zn, Mo); deficiency of fruits/vegetables; high nitrites/nitrosamines; betel chewing.
Lifestyle: poverty, frequent consumption of very hot beverages, fungus-contaminated foods, alcohol consumption, tobacco use.
Oesophageal disorders: caustic oesophageal injury, achalasia, tylosis, hiatal hernia, diverticula, Plummer-Vinson syndrome.
Genetic predisposition: long-standing coeliac disease, ectodermal dysplasia, epidermolysis bullosa, racial disposition (more common in African Americans).
Previous radiation to the mediastinum (5–10+ years after exposure).

Robbins 10e pp.763–764

<b>Mention the sites of oesophageal cancer.</b>

Squamous cell carcinoma: arises most frequently in the upper and middle thirds of the oesophagus.
Adenocarcinoma: arises most frequently in the lower third of the oesophagus.

Robbins 10e pp.762–763 · BRS Pathology 5e p.229

<b>Write the morphologic patterns of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma.</b>

Three morphologic patterns:

Protruded (60%): polypoid / exophytic lesion protruding into and obstructing the lumen.
Flat (15%): ulcerated or diffusely infiltrative lesion within the wall → thickening, rigidity, luminal narrowing.
Excavated (25%): tumour may invade surrounding structures including the respiratory tree (→ pneumonia), aorta (→ catastrophic exsanguination), mediastinum and pericardium.

Robbins 10e p.764

## 2 · Stomach — Diseases Overview
<b>Classify diseases of the stomach. Name the common malignant neoplasms of the stomach.</b>

Congenital anomalies: ectopia, diaphragmatic hernia, congenital hypertrophic pyloric stenosis.
Gastropathy & acute gastritis.
Stress-related mucosal disease: stress ulcers, Curling ulcers (burns/trauma), Cushing ulcers (intracranial disease).
Chronic gastritis & complications: Helicobacter pylori gastritis, autoimmune atrophic gastritis, uncommon forms (eosinophilic, lymphocytic, granulomatous), peptic ulcer disease (PUD), mucosal atrophy & intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, gastritis cystica.
Hypertrophic gastropathies: Ménétrier disease, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome.
Acquired pyloric stenosis.
Polyps & tumours:
Benign: inflammatory/hyperplastic polyps, fundic gland polyps, adenoma, leiomyoma, neurofibroma, lipoma, tumours of enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells, granular cell tumours, GIST.
Malignant: gastric carcinoma (adenocarcinoma >90%), carcinoid (neuroendocrine) tumours, lymphoma, leiomyosarcoma, schwannoma.

🎯 <b>Who classification of gastric tumours:</b> Epithelial (adenoma → adenocarcinoma), mesenchymal (GIST, leiomyoma/sarcoma), neuroendocrine (carcinoid, ECL-cell), lymphoid (MALToma).

Robbins 10e pp.753, 764–779 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.529

🎯 <b>Congenital hypertrophic pyloric stenosis:</b> congenital hypertrophy of pyloric smooth muscle (more common in males). Presents 3–6 weeks of life: projectile non-bilious vomiting, visible peristalsis (left-to-right), olive-like abdominal mass. Diagnosed by ultrasonography; treated by surgical myotomy. <i>(Ref: Robbins 10e p.755)</i>
## 2a · Gastritis (Acute & Chronic)
<b>🔴 Define gastritis. Mention its types and causes. Write the pathogenesis and clinical features.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Inflammation of the gastric mucosa.

1. Acute gastritis

Acidic damage to the stomach mucosa due to imbalance between mucosal defences and acidic environment. Acid damage → superficial inflammation, mucosal erosion, or ulcer.

<b>Causes/risk factors:</b> heavy NSAID use (especially aspirin), excessive alcohol, ingestion of harsh chemicals (acids/bases), heavy smoking, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, stress-related mucosal diseases (stress ulcers, Curling ulcers, Cushing ulcers), systemic bacterial/viral infections (salmonellosis, CMV), mechanical trauma (nasogastric intubation), distal gastrectomy, decreased oxygen delivery at high altitude.

<b>Clinical features:</b> may be asymptomatic; epigastric pain, nausea, vomiting; mucosal erosion, ulceration, haemorrhage, haematemesis, melaena; rarely massive blood loss.

2. Chronic gastritis

Characterised by chronic mucosal inflammation and atrophy of the mucosal glands. Symptoms less severe but more persistent than acute gastritis.

<b>Causes:</b> <i>H. pylori</i> infection, autoimmune gastritis, radiation injury, chronic bile reflux, mechanical injury (nasogastric tube), systemic diseases (Crohn disease, amyloidosis, graft-versus-host disease [GVHD]).

A. <i>H. pylori</i> gastritis (most common — 90%)

Location: antrum (most commonly infected site). Clinical feature: epigastric abdominal pain.

<b>Sequelae:</b> PUD; gastric adenocarcinoma (intestinal type); MALT lymphoma (MALToma).

<b>Treatment:</b> triple therapy (antibiotics + proton pump inhibitors).

B. Autoimmune atrophic gastritis

Autoimmune destruction of gastric parietal cells (body & fundus). Antibodies to parietal cells / intrinsic factor → vitamin B₁₂ deficiency → defective acid secretion (achlorhydria). Median age at diagnosis 60 (slightly more women).

<b>Clinical features (related to B₁₂ deficiency):</b> pernicious anaemia (megaloblastic anaemia), atrophic glossitis (smooth beefy-red tongue), epithelial megaloblastosis, malabsorptive diarrhoea, CNS features (subacute combined degeneration of spinal cord). Often associated with autoimmune diseases (Hashimoto thyroiditis, type 1 diabetes mellitus, Addison disease).

<b>Sequelae:</b> intestinal metaplasia → increased risk of gastric adenocarcinoma (intestinal type).

## 2b · Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)
<b>🔴 Define peptic ulcer disease (PUD). Enumerate the common sites. (DU-19/17Nv, RU-21/20/19/18Nv)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Chronic mucosal ulceration affecting the duodenum or stomach, almost always associated with <i>H. pylori</i> infection, NSAIDs, or cigarette smoking. Occur most commonly (98–99%) in duodenum or stomach in ratio 4:1.

Common sites (VIVA)

First part of duodenum
Stomach (usually antrum)
Gastro-oesophageal junction
Within margins of gastro-jejunostomy (marginal ulcer)
Throughout the GIT in Zollinger-Ellison syndrome
Within or adjacent to an ileal Meckel diverticulum containing ectopic gastric mucosa

Robbins 10e p.770 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.535

<b>🔴 What are the causes / risk factors of peptic ulcer disease? (SMU-23/22M, CU-13Ju)</b>

<i>H. pylori</i> infection (~90% duodenal ulcer patients, ~70% gastric ulcer patients)
Cigarette smoking
NSAIDs (potentiated by corticosteroids)
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
Illicit drugs (e.g. cocaine — reduces mucosal blood flow)
Alcoholic cirrhosis (primarily duodenal PUD)
Psychological stress (increases gastric acid secretion)
Endocrine cell hyperplasia (stimulates parietal cell growth & acid secretion)
Zollinger-Ellison syndrome (PUD of stomach, duodenum and jejunum)
Viral infection (CMV, herpes simplex virus)

Robbins 10e p.770 · Davidson's 23e p.798

<b>🔴 Write down the defense mechanisms of gastro-duodenal mucosa / mucosal barrier. (RMU-21M)</b>

Surface mucous secretion
Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) secretion into mucus
Increased mucosal blood flow (washes out H⁺)
Intercellular tight junctions (epithelial barrier to back-diffusion of H⁺)
Apical surface membrane transport
Quick regenerative capacity of lining epithelium
Elaboration of prostaglandin (↑mucus secretion, blood flow and epithelial regeneration)

Robbins 10e p.765

<b>🔴 Describe the pathogenesis of peptic ulcer disease. (DU-22Nv, CMU-23Nv,22M,19M)</b>

PUD results from imbalances between <b>mucosal defence mechanisms</b> (protective factors) and <b>damaging forces</b>. PUD generally develops on a background of chronic gastritis.

1. Impairment of defence mechanism (Very Important for VIVA)

Mucus secretion ↓
Bicarbonate secretion in mucus ↓
Mucosal blood flow ↓
Apical surface membrane transport ↓
Epithelial regenerative capacity ↓
Prostaglandin elaboration ↓

2. Increased damaging forces

Gastric hyperacidity
Peptic enzymes (pepsin)
Helicobacter pylori infection
NSAIDs, aspirin
Cigarettes, alcohol
Duodenogastric reflux

Robbins 10e pp.765, 770

<b>🔴 What is the role of <i>H. pylori</i> in the pathogenesis of peptic ulcer? (CU-12Ja, SU-13Ju)</b>

Although <i>H. pylori</i> does not invade tissues, it causes ulcer through:

Induces intense inflammatory & immune response → liberates proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α).
Secretes urease enzyme → breaks down urea → toxic compounds (ammonium chloride, monochloramine).
Bacterial proteases & phospholipases → breakdown of glycoprotein-lipid complexes in gastric mucus → weakens first-line mucosal defence.
Enhances gastric acid secretion & impairs duodenal bicarbonate production.
Thrombotic occlusion of surface capillaries (via bacterial platelet-activating factor).
Bacterial antigens (including lipopolysaccharide) → recruit inflammatory cells to mucosa.

<i>H. pylori</i> infection most often presents as predominantly antral gastritis with normal or increased acid production.

Robbins 10e p.767

<b>🔴 Diseases associated with <i>H. pylori</i> infection. (SU-20Nv, SMU-23/22M)</b>

Peptic ulcer disease (PUD)
Chronic gastritis
Gastric carcinoma
Gastric lymphoma / MALToma

Robbins 10e p.767

<b>🔴 Write the morphology (gross + microscopic) of peptic ulcer. (DU-13/11Ja, SU-13/10Ja)</b>

Gross / macroscopic

Location: Gastric ulcer: predominantly along lesser curvature in pyloric antrum, more commonly posterior than anterior wall. Duodenal ulcer: mostly first part of duodenum, more commonly anterior than posterior wall.
Shape: round to oval, sharply punched-out defect.
Margin: usually level with surrounding mucosa or only slightly elevated; mucosal margin may overhang base slightly.
Depth: superficial (confined to mucosa) to deep (penetrating into muscularis propria).
Base: smooth and clean (peptic digestion of exudates); blood vessels may be evident.

Microscopic — 4 histological zones (from within outward)

Superficial thin layer of necrotic fibrinoid debris.
Zone of non-specific inflammatory infiltrate (neutrophils predominating).
Zone of active granulation tissue infiltrated with mononuclear leucocytes.
Base of scar tissue.

<i>N.B: Malignant gastric ulcers are larger, bowl-shaped with elevated and indurated mucosa at the margin.</i>

Robbins 10e p.770 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.535

<b>🔴 Mention the clinical features of peptic ulcer disease.</b>

Peptic ulcers are remitting and relapsing lesions.

Periodic epigastric pain — burning or aching: Gastric ulcer: pain immediately or within 1–3 hours after food during the day; never occurs at night. Duodenal ulcer: pain severe, occurs late night (11 pm – 2 am, "hunger pain"); relieved by alkali or food.
Nausea, vomiting, bloating, belching, heartburn (retrosternal pain) and "water brash" (burning fluid into the mouth).
Anorexia and early satiety.
Weight loss in gastric ulcer patients; weight gain in duodenal ulcer patients.
Haematemesis and melaena.
Iron-deficiency anaemia.
Acute perforation.

<i>N.B: With penetrating ulcers, pain may be referred to the back, left upper quadrant, or chest (misinterpreted as cardiac).</i>

Robbins 10e p.771 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.536

<b>🔴 Write down the complications of peptic ulcer disease (PUD). (DU-22/19Nv, RU-21/20/18Nv)</b>

Bleeding: occurs in 15–20% of patients (most frequent complication); may be life-threatening; accounts for 25% of ulcer-related deaths; may be the 1st indication of an ulcer.
Perforation: occurs in up to 5% of patients; accounts for two-thirds of ulcer-related deaths; rarely 1st indication.
Obstruction: mostly in chronic ulcers; secondary to oedema and scarring; ~2% of patients; most often with pyloric channel ulcers; causes incapacitating crampy abdominal pain; rarely causes total obstruction and intractable vomiting.
Malignant transformation: chronic duodenal ulcer never turns malignant; chronic gastric ulcer → carcinoma in <1% of cases.

Robbins 10e p.771 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.535

## 2c · Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumour (GIST)
<b>🔴 Short note: Gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST). (DU-21Ja,20Nv/M)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Mesenchymal tumours derived from the interstitial cells of Cajal (pacemaker cells of the gastrointestinal muscularis propria). Most common mesenchymal tumour of the abdomen.

<b>Peak age:</b> 60 years (<10% before age 40).

<b>Site:</b> >50% in the stomach; also small intestine, large intestine, extragastrointestinal sites.

<b>Behaviour:</b> generally benign but may be recurrent, aggressive, or metastatic.

Genetic mutation

<code>c-kit</code> oncogene (CD117) — detected by immunohistochemistry from Cajal cells.
Platelet-derived growth factor receptor-α (PDGFRA).

Morphology

Gross: can reach 30 cm; solitary, well-circumscribed, fleshy mass; overlying mucosa frequently ulcerated; cut surface has whorled appearance.
Microscopic: spindle cell type (thin elongated cells) or epithelioid type (plumper epithelial-appearing cells); mixtures common.

Symptoms

Mass effects; mucosal ulceration → blood loss → anaemia.

Treatment

Complete surgical resection (primary treatment for localised GIST); targeted therapy / tyrosine kinase inhibitor (<b>imatinib</b>) — inhibits tyrosine kinase activity of c-kit and PDGFRA.

Robbins 10e p.779 · BRS Pathology 5e p.232

## 2d · Gastric Adenocarcinoma
<b>🔴 Mention the sites, risk factors, clinical features, morphology (gross & microscopic) and spread of gastric carcinoma.</b>

Adenocarcinoma is the most common gastric malignancy (>90% of all gastric cancers).

Sites

Pylorus & antrum (50–60%)
Cardia (25%)
Body & fundus (15–25%)

<b>Early gastric carcinoma:</b> confined to mucosa & submucosa. <b>Advanced:</b> extends into muscularis propria and beyond.

Risk factors

Environmental: geographic (Japan, Finland, Iceland), smoked foods, nitrates/nitrites, pickled vegetables, excessive salt, low fruit/vegetables, low SES, smoking, alcohol.
Host factors: age >50, male sex, H. pylori infection, achlorhydria, chronic gastritis / pernicious anaemia, gastric adenoma, partial gastrectomy (stump carcinoma), chronic gastric ulcer.
Genetic: blood group A slightly increased risk, family history, hereditary nonpolyposis colon cancer (HNPCC), familial gastric carcinoma (E-cadherin mutation).

Role of <i>H. pylori</i> in gastric carcinoma

<i>H. pylori</i> → disruption of protective mechanisms → chronic gastritis → pangastritis → multifocal atrophic gastritis → reduced acid secretion → intestinal metaplasia/dysplasia → increased risk of gastric adenocarcinoma.

Clinical features

Early: dyspepsia, dysphagia, nausea.
Advanced: abdominal pain, weight loss (cachexia), anorexia, early satiety, anaemia, altered bowel habits, haematemesis and/or melaena.

Morphology

Gross — 3 macroscopic patterns: (1) Exophytic — protrusion into lumen; (2) Flat or depressed; (3) Excavated — shallow/deep ulcer. Large areas of infiltration → diffuse rugal flattening → rigid, thickened wall → linitis plastica ("leather bottle" appearance).
Microscopic:
Intestinal type: more common; expanding growth; bulky tumours of columnar gland-forming cells; abundant mucin in gland lumens.
Diffuse type: infiltrative growth; signet-ring cells diffusely infiltrating gastric mucosa; excessive mucin production.

Spread

Local invasion: oesophagus, duodenum, omentum, pancreas, liver, spleen, transverse colon.
Metastatic spread:
Virchow's node (left supraclavicular lymph node) — Troisier's sign.
Sister Mary Joseph nodule (periumbilical lymph node metastasis).
Krukenberg tumour — bilateral ovarian metastasis (signet-ring cells); gastric adenocarcinoma is the most common source.
Left axillary lymph node, Pouch of Douglas.
Haematogenous spread to liver, lungs, brain, bones, kidneys, adrenals.

Investigations

Barium meal X-ray; upper GI endoscopy + biopsy (gold standard); USG/CT (liver metastasis); chest X-ray; tumour markers: CEA, CA 19-9.

Robbins 10e pp.774–775 · Harsh Mohan 7e pp.538–542

## 3a · Intestinal Obstruction
<b>🔴 Mention the causes of intestinal obstruction. (VIVA)</b>

Hernias: weakness/defect in abdominal wall → protrusion of a serosa-lined pouch of peritoneum (hernia sac).
Adhesions: from surgical procedures, infection, or peritoneal inflammation (e.g. endometriosis).
Volvulus: loop of bowel twists about its mesenteric attachment → luminal and vascular compromise (obstruction + infarction). Most often in sigmoid colon, then caecum, small bowel.
Intussusception: segment of intestine telescopes into the immediately distal segment (propelled by peristalsis). Most common cause of intestinal obstruction in children <2 years. May progress to obstruction, compression of mesenteric vessels, and infarction.

Robbins 10e pp.780–781

## 3b · Meckel Diverticulum
<b>Short note: Meckel diverticulum.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> blind outpouching of all three layers of the bowel wall (true diverticulum) due to incomplete obliteration of the vitellointestinal duct. Most common congenital anomaly of the GI tract; occurs in the ileum.

🎯 <b>"Rule of 2s":</b> occurs in ~2% of population; generally within 2 feet (60 cm) of the ileocaecal valve; ~2 inches (5 cm) long; twice as common in males; most often symptomatic by age 2 (only ~4% are ever symptomatic).

Lining & complications

Almost always lined by small-intestinal epithelium; rarely contains ectopic gastric mucosa or pancreatic tissue.

<b>Complications:</b> perforation, haemorrhage, diverticulitis.

Robbins 10e p.755 · Pathoma 2014 p.106

## 3c · Megacolon & Hirschsprung Disease
<b>Short note: Megacolon. (CMU-22Nv)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Any form of marked dilatation of the entire colon or its segment.

Types & causes

Congenital:
Hirschsprung disease (common): congenital absence of ganglion cells in the bowel wall → aganglionic segment remains contracted.
Myopathies, neuropathies (rare).
Acquired:
Obstructive (tumour, post-inflammatory strictures)
Endocrine (myxoedema, cretinism)
CNS disorders (spina bifida, paraplegia, Parkinsonism)
Psychogenic (emotional disturbances, psychiatric disorders)
Connective tissue disease (scleroderma)
Chagas disease (Trypanosoma cruzi) — only example of acquired loss of ganglion cells; in all other acquired causes, bowel innervation is normal.

Harsh Mohan 7e pp.563–564

<b>🔴 Short note: Hirschsprung disease / congenital aganglionic megacolon. (DU-12Ju, CU-15Ja)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Congenital disease caused by absence of ganglion cells in the nerve plexus (Meissner's & Auerbach's plexus), usually of the distal hind gut, with hypertrophy of nerve fibres → functional obstruction of the colon. Incidence: ~1 in 5000 live births.

Pathogenesis

Migration of neural crest cells from caecum to rectum arrested prematurely (or premature death of ganglion cells) → distal intestinal segment lacking both Meissner submucosal plexus and Auerbach myenteric plexus = <b>aganglionosis</b> → absent coordinated peristalsis → functional obstruction → dilation proximal to the affected segment.

Sites

In ~⅔ of cases: rectum & sigmoid colon. Also: anorectal junction, rectosigmoid junction, whole colon, rarely terminal ileum.

Clinical features

Neonate: failure to pass meconium, abdominal distension, bilious vomiting.
Chronic (first few weeks of life): chronic intermittent constipation, gradual abdominal distension, failure to thrive.
Adult: severe constipation.

Investigations

Plain X-ray abdomen; barium enema X-ray; rectal suction biopsy (lack of ganglion cells); anorectal manometry.

<b>Diagnosis requires histologic confirmation that ganglion cells are absent within the affected segment.</b>

Treatment & complications

Surgical resection of the aganglionic segment + anastomosis of normal proximal colon to the rectum.

<b>Complications:</b> enterocolitis, intestinal obstruction, fluid/electrolyte disturbances, growth retardation, perforation, peritonitis, septicaemia.

Robbins 10e p.756 · Pathoma 2014 p.109

## 3d · Malabsorption & Diarrhoea
<b>🔴 Short note: Malabsorption syndrome. (For VIVA)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Defective absorption of fats, fat- and water-soluble vitamins, proteins, carbohydrates, electrolytes/minerals, and water.

<b>Hallmark:</b> steatorrhoea — excessive faecal fat; bulky, frothy, greasy, yellow or clay-coloured stools.

Five important causes

Pancreatic insufficiency
Coeliac disease
Tropical sprue
Crohn disease
Cystic fibrosis

<i>Others:</i> primary bile acid malabsorption, autoimmune enteropathy, disaccharidase deficiency, intestinal GVHD.

Symptoms

General: diarrhoea, flatus, abdominal pain, weight loss, muscle wasting, anorexia, distension, borborygmi.
Vitamin/mineral deficiency: anaemia (folate, B₁₂, pyridoxine), bleeding (vitamin K), osteopenia/tetany (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, vitamin D), peripheral neuropathy (vitamin A, B₁₂), goitre/vitiligo.

Robbins 10e p.783

<b>Define and classify diarrhoea.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Increase in stool mass, frequency, or fluidity (>200 g/day). Severe cases: >1 L/day; without fluid resuscitation → death. <i>Painful, bloody, small-volume diarrhoea = dysentery.</i>

Four major categories

Secretory diarrhoea: isotonic stool; persists during fasting.
Osmotic diarrhoea (e.g. lactase deficiency): excessive osmotic forces from unabsorbed luminal solutes; fluid >50 mOsm more concentrated than plasma; abates with fasting.
Malabsorptive diarrhoea: generalised failure of nutrient absorption; associated with steatorrhoea; relieved by fasting.
Exudative diarrhoea: inflammatory disease; purulent, often bloody stools; continues during fasting.

Robbins 10e p.784

## 3e · Coeliac Disease
<b>What is coeliac disease?</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Immune-mediated disorder triggered by ingestion of gluten-containing foods (wheat, rye, barley) in genetically predisposed individuals. Also known as coeliac sprue or gluten-sensitive enteropathy.

<b>Mechanism of malabsorption:</b> loss of brush-border surface area due to villous atrophy and possibly deficient enterocyte maturation from immune-mediated epithelial damage.

## 3f · Infectious Enterocolitis
<b>Write about pseudomembranous colitis.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Generally caused by <i>Clostridioides (Clostridium) difficile</i>; also called antibiotic-associated colitis / antibiotic-associated diarrhoea.

<b>Risk factors:</b> antibiotic treatment, advanced age, hospitalisation, immunosuppression.

<b>Clinical features:</b> fever, leukocytosis, abdominal pain, cramps, watery diarrhoea, dehydration.

<b>Diagnosis:</b> detection of <i>C. difficile</i> toxin (rather than by culture); supported by histopathology.

<b>Morphology:</b> mucopurulent exudate erupting from damaged crypts → "volcano" lesions → coalesce to form pseudomembranes (greyish mucosal exudates of necrotic, loosely adherent mucosal debris).

<b>Treatment:</b> metronidazole and vancomycin.

Robbins 10e p.793

<b>Write about Whipple disease.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Rare, multi-visceral chronic disease characterised by macrophages loaded with <i>Tropheryma whippelii</i> organisms.

<b>Clinical presentation (triad):</b> diarrhoea, weight loss, arthralgia. Extraintestinal symptoms (arthritis, fever, lymphadenopathy, neurologic/cardiac/pulmonary disease) may precede malabsorption by months/years.

<b>Morphologic features:</b> distinctive PAS-positive macrophages in intestinal mucosa; <i>T. whippelii</i> bacilli visualised by electron microscopy.

<b>Mechanism of malabsorption:</b> bacteria-laden macrophages accumulate within small-intestinal lamina propria and mesenteric lymph nodes → lymphatic obstruction → impaired lymph drainage.

Robbins 10e p.794

## 3g · Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)
<b>What do you mean by IBS? (SU-17M)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Functional gastrointestinal disorder characterised by chronic, relapsing abdominal pain, bloating, and changes in bowel habits. Peak prevalence: 20–40 years; significant female predominance.

Types (Rome criteria)

Diarrhoea-predominant IBS
Constipation-predominant IBS
Mixed subtypes

<b>Diagnosis:</b> endoscopic and microscopic evaluations are <i>normal</i>; diagnosis depends on clinical symptoms (abdominal pain/discomfort ≥3 days/month over 3 months, improving with defecation, with change in stool frequency or form).

Robbins 10e p.798

## 3h · Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)
<b>🔴 What is inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)? Name the IBDs. Describe the pathogenesis. (DU-24Nv,23M)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Chronic condition resulting from complex interactions between intestinal microbiota and host immunity in genetically predisposed individuals → inappropriate mucosal immune activation.

<b>Two disorders:</b> (1) ulcerative colitis — limited to colon & rectum, generally mucosa & submucosa; (2) Crohn disease — may involve any area from mouth to anus, typically transmural.

Pathogenesis — three components

Mucosal immunity: immune signalling and regulatory genes (HLA, cytokines) associated with IBD.
Autophagy & cellular stress responses: defects in autophagy contribute to IBD pathogenesis.
Host-microbial interactions: gut microbiome modulates mucosal immunity; interplay contributes to IBD.

<i>Why idiopathic?</i> The basis of the abnormalities is still not established.

Robbins 10e pp.798–800

<b>🔴 Short note: Crohn disease. (DU-24Nv,23M, SU-20M,09Ja)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Form of IBD; also called regional enteritis; commonly affects terminal ileum, ileocaecal valve & cecum but may occur anywhere from mouth to anus; typically <b>transmural</b>.

Pathogenesis

Prime culprits: T cells (particularly CD4⁺) — delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction induced by IFN-γ & TNF-α produced by T<sub>H</sub>1 cells.

Clinical features

Intestinal: non-bloody diarrhoea, fever, abdominal pain, weight loss, iron-deficiency anaemia, fibrosing strictures, fistulae, perforations, peritoneal abscesses.
Extra-intestinal: migratory polyarthritis, uveitis, sacroiliitis, ankylosing spondylitis, erythema nodosum, cutaneous granulomas, clubbing, pericholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis.

Morphology

Gross:
Site: any portion of GIT (most common: terminal ileum & colon).
Distribution: multiple separate, sharply delineated areas (skip lesions).
Wall: transmural inflammation down to serosa → strictures, fistulas, adhesions.
Lumen: narrowed → "string sign" on X-ray; mucosa shows serpiginous ulcers; intervening surviving mucosa swollen → "cobblestone appearance".
Mesenteric fat: extends around serosal surface ("creeping fat").
Microscopic:
Non-caseating granulomas (hallmark — present in ~35%).
Transmural infiltration of chronic inflammatory cells (lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages).
Distortion of mucosal architecture with loss of crypt epithelium.
Patchy ulceration (deep fissures).
Epithelial metaplasia.

Robbins 10e pp.799–801

<b>🔴 Short note: Ulcerative colitis. (DU-17M)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Severe ulcerating inflammatory bowel disease limited to the <b>colon & rectum</b>; generally limited to the <b>mucosa & submucosa</b>.

Clinical features

Intestinal: bloody diarrhoea with stringy mucoid material, urgency, tenesmus, lower abdominal pain and cramps (temporarily relieved by defecation).
Extra-intestinal: migratory polyarthritis, uveitis, sacroiliitis, ankylosing spondylitis, skin lesions (pyoderma gangrenosum), pericholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis.

<i>Arthritis is the most common extra-intestinal feature in both Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis.</i>

Morphology

Gross:
Continuous involvement of rectum and colon (no skip areas).
Red, raw colonic mucosa that bleeds easily.
Linear and superficial ulcers; adjacent preserved mucosa appears as polyps ("pseudopolyps"); tips may fuse to create mucosal bridges.
Chronic disease → mucosal atrophy (smooth surface, no folds).
Not transmural → colonic wall not thickened, serosal surface normal, strictures do not occur.
Microscopic:
Diffuse inflammation limited to mucosa & superficial submucosa.
Inflammatory infiltrates in lamina propria.
Crypt abscesses.
Crypt distortion.
Pseudopyloric epithelial metaplasia.
Depletion of goblet cells & mucin.
Granulomas not present.

Robbins 10e pp.799, 801–803

## 3i · Colorectal Carcinoma
<b>🔴 Colorectal carcinoma (CRC) — predisposing factors, clinical features, pathogenesis (APC/β-catenin & MSI), types.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Colorectal adenocarcinoma is the most common malignancy of the GI tract; ~15% of all cancer-related deaths; second only to lung cancer.

Predisposing factors

Age: peaks at 60–70 years; <20% before age 50.
Sex: males slightly more.
Dietary: low unabsorbable vegetable fibre; high refined carbohydrates & fat.
Geographic variations, obesity, physical inactivity.
Pre-existing adenomas.
Hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC / Lynch syndrome).
Inflammatory bowel disease (especially ulcerative colitis).
Diverticular disease (long duration), tobacco smoking.

Clinical features

Occult bleeding (melaena)
Change in bowel habits (more with left-sided growth)
Abdominal pain, weight loss (cachexia), anorexia, anaemia, weakness, malaise

<b>Distribution:</b> ~60% rectum; sigmoid/descending colon 25%; caecum/ileocaecal 10%; ascending colon/flexures 5%; transverse colon uncommon.

Pathogenesis

APC mutation / β-catenin mechanism (80% sporadic cases): loss of APC (adenomatous polyposis coli) tumour suppressor gene → β-catenin translocates to nucleus → activates MYC & cyclin D1 → cell proliferation. Followed by K-RAS point mutation (10–50%), DCC gene deletion (60–70%), p53 loss (70–80%).
Microsatellite instability (MSI) mechanism (10–15%): loss of DNA repair gene → repetitive DNA sequences (microsatellites) unstable during replication = hallmark of this pathway.

Investigations

Barium enema; MRI pelvis; USG/CT abdomen; transrectal ultrasound (TRUS); sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy + biopsy (gold standard); tumour markers: CEA, CA 19-9.

<b>Histopathological types:</b> 95% adenocarcinomas; remainder: undifferentiated, signet-ring cell, adenosquamous carcinomas; also sarcoma (leiomyosarcoma), lymphoma, carcinoid tumours.

Robbins 10e pp.813–814 · Harsh Mohan 7e pp.571–572, 587–588

<b>What information do you want from the histopathology report after hemicolectomy for colon cancer? (CMU-23Nv)</b>

Tumour characteristics: histological type, grade, size, depth of invasion.
Lymph node involvement: total nodes examined, positive nodes, extranodal extension.
Resection margins: proximal & distal (free of tumour?).
Lymphovascular and perineural invasion (LVI, PNI).
Pathologic staging (pTNM): pT (depth), pN (nodal), pM (distant metastasis).
Molecular/immunohistochemical markers: microsatellite instability (MSI) / mismatch repair (MMR); KRAS, NRAS, BRAF mutations; HER2; PD-L1.
Additional findings: precursor lesions (adenomas, dysplasia), signet-ring cells, mucinous features, tumour budding.

## 3j · Intestinal Polyps
<b>🔴 Define polyp. Classify intestinal polyps. (CU-18M, CMU-24M,23Nv)</b>

<b>Definition:</b> A tumourous mass that protrudes into the lumen of the gut; any elevation of the intestinal surface.

Polyps are most common in the colon but may occur in oesophagus, stomach, or small intestine.

Types

Non-neoplastic polyps:
Hyperplastic polyp (commonest)
Inflammatory polyp
Hamartomatous polyp: (a) juvenile polyps, (b) Peutz-Jeghers polyps
Neoplastic (adenomatous) polyps — true neoplasms:
Tubular adenoma (most common — 75%)
Tubulovillous adenoma
Villous adenoma (highest malignant potential — >30% become malignant; large sessile tumour with velvety surface, fingerlike villi)
Sessile serrated adenoma

🎯 <b>Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP):</b> autosomal dominant; numerous colorectal adenomas (≥100 needed for diagnosis) in teenagers; caused by somatic mutations of APC gene. <b>HNPCC (Lynch syndrome):</b> inherited mutations in mismatch repair genes; most common syndromic form of colon cancer; right colon predilection.

Robbins 10e pp.807, 810, 812–813

## 4 · Appendix
<b>🔴 Give the aetio-pathogenesis of acute appendicitis. (RMU-24M,22Nv)</b>

<b>Aetiology (50–80% associated with luminal obstruction):</b> faecalith (small stone-like stool mass), gallstone, tumour at the appendiceal opening (e.g. benign polyp), mass of worms (e.g. <i>Oxyuris vermicularis</i>).

Pathogenesis

Obstruction of appendiceal lumen → increased mucous secretion & accumulation → increased intraluminal pressure → venous obstruction → ischaemia & venous congestion → microbial proliferation → inflammation → additional oedema & exudates → further embarrassment of blood supply → more injury → acute appendicitis. If not stopped → gangrenous necrosis.

Robbins 10e p.819

<b>Describe the morphology of acute appendicitis. What are its complications? (DU-03S, RMU-24M,22Nv)</b>

Morphology

Gross: appendix swollen & distended; serosal covering reddened; cut section: lumen filled with pus; mucosa shows ulceration & foci of suppurative necrosis.
Microscopic: subserosal vessels congested; neutrophil infiltration throughout mucosa, submucosa & muscularis propria; mucosal ulcerations; muscle fibres separated by oedema fluid.

Clinical features

Colicky pain (initially periumbilical, then localised to right iliac fossa); nausea, vomiting; mild pyrexia.

Complications

Appendicular lump
Appendicular abscess
Perforation / burst appendix
Gangrene of the appendix
Peritonitis (generalised)
Pyelophlebitis
Portal venous thrombosis
Liver abscess
Bacteraemia
Pelvic abscess
Subphrenic abscess

<b>Laboratory tests:</b> CBC (neutrophilic leukocytosis); CRP elevated (>10 mg/L); imaging (ultrasound/CT) for confirmation.

Robbins 10e p.819

<b>Name the tumours of the appendix. What is pseudomyxoma peritonei?</b>

Tumours of the appendix

Neuroendocrine (carcinoid) tumour (most common)
Adenoma
Adenocarcinoma
Mucinous cystadenoma
Mucinous cystadenocarcinoma

<b>Pseudomyxoma peritonei:</b> in mucinous cystadenocarcinoma, invasion of appendicular wall by malignant neoplastic cells and spread beyond appendix as peritoneal implants → peritoneal cavity distended with tenacious, semisolid mucin.

Robbins 10e p.819
