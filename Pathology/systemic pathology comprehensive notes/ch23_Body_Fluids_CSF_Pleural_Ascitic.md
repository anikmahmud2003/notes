# Ch 23 — Body Fluids (CSF, Pleural, Ascitic)     <i>Full-detail flashcards (Q→A) · All abbreviations expanded · Robbins 4-pillar answers</i>
### 🧠 Big Picture — Body Fluids (CSF, Pleural, Ascitic)
<b>Core concept:</b> Body fluids occupy defined compartments — intracellular (ICF, ~28 L) and extracellular (ECF, ~14 L). ECF divides into interstitial fluid (11 L), plasma (3 L), and transcellular fluid (CSF, pleural, pericardial, peritoneal, synovial, intra-ocular). Laboratory examination of these fluids is a cornerstone of clinical pathology.
<b>🔴 CSF:</b> Collected by <b>lumbar puncture (LP)</b> between L3–L4. Normal values: pressure 50–250 mm H₂O, glucose 50–70 mg/dL (>50–60% of blood), protein 15–45 mg/dL, chloride 710–750 mg/dL, cells 0–4/mm³ (lymphocytes). <b>Meningitis</b> is the #1 indication — bacterial (pyogenic) vs viral vs tubercular vs fungal have distinct CSF profiles.
<b>📌 Pleural fluid:</b> Distinguishes transudate (low protein, e.g. heart failure, nephrotic, cirrhosis) from <b>exudate</b> (high protein, e.g. TB, pneumonia, malignancy). Tubercular effusion: lymphocytic, high ADA (>40 U/L). Malignant effusion: haemorrhagic, malignant cells on Pap smear.
<b>📌 Ascitic fluid:</b> Gross inspection + differential cell count + albumin assay. SAAG (Serum–Ascites Albumin Gradient) distinguishes portal-hypertensive from non-portal-hypertensive causes. Exudative findings: protein >25 g/L, elevated triglycerides, decreased glucose, increased amylase.
Ref: Arif Guide 2025 Vol-2, pp.456–469 · Davidson's 23rd · Guyton 11th · Prof. Khaleque

## 2 · CSF Examination & Indications
<b>🔴 What are the indications of CSF examination? Name 5 important indications.</b>

<b>Very Important for VIVA</b> — Indications of CSF examination:

Infections: meningitis, encephalitis
Cerebral abscess
Sub-arachnoid haemorrhage (SAH): if CT scan is negative or unavailable
Inflammatory conditions: Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), neurosyphilis, acute poliomyelitis, multiple sclerosis, sarcoidosis, acute polyneuritis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)
Infiltrative conditions / neurological malignancies: carcinomatous meningitis, lymphoma, leukaemia
To confirm raised intracranial pressure: when CT scan excludes danger of brainstem herniation — benign intracranial hypertension (BIH), cerebral venous thrombosis

<b>What is lumbar puncture? Give its indications (diagnostic + therapeutic) and contraindications.</b>

<b>Lumbar puncture (LP):</b> A technique used to obtain both a CSF sample and an indirect measure of intracranial pressure.

A. Diagnostic indications

Infections: meningitis, encephalitis
Cerebral abscess
Sub-arachnoid haemorrhage (if CT scan negative/unavailable)
Inflammatory conditions: acute poliomyelitis, multiple sclerosis, sarcoidosis, acute polyneuritis, SLE, neurosyphilis
Primary or secondary metastatic malignancies: carcinomatous meningitis, lymphoma, leukaemia
Raised intracranial pressure: BIH, cerebral venous thrombosis

B. Therapeutic indications

Administration of drugs: antibiotics (meningitis), antimitotics (oncology)
Instillation of contrast media or isotopes: myelography, cisternography
Spinal anaesthesia

Contraindications of lumbar puncture

Local tissue suppuration
Raised intracranial pressure
Suspected intracranial mass lesions
Patient on aspirin, heparin, warfarin therapy
Depressed level of consciousness
Bleeding disorder
Lack of cooperation

Complications of lumbar puncture

Infection
Brainstem herniation
Post-lumbar puncture headache
Injury to intervertebral disc
Bleeding due to injury to vertebral plexus
CSF leakage
Local pain
Shock
Transient ischaemic attack (TIA)
Vaso-vagal attack

Quantity or volume
Colour
Appearance
Pressure
Specific gravity
Presence of blood
Clot formation (CSF is normally clear and colourless)

2. Cell count

Mainly lymphocytes (normally 0–5 lymphocytes/mm³).

3. Chemical examination

Protein, glucose & chloride levels.

7. Cytological examination

To detect malignant cells.

Useful to differentiate pyogenic meningitis from other meningitis — in pyogenic meningitis, CSF sugar is very low or absent

Any inflammatory condition, such as:

Pyogenic meningitis
Tubercular meningitis

<b>What is aseptic meningitis?</b>

<b>Aseptic meningitis:</b> Usually refers to <b>viral meningitis</b> — CSF cultures are negative (sterile) but there is meningeal inflammation.

<b>What do you mean by pleocytosis?</b>

<b>Pleocytosis:</b> A term used to describe an <b>increased cell count</b> (Gk. <i>pleion</i> = more), usually referring to an increase in white blood cell (WBC) count in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). It is indicative of an inflammatory, infectious, or malignant condition.

## 7 · VIVA Corner on CSF Examination & Meningitis
<b>Why does CSF look cloudy or turbid?</b>

<b>Cloudy/turbid CSF:</b> Due to excess amount of protein and/or the presence of cells (neutrophils in pyogenic, lymphocytes in tubercular/viral).

<b>Why is protein increased in all types of meningitis?</b>

<b>Why protein is increased in ALL types of meningitis:</b> Because meningitis is an inflammation and in every inflammation there is <b>exudate formation</b>, which contains much more protein than normal CSF.

<b>Why is CSF sugar markedly reduced in pyogenic meningitis?</b>

<b>Low glucose in pyogenic meningitis:</b> Because pyogenic bacteria <b>consume glucose as their food and energy source</b>.

<b>Why is glucose normal in viral meningitis?</b>

<b>Normal glucose in viral meningitis:</b> Because <b>viruses do not consume glucose</b>.

🎯 <b>Key VIVA point:</b> CSF glucose = 60% of blood glucose. If blood glucose is very high (e.g. uncontrolled diabetes mellitus with blood glucose >200 mg/dL), CSF glucose can still be "high" (>120 mg/dL) even in pyogenic meningitis — because the baseline is elevated.

<b>Mention the hallmark of meningitis caused by Neisseria. Name two important physical signs in meningitis.</b>

<b>Hallmark of meningococcal (<i>N. meningitidis</i>) meningitis:</b> <b>Meningococcal skin rash (purpuric rash)</b>.

Important physical signs in meningitis

Neck rigidity
Kernig's sign: extension at the knee with the hip joint flexed causes spasm in the hamstring muscles
Brudzinski's sign: passive flexion of the neck causes flexion of the hips and knees

Ref: Davidson's 23rd/1019

<b>Mention the commonest cause of pyogenic meningitis. Mention the commonest cause of viral meningitis. Name one special test for tubercular meningitis.</b>

<b>Commonest cause of pyogenic meningitis:</b> <i>Neisseria meningitidis</i>.

<b>Commonest cause of viral meningitis:</b> <i>Echovirus</i> and <i>Coxsackie virus</i>.

<b>Special test for tubercular meningitis:</b> <b>Adenosine deaminase (ADA) test</b> — elevated (>40 U/L) in tubercular meningitis.

<b>Clinical diagnosis: Meningitis (probably pyogenic).</b>

<b>In that meningitis case: what laboratory tests should be done?</b>

<b>Confirmatory investigation:</b> CSF analysis — lumbar puncture followed by physical, cytological, biochemical, and bacteriological examination.

Robbins 10e – CNS: Meningitis

<b>📋 Clinical case: A child with sudden high-grade intermittent fever, neck rigidity, and purpuric rash. What is your diagnosis? Give expected CSF findings.</b>

<b>Diagnosis:</b> <b>Meningococcal meningitis</b> — the purpuric rash is the hallmark of <i>N. meningitidis</i> infection.

Expected CSF findings

Physical: Pressure — increased; Colour — cloudy or hazy
Cytology: Cell count — 1000–5000/mm³; predominantly neutrophils
Biochemical: Protein — normal/elevated; Glucose — decreased
Gram stain: Gram-positive diplococci (intracellular)
Culture on blood agar: Growth found

<b>📋 Clinical case: A child with recurrent otitis media, headache, fever, drowsiness, neck stiffness. What is the likely diagnosis? Give positive laboratory findings.</b>

<b>Diagnosis:</b> Meningitis — most probably due to <i>H. influenzae</i> or <i>S. pneumoniae</i> (otitis media is a common predisposing factor).

Laboratory findings

Physical: Pressure — increased; Colour — cloudy/hazy
Cytology: Cell count 1000–5000/mm³; predominantly neutrophils
Biochemical: Protein — normal/elevated; Glucose — decreased
Gram stain: Gram-positive diplococci (S. pneumoniae) or gram-negative bacilli (H. influenzae)
Culture: Blood agar media for S. pneumoniae; Chocolate agar media for H. influenzae

<b>📋 Clinical case: A 6-year-old malnourished child with low-grade fever for 15 days, loss of consciousness, neck rigidity. What is the diagnosis?</b>

<b>Probable diagnosis: Meningitis, probably tubercular</b> — low-grade fever for 15 days + malnourished child favours tuberculosis (TB).

Robbins 10e – TB meningitis

<b>In that TB-meningitis case: what lab tests are needed?</b>

Lumbar puncture & CSF study

Colour: clear/cloudy
Cell count: 50–5000/mm³, mainly lymphocytes
Glucose: low, typically <40 mg/dL
Protein: elevated
Acid-fast bacilli (AFB) staining: may be positive
Culture on Lowenstein-Jensen (LJ) media: growth found
ADA (adenosine deaminase): elevated

Other tests

Complete Blood Count (CBC): low Hb%, ESR may be high
Chest X-ray
Tuberculin test (Mantoux)
Gastric aspirates for AFB & culture

Robbins 10e – TB meningitis

<b>📋 Clinical case: A patient with pyogenic meningitis has HIGH CSF glucose. How is this possible?</b>

The patient has <b>uncontrolled diabetes mellitus (DM)</b>.

<b>Explanation:</b>

CSF glucose = 60% of blood glucose level
If blood glucose = 100 mg/dL → normal CSF = 60 mg/dL
If blood glucose = 200 mg/dL → CSF glucose = 120 mg/dL (still "high" despite pyogenic meningitis)

Therefore, although the patient has pyogenic meningitis, the CSF glucose will be higher than normal because the blood glucose baseline is elevated.

## 8 · Pleural Fluid Study
<b>What is pleural fluid? Mention the significance of pleural fluid study. Differentiate transudate from exudate.</b>

<b>Pleural fluid:</b> The fluid present in the pleural cavity. Normal amount: 1–10 mL. Abnormal accumulation of fluid beyond this level = <b>pleural effusion</b>.

Significance of pleural fluid study

By studying pleural fluid, we can detect the following diseases:

Exudative pleural fluid (high protein content)

Pulmonary tuberculosis
Pneumonia (para-pneumonic effusion)
Bronchial carcinoma
Pulmonary infarction
Collagen disease (SLE, rheumatoid arthritis)
Lymphoma

Transudative pleural fluid (low protein content)

Congestive cardiac failure (CCF)
Nephrotic syndrome
Cirrhosis of liver
Malnutrition
Hypothyroidism
Meigs' syndrome (ovarian fibroma + ascites + right-sided pleural effusion)
Acute rheumatic fever

🎯 <b>Transudate = systemic causes (low protein):</b> Heart, Liver, Kidney failure. <b>Exudate = local causes (high protein):</b> TB, Pneumonia, Cancer.

Positive findings of exudative ascitic fluid

Protein: >25 g/L

<b>What is SAAG? How does it help differentiate causes of ascites?</b>

<b>SAAG (Serum–Ascites Albumin Gradient):</b> Calculated by subtracting the ascitic fluid albumin from the serum albumin value obtained at the same time.
