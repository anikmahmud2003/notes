🧠 Big Picture — Semen Analysis (boss-level overview)

Semen analysis evaluates the quality and quantity of seminal fluid (liquid portion) and spermatozoa (microscopic moving cells) secreted during ejaculation. It is the cornerstone investigation for male infertility and also serves medicolegal purposes (paternity, rape cases) and post-vasectomy follow-up.

What is measured: 🔴 Volume (2–5 ml) → Appearance/Colour (opalescent, greyish-white) → pH (≥7.8) → Sperm concentration (20–110 million/ml) → Motility (≥50% forward) → Morphology (≥30–50% normal) → Viability (≥75%) → Liquefaction (within 30 min) → Fructose (1.5–6.5 mg/dl) → Leucocytes (<1 million/ml).

Key terminology map: Normozoospermia (all normal) → Oligospermia (low count) → Asthenozoospermia (low motility) → Teratozoospermia (abnormal morphology) → Azoospermia (no sperm) → Necrozoospermia (all dead) → Polyzoospermia (excessively high count). Causes of oligospermia/azoospermia are classified as pre-testicular, testicular, and post-testicular — always think systematically!

1 · Indications of Semen Analysis

🔴 What are the indications of semen analysis? Enumerate the medicolegal importance.

Indications of semen analysis (Very Important for VIVA — asked repeatedly DU, RMU, CMU, BUP, SU):

  1. Evaluation of male infertility — the most common indication; assesses count, motility, morphology.
  2. Follow-up after vasectomy — to confirm successful occlusion (absence of sperm).
  3. Artificial insemination — to evaluate donor or partner semen quality before assisted reproduction.
  4. Paternity test (forensic medicine purpose) — DNA profiling from seminal material.
  5. In suspected rape cases — for identification of the accused (forensic identification).

Medicolegal importance: semen analysis is crucial in (a) paternity disputes — linking biological fatherhood, (b) sexual assault cases — identifying the perpetrator through spermatozoa or seminal markers, and (c) post-vasectomy confirmation — legal proof of sterility.

2 · Semen Collection Procedure

How is semen collected for analysis? Describe the collection and transport procedure.

A. Preparation of the Patient

  • Patient should be instructed to collect semen after 3–5 days of sexual abstinence.
  • Should be obtained by masturbation (self-stimulation) or collected at home during intercourse by coitus interruptus (withdrawal method).
  • Condom should be avoided, as condoms contain spermicidal agents that kill sperm.
  • Patient should evacuate his bladder before ejaculation.
  • Patient must wash hands before collection.

B. Collection of Specimen & Transport

  • Should be collected in a dry, clean, plastic container provided by the laboratory.
  • Condom collection is unacceptable (spermicidal contamination).
  • Patient's name, age, date, and time of collection should be recorded on the container.
  • Sample should be taken to the laboratory within 1 hour of collection and kept at body temperature during transport.

What advices/prerequisites should be given before doing semen analysis?

Advices before semen analysis (asked as DU-20Ju, 16Ja, SU-13Ju, CU-15Ja):

  1. Semen should be collected after 3–5 days of sexual abstinence.
  2. Should be obtained by masturbation or collected at home during intercourse (coitus interruptus). Condom should be avoided as it contains spermicidal agent.
  3. Patient should evacuate his bladder before ejaculation.
  4. Should be collected in a dry, clean, plastic container provided by the laboratory.
  5. Condom collection is unacceptable.
  6. Sample should be taken to the laboratory within 1 hour of collection and kept at body temperature.
🎯 Key point: The "3–5 days abstinence" rule ensures optimal sperm concentration and quality for accurate analysis.

4 · Normal Findings of Semen Analysis (Reference Values)

🔴 Mention the normal findings of semen analysis. Write a normal semen analysis report.

Normal findings of semen analysis ("Please read this at least 10 times for VIVA" — Arif):

A. Macroscopic (Physical) Examination

ParameterNormal Value
AppearanceOpalescent / Opaque
ColourGreyish white
Volume2–5 ml per ejaculation
ViscosityHigh
ConsistencyThick
OdourMusty / Fishy
pH range≥ 7.8 (slightly alkaline)

B. Microscopic Examination

ParameterNormal Value
Sperm concentration20–110 million / ml
Sperm motility60–65% sperms actively motile within 1 hour
Sperm morphology>50% sperms morphologically normal (normal head, body & tail)
Sperm viability>75% sperms are viable (alive)
Leucocytes (WBC)<1 million / ml
LiquefactionComplete within 30 minutes
Sperm clumpingAbsent

C. Biochemical

ParameterNormal Value
Fructose estimation1.5–6.5 mg/dl

WHO cut-offs (note differences):

  • Most WHO laboratories use a cut-off of 50% motility as the lower limit of normal.
  • Greater than 30% normal morphology is accepted by WHO (some newer WHO 2010+ criteria use stricter >4% normal forms).

Low semen volume may indicate retrograde ejaculation (semen enters the bladder instead of being expelled externally).

What information can we get from semen analysis? Mention the features examined and important messages in reporting.

Basic semen analysis measures (4 parameters):

  1. Semen volume
  2. Sperm concentration (count)
  3. Sperm motility
  4. Sperm morphology

Additional tests:

  • pH — high pH may be associated with ejaculatory tract obstruction or seminal vesicle dysfunction.
  • Fructose levels — absence of fructose may indicate ejaculatory tract obstruction or seminal vesicle dysfunction.
  • WBC (leucocyte) counts within semen — to detect infection (leucospermia).

Important messages in semen analysis reporting: Always comment on (1) volume, (2) sperm count, (3) motility percentage, (4) morphology, (5) liquefaction status, (6) presence/absence of agglutination, (7) WBC count, (8) fructose (if requested).

Ref: Lecture SSMC, BSMMU Lab. Medicine & AFIP Clinical Pathology

Define normozoospermia.

Normozoospermia: a condition where all semen parameters are within normal reference ranges — normal volume, normal sperm count (>20 million/ml), normal motility (>50% forward motile), and normal morphology (>30% normal forms). The male is considered fertile.

5 · Sperm Analysis Terminology (Classification)

🔴 Define oligospermia and necrospermia. Enumerate all sperm analysis terminologies.

Complete sperm analysis terminology (must-know for VIVA):

TermDefinition
NormozoospermiaAll semen parameters are normal
Oligospermia (Oligozoospermia)Reduced sperm number (<20 million sperm/ml of semen)
• Mild to moderate: 5–20 million/ml
• Severe: <5 million/ml
AsthenozoospermiaReduced sperm motility (<50% sperm have forward motility)
TeratozoospermiaIncreased abnormal forms of sperm (<30% are morphologically normal)
AzoospermiaNo sperm in semen (complete absence of spermatozoa)
Necrozoospermia (Necrospermia)All sperms are non-viable or dead
Polyzoospermia (Polypermia)Sperm count is >350 million/ml

Note on Aspermia (= Ejaculation failure): absence of semen entirely — no ejaculate produced.

🎯 Mnemonic: "Normo → all OK; Oligo → few; Astheno → slow; Terato → ugly; Azo → none; Necro → dead; Poly → too many."

6 · Male Fertility Status by Examining Semen

How can you know male fertility status by examining semen?

Three parameters determine fertility status:

A. Sperm Count → Fertility Correlation

ConditionSperm CountFertility Status
NormozoospermiaNormal (>20 million/ml)Fertile
Oligozoospermia<20 million/ml
(Mild–moderate: 5–20; Severe: <5)		Susceptible to infertility
AzoospermiaNo sperm in semenInfertile
NecrozoospermiaAll sperms non-viable / deadInfertile
Polyzoospermia>350 million/mlSusceptible to infertility

B. Motility of Sperm

For normal fertility, >50% spermatozoa must be motile (forward progressive motility). Most WHO laboratories use 50% as the lower limit of normal.

C. Morphology of Sperm

For normal fertility, >30% of sperms must be morphologically normal (normal head, body & tail). WHO accepts >30%; newer WHO 2010 criteria use >4% strict normal forms.

7 · Oligospermia — Short Note

🔴 What is oligospermia? Give its causes.

Oligospermia: less than 20 million sperm/ml of semen. Classified as mild–moderate (5–20 million/ml) or severe (<5 million/ml).

Causes (organised systematically — pre-testicular, testicular, post-testicular)

A. Idiopathic

No identifiable cause found.

B. Pre-testicular Causes

  • Hypogonadism due to any cause
  • Drugs, alcohol, smoking
  • Heat exposure (to the testes)
  • Strenuous riding of bicycle (repeated perineal pressure & heat)
  • Tight-fitting undergarments (heat retention)
  • Diabetes mellitus (DM)
  • Cancer treatment — radiotherapy / chemotherapy
  • Severe illness: malignancy, liver failure, renal failure

C. Testicular Causes

  • Age (advancing age)
  • Genetic defect
  • Chromosomal abnormality — e.g., Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)
  • Cryptorchidism (undescended testis)
  • Orchitis (testicular infection — e.g., mumps orchitis)
  • Varicocele, hydrocele
  • Trauma / surgery to the testes
  • Neoplasm: seminoma

D. Post-testicular Causes

  • Infection: prostatitis
  • Obstruction in vas deferens
  • Obstruction in ejaculatory duct
  • Absence of vas deferens (congenital bilateral absence — associated with CFTR mutations / cystic fibrosis carrier state)

8 · Azoospermia — Short Note

🔴 What is azoospermia? What are the causes of azoospermia?

Azoospermia: no sperm in semen — complete lack of spermatozoa in the ejaculate. It occurs in approximately 5% of infertile men.

Causes (pre-testicular, testicular, post-testicular + idiopathic)

A. Idiopathic

No identifiable cause found.

B. Pre-testicular Causes

  • Hypogonadism due to any cause
  • Drugs, alcohol
  • Diabetes mellitus (DM)
  • Cancer treatment — radiotherapy / chemotherapy
  • Severe illness: malignancy, liver failure, renal failure
  • Haemochromatosis (iron overload → pituitary/gonadal damage)
  • Sickle cell anaemia

C. Testicular Causes

  • Primary / Secondary testicular failure
  • Genetic defect
  • Chromosomal abnormality — e.g., Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)
  • Cryptorchidism (undescended testis)
  • Orchitis
  • Varicocele, hydrocele
  • Trauma / surgery
  • Sperm autoimmunity — anti-sperm antibodies (Ab)

D. Post-testicular Causes

  • Infection: epididymitis, prostatitis
  • Obstruction in vas deferens
  • Obstruction in ejaculatory duct
  • Absence of vas deferens
  • Vasectomy (surgical occlusion)

Ref: Lecture SSMC, BSMMU Lab. Medicine & AFIP Clinical Pathology

A 36-year-old male presented with infertility. Semen analysis showed absent sperm. Comment on the semen analysis and give possible causes.

Comment: Azoospermia — complete absence of spermatozoa in the ejaculate.

Possible causes: (organise as pre-testicular, testicular, post-testicular)

  • Pre-testicular: hypogonadism, DM, drugs/chemotherapy, severe systemic illness, haemochromatosis, sickle cell anaemia.
  • Testicular: primary/secondary testicular failure, Klinefelter syndrome, cryptorchidism, orchitis, varicocele, anti-sperm antibodies, prior trauma/surgery.
  • Post-testicular: epididymitis/prostatitis, vas deferens obstruction (congenital or acquired), ejaculatory duct obstruction, vasectomy.
  • Idiopathic: no identifiable cause.

9 · Oligoasthenozoospermia

Short note: Oligoasthenozoospermia.

Oligoasthenozoospermia: a sperm disorder involving two abnormalities simultaneously:

  1. Oligospermia: low sperm count (<20 million/ml).
  2. Asthenozoospermia: abnormal sperm motility — more than 60% of sperm being immotile or unable to move in a straight direction.

Causes

  • In most cases, causes are unknown / idiopathic.
  • Testicular diseases — e.g., orchitis.
  • Some genetic disorders — e.g., Klinefelter syndrome, immotile cilia syndrome (Kartagener syndrome).
  • Immunologic — anti-sperm antibodies causing agglutination and reduced motility.

Treatment

Treatment of reversible causes, if any (e.g., infection → antibiotics; varicocele → surgery; hormonal → correction).

10 · Etiological Factors of Male Infertility

🔴 Mention the etiological factors of male infertility.

Etiological factors of male infertility (Very Important for VIVA — asked DU-10Ja):

A. Failure of Spermatogenesis

  • Incomplete development of testis / undescended testis (cryptorchidism)
  • Absence of testis
  • Orchitis — due to mumps, influenza, Chlamydia
  • Cryptorchidism (repeated for emphasis)
  • Chromosomal abnormality: Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)
  • Damage of testis — due to radiation, trauma, or heat
  • Diseases of testis — TB (tuberculosis), syphilis, seminoma
  • Varicocele (↑scrotal temperature → impaired spermatogenesis)
  • Drugs: nicotine, sulfonamide, antihistamines, cytotoxic drugs, alcohol, pesticides
  • Anti-sperm antibody (autoimmune destruction)
  • Endocrine disease:
    • Hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction (trauma, radiation, tumour, surgery)
    • Hyperprolactinaemia
    • Thyroid disorders (thyrotoxicosis / hyperthyroidism)
    • Adrenal hyperplasia
  • DNA damage by reactive oxygen species (ROS), fever, and high testicular temperature
  • General factors: diabetes mellitus (DM), smoking

B. Failure of Transport of Semen

Bilateral obstruction of epididymis, vas deferens, or ejaculatory duct.

C. Failure to Deposit Sperm in Vagina

  • Impotency (erectile dysfunction)
  • Retrograde ejaculation (semen enters bladder)
  • Premature ejaculation

D. Errors of Seminal Fluid

Low volume, physicochemical abnormality of semen — including azoospermia, oligospermia, teratospermia, hypospermia, etc.

11 · Pseudocyesis (False Pregnancy)

Define pseudocyesis.

Pseudocyesis (false pregnancy): a syndrome in which a woman shows all the symptoms and signs of being pregnant except for the presence of a fetus. Her belly grows like a pregnant woman, and she can even go into false labour. It is a psychosomatic condition.

12 · Infertility Test (Male & Female)

🔴 How will you proceed to diagnose a case of male and female infertility? Write a short note on infertility test.

Infertility test: tests meant to establish the reason why a couple is not able to conceive naturally. Overall, 20–30% of infertility cases are due to a problem with the male partner.

A. Diagnosis of Male Infertility

  1. Medical, sexual, and social history
  2. General physical examination (including examination of genitals)
  3. Specific fertility tests:
    • Semen analysis — physical, chemical, and microscopic test
    • Immunological assay / Anti-sperm antibody test
    • Sperm function test
    • Post-ejaculatory urine analysis (to rule out retrograde ejaculation)
    • Hormone testing (testosterone and other male hormones)
    • Transrectal and scrotal ultrasound
    • Genetic testing

B. Diagnosis of Female Infertility

  1. Medical, sexual, and social history
  2. General physical examination (including regular gynaecological examination)
  3. Specific fertility tests:
    • Hormone assay — LH (luteinising hormone), FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone), oestradiol, progesterone, prolactin, thyroid & pituitary hormones
    • Hysterosalpingography — special X-ray to locate blockage or problems in the uterus and fallopian tubes
    • Ovulation testing — blood test to measure hormone levels to determine ovulation
    • Ovarian reserve test — determines quality and quantity of eggs available for ovulation
    • Diagnostic laparoscopy — identifies endometriosis, scarring, blockages of fallopian tubes, ovarian & uterine problems
    • Imaging tests — pelvic ultrasound, hysterosonography, hysteroscopy
    • Endometrial biopsy — to verify ovulation and inspect uterine lining
    • Pap's smear
    • Genetic testing — to determine genetic defects causing infertility

13 · Hormones for Infertility Evaluation

What are the common hormones done in male and female to evaluate infertility?

A. Common Hormones in Male Infertility Evaluation

  1. Testosterone (primary male sex hormone — low levels → impaired spermatogenesis)
  2. Thyroid hormone (T3, T4, TSH — thyroid dysfunction impairs fertility)
  3. Prolactin (hyperprolactinaemia suppresses GnRH → hypogonadism)

B. Common Hormones in Female Infertility Evaluation

  1. Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH) — measured on day 3 of the menstrual cycle; elevated FSH → diminished ovarian reserve
  2. Luteinising Hormone (LH) — LH surge triggers ovulation
  3. Progesterone — to confirm ovulation (elevated in luteal phase)
  4. Thyroid hormone (T3, T4, TSH)
  5. Prolactin — hyperprolactinaemia inhibits ovulation
  6. Testosterone (elevated in PCOS — polycystic ovarian syndrome)
  7. Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH) — marker of ovarian reserve
