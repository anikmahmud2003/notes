# Ch 26 — Lipid and Cholesterol     <i>Full-detail flashcards (Q→A) · one short question per card · inline True/False practice · Robbins 4-pillar answers</i>
### 🧠 Big Picture — Lipids, Lipoproteins & Atherosclerosis Risk
Lipids are <b>water-insoluble</b> molecules that must travel in blood as <b>lipoproteins</b> (lipid + apolipoprotein complexes). The clinical <b>lipid profile</b> measures: Total Cholesterol (TC), Triacylglycerols (TAG/TG), Low-Density Lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and High-Density Lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C).
🔴 <b>LDL = "bad cholesterol"</b> — carries cholesterol from liver → peripheral tissues → <b>promotes atherosclerosis</b>. 🔴 <b>HDL = "good cholesterol"</b> — carries cholesterol from periphery → liver → protects against atherosclerosis (reverse cholesterol transport).
📌 Four lipoprotein classes by density: <b>Chylomicrons → VLDL → LDL → HDL</b> (density ↑, size ↓). VLDL and LDL are <b>atherogenic</b>; HDL is anti-atherogenic.
🎯 <b>Dyslipidaemia</b> = abnormal lipid profile → major modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and coronary artery disease (CAD). Diagnosed by fasting lipid profile; managed by diet, exercise, and drugs (statins, fibrates).
Arif Guide 2025 Vol-2, Ch 26, pp.487–492 · Ref: Davidson's 21st ed; Harper's 28th/29th ed; Lippincott's 5th ed

## 1 · What Is a Lipid Profile?
<b>❓ What is a lipid profile? Give its definition and objectives.</b>

<b>Lipid profile</b> means the pattern or status of lipids in plasma — a group of laboratory tests that assess lipid status and evaluate risk factors for <b>atherosclerosis</b> and <b>coronary artery disease (CAD)</b>.

<b>Frequently examined:</b> DU-13/07Ja, BUP-22Nv, CMU-21/18M, DU-17M, RU-10Ju, CU-19Nv, DU-23Nv/21Oct/18Nv/17/14/11/07Ja/06S, SMU-23M/21Nv, RU-17Jw/Ja/16Ja/13Ju, CU-15Ju/Ja/10/09Ju/08Ja.

## 4 · Patient Preparation & Procedure
<b>❓ How do you prepare a patient for lipid profile testing? (Preparation rules)</b>

Preparation (4 rules)

10–12 hours fasting prior to the test.
Usual diet in the previous 3 days — no dietary change allowed.
Usual activities in the previous 3 days.
Individual must be free from trauma, surgery, acute infection, and pregnancy.

Arif Guide 2025 Vol-2 p.488

<b>❓ Describe the procedure for lipid profile testing.</b>

Collect a fasting morning blood sample.
Blood is taken in a plain (red-top) test tube and allowed to clot.
Serum is separated and lipid profile components are measured.

Arif Guide 2025 Vol-2 p.488

## 5 · Which Fraction Is "Better"? — TG
<b>❓ Which fraction of the lipid profile is better and why?</b>

<b>Triacylglycerol (TG)</b> is the better fraction of the lipid profile because it is one of the <b>major treatable risk factors for cardiovascular disease</b>, whilst severe hypertriglyceridaemia also predisposes to <b>acute pancreatitis</b>.

Ref: Davidson's 21st/449

## 6 · Indications of Lipid Profile
<b>❓ Enumerate the indications/objectives of lipid profile.</b>

Diagnosis of dyslipidaemia (abnormal lipid levels).
Assessment of prognosis for a known dyslipidaemic patient.
Assessment of risk for atherosclerosis and coronary artery disease (CAD).

Arif Guide 2025 Vol-2 p.487

## 7 · Clinical Importance & Screening
<b>❓ What is the clinical importance of lipid profile in cardiovascular disease screening?</b>

<b>Screening for cardiovascular disease (CVD):</b>

Increased LDL-C is directly related to cardiovascular disease (more LDL = more risk).
Increased HDL-C is inversely related to cardiovascular disease (more HDL = less risk).

Ref: Davidson's 21st/451, 1296; Harper's 25th/869

## 8 · Dyslipidaemia & Hyperlipidaemia
<b>❓ Define dyslipidaemia, hyperlipidaemia, and hypercholesterolaemia.</b>

<b>Dyslipidaemia:</b> abnormalities in the lipid profile (any or all of TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C abnormal).

<b>Hyperlipidaemia:</b> excess lipid in blood (above normal range).

<b>Hypercholesterolaemia:</b> increased cholesterol level in blood above normal (<b>> 300 mg/dL</b>).

Arif Guide 2025 Vol-2 p.488

## 10 · Diagnostic Approach to Dyslipidaemia
<b>❓ How will you diagnose a case of dyslipidaemia? (History & physical)</b>

A. History

Obesity, Diabetes, Hypertension
Nephrotic syndrome
Occasional severe abdominal pain (history of pancreatitis — suggests severe hypertriglyceridaemia)
Family history of dyslipidaemia, Hypothyroidism
Drugs — OCP, steroid, thiazide, β-blocker; Alcohol intake; Dietary habits

B. Physical Examination

Corneal arcus — lipid deposit in cornea
Xanthelasma — yellow plaques around eyelids
Eruptive xanthoma — small yellow papules on skin (↑TG)
Tendon xanthoma — firm nodules over tendons (familial hypercholesterolaemia)

Ref: Davidson's 21st/451–456

<b>❓ What laboratory investigation is used to diagnose dyslipidaemia?</b>

Estimation of <b>serum lipid profile</b> (fasting sample).

Ref: Davidson's 21st/451–456

## 11 · Lipoprotein — Structure & Classification
<b>❓ What is a lipoprotein? Describe its structure.</b>

<b>Lipoprotein:</b> a complex lipid made up of <b>lipid + protein</b> (called <b>apolipoproteins</b> or <b>apoproteins</b>) — the transport vehicle for lipids in blood.

Structure

Lipid core: non-polar triglyceride (TAG) and cholesteryl ester.
Single surface layer: phospholipid, free cholesterol, and apoproteins. Phospholipid/cholesterol polar groups face outward → particle is water-soluble.

Constituents

TAG · Phospholipid · Free cholesterol · Cholesteryl ester · Apoprotein.

Ref: Lecture AFIP; Lippincott's 5th/227; Arif Guide 2025 Vol-2 pp.489–490

## 12 · Functions of Lipoproteins
<b>❓ What are the functions of each class of lipoprotein?</b>

Since lipids are <b>insoluble in water</b>, lipoproteins act as water-soluble transport vehicles.

<table>
<tr><th>Lipoprotein</th><th>Origin</th><th>Main Lipid</th><th>Function</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Chylomicrons</strong></td><td>Intestine</td><td>TAG</td><td>Transport <strong>exogenous</strong> (dietary) TAG from intestine to liver &amp; peripheral tissue</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>VLDL</strong></td><td>Liver</td><td>TAG</td><td>Transport <strong>endogenous</strong> TAG to peripheral tissue; <strong>precursor of LDL</strong></td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>LDL</strong></td><td>From VLDL in blood</td><td>Cholesterol</td><td>Transport cholesterol from <strong>liver → peripheral tissues</strong></td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>HDL</strong></td><td>Liver &amp; intestine</td><td>Phospholipids</td><td>Transport cholesterol from <strong>peripheral tissues → liver</strong> (reverse cholesterol transport)</td></tr>
</table>

Ref: Harper's 28th/213–215; Lippincott's 5th/227–228

<b>❓ What is the clinical importance of lipoprotein function?</b>

High LDL → increased cholesterol deposition in arterial wall → atherosclerosis.
High HDL → removes cholesterol from peripheral tissues → prevents atherosclerosis.
Imbalance in rate of TG formation in liver vs VLDL secretion → fatty liver.

Ref: Harper's 28th/213–215

## 15 · LDL — Low-Density Lipoprotein
<b>❓ Write a short note on LDL — what is it and its properties?</b>

<b>LDL</b> is one of the four major groups of lipoproteins, representing a <b>final stage in the catabolism of VLDL</b>.

<table>
<tr><th>Property</th><th>Detail</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Synonym</strong></td><td>β-lipoprotein</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Origin/source</strong></td><td>From VLDL (in plasma)</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Diameter</strong></td><td>20–25 nm</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Density</strong></td><td>1.019–1.063 g/mL</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Composition</strong></td><td>Protein 21%, Lipid 79%</td></tr>
</table>

<b>Lipid components:</b> high cholesterol, phospholipid & cholesteryl ester; low triacylglycerol (TAG).

Ref: Lippincott's 3rd/229–232; Harper's 28th/212–219

<b>❓ What is the function and clinical significance of LDL?</b>

LDL transports cholesterol from liver → peripheral tissues.
It may be responsible for atherosclerosis, which may lead to MI, stroke, peripheral arterial thrombosis → gangrene.
So, increased LDL is harmful to our body.

Ref: Lippincott's 3rd/229–232

## 16 · HDL — High-Density Lipoprotein
<b>❓ Write a short note on HDL — properties & subfractions.</b>

<b>HDL</b> is a lipoprotein having the <b>highest density</b>.

<table>
<tr><th>Property</th><th>Detail</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Synonym</strong></td><td>α-lipoprotein</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Origin</strong></td><td>Liver &amp; intestine</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Main lipid component</strong></td><td>Phospholipid</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Apoproteins</strong></td><td>A, C, E, A-I</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Blood level</strong></td><td>&gt; 40 mg/dL</td></tr>
</table>

Subfractions

HDL₂ — Cholesterol
HDL₃ — Cholesteryl ester
Pre-β-HDL — TAG

Ref: Harper's 29th/242; Lippincott's 5th/234–237

<b>❓ Why is HDL called "good cholesterol"?</b>

HDL transports cholesterol from peripheral tissues → liver (Reverse Cholesterol Transporter).
In the liver, cholesterol is repackaged into lipoproteins, converted into bile acids, or secreted into bile for removal.
More HDL-C = less cholesterol deposition in peripheral tissue = less atherosclerosis.
HDL is therefore anti-atherogenic = "good cholesterol".

Ref: Harper's 29th/242; Lippincott's 5th/234–237

<b>❓ What is the clinical significance of HDL levels?</b>

A high level of HDL-C seems to protect against cardiovascular diseases.
Low HDL-C (< 40 mg/dL) increases the risk for heart disease.
HDL is considered a protection to cardiovascular health.

Ref: Harper's 29th/242

<b>❓ What is good and bad cholesterol? Why are they called so?</b>

<b>HDL is "good cholesterol"</b> because it removes cholesterol from peripheral tissues to the liver for metabolism/excretion → reduces tissue atherogenicity.

<b>LDL is "bad cholesterol"</b> because it carries cholesterol from the liver to peripheral tissues → <b>increases tissue atherogenicity</b>.

Ref: Davidson's 21st/451

## 🔁 Rapid-Fire One-liners (test yourself, then reveal)
<b>Lipid & Cholesterol — one-liners & mnemonics</b>

🎯 Increased LDL vs HDL relation to CVD?

<b>LDL = directly related;</b> HDL = <b>inversely related</b> to CVD.

🎯 Synonyms: LDL · HDL?

LDL = <b>β-lipoprotein</b> · HDL = <b>α-lipoprotein</b>.

🎯 HDL synthesised where?

<b>Liver and intestine</b> (not liver alone).

🎯 Chylomicrons transport which TAG? VLDL?

Chylomicrons = <b>exogenous (intestinal)</b> TAG · VLDL = <b>endogenous (liver)</b> TAG.

🎯 Is VLDL a precursor of LDL?

<b>Yes</b> — VLDL → IDL → LDL.

🎯 % of total plasma lipid that is triacylglycerol? Phospholipid?

TAG <b>45%</b> (largest) · Phospholipid <b>35%</b>.

🎯 Clinical signs of hyperlipidaemia?

<b>Corneal arcus, xanthelasma, eruptive xanthoma, tendon xanthoma.</b>

🎯 Total lipid normal range?

<b>450–1000 mg/dL.</b>
