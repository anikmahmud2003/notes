# Ch 10 — Female Genital System     <i>Full-detail flashcards (Q→A) · All abbreviations expanded · Robbins 4-pillar answers</i>
### 🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of the Female Genital System
The female genital tract spans <b>vulva → vagina → cervix → uterus (endometrium + myometrium) → fallopian tubes → ovaries</b>. Disease follows a logical anatomical progression:
🔴 <b>Infections</b> (lower tract: HSV, HPV; PID: gonorrhoea/chlamydia) → <b>Vulvar disorders</b> (lichen sclerosus, VIN, vulvar carcinoma, extramammary Paget disease) → <b>Vaginal lesions</b> (VAIN, rhabdomyosarcoma) → <b>Cervix</b> (CIN I→II→III → invasive carcinoma, almost always HPV-driven) → <b>Endometrium</b> (AUB/DUB, endometriosis, adenomyosis, endometrial hyperplasia → endometrial carcinoma) → <b>Myometrium</b> (leiomyoma = "fibroid," leiomyosarcoma) → <b>Ovaries</b> (WHO classification: surface epithelial, germ cell, sex-cord stromal, metastatic tumours; Krukenberg tumour, Meigs syndrome).
📌 Key themes: <b>HPV = oncogenic driver</b> of cervical (and vaginal/vulvar) cancer; <b>unopposed oestrogen</b> drives endometrial hyperplasia/cancer; <b>leiomyoma</b> is the most common gynaecological tumour; <b>ovarian germ cell tumours</b> dominate in young women; <b>Krukenberg</b> = bilateral GI metastases with signet-ring cells.
Robbins 10e, Ch 23 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1028 · Arif Guide 2025 Vol-2, pp.264–291

## 1 · Infections of the Female Genital Tract
<b>What are the major infections of the female genital tract? Mention their clinical significance.</b>

<b>Bacteria</b> — <i>Neisseria gonorrhoeae</i> and <i>Chlamydia trachomatis</i> are the major causes of female infertility. <i>Streptococcus</i> infections are implicated in preterm deliveries, stillbirths, and neonatal sepsis.

<b>Viruses</b> — especially <b>herpes simplex virus (HSV)</b> and <b>human papillomavirus (HPV)</b>, account for considerable morbidity; HSVs cause painful genital ulcerations, whereas HPVs are involved in the pathogenesis of cervical, vaginal, and vulvar cancers. <b>Molluscum contagiosum</b> is a cutaneous or mucosal lesion caused by poxvirus.

Robbins 10e pp.986–988

## 2 · Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)
<b>🔴 Define PID. Write down its etiology, pathogenesis, and complications.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> PID is an infection that begins in the vulva or vagina and spreads upward to involve most of the structures in the female genital system, resulting in <b>pelvic pain, adnexal tenderness, fever, and vaginal discharge</b>.

Etiology

Gonococcus infection (most common cause)
Chlamydia infection
Staphylococci
Streptococci
Coliform bacteria
Clostridium perfringens

Pathogenesis

Gonococcal PID: inflammatory changes appear 2–7 days after inoculation. Endocervical mucosa is the most common site of initial involvement. From here, organisms may spread upward to involve the fallopian tubes and tubo-ovarian region.
Non-gonococcal PID: bacterial infections that follow induced abortion, dilation and curettage (D&C), and other surgical procedures spread upward from the uterus through the lymphatics and venous channels rather than on mucosal surfaces. These infections tend to produce more inflammation within the deeper layers of the organs than gonococcal infections.

Complications

Acute: peritonitis; bacteraemia (which in turn may result in endocarditis, meningitis, and suppurative arthritis).
Chronic sequelae: infertility and tubal obstruction; ectopic pregnancy; pelvic pain; intestinal obstruction (due to adhesions between the small bowel and the pelvic organs).

Robbins 10e pp.988–989

## 6 · Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CIN)
<b>🔴 What is CIN? Describe its characteristics, classifications, and morphology.</b>

<b>Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN):</b> a spectrum of precancerous changes in cervical epithelium, graded on progressively severe degree of changes. It includes dysplasia and carcinoma in situ (CIS).

Characteristics

They represent a continuum of morphologic change with indistinct boundaries.
They do not invariably progress to cancer and may spontaneously regress, with the risk of persistence or progression increasing with the severity of the precancerous change.
They are associated with human papillomavirus (HPV), and high-risk HPV (types 16, 18 & above) are found in increasing frequency in high-grade CIN.
Progression to invasive carcinoma (when it occurs) may take place in a few months to more than a decade.

Classification systems

<b>A. CIN system (based on extent of epithelial involvement):</b>

CIN-I: Mild dysplasia
CIN-II: Moderate dysplasia
CIN-III: Severe dysplasia (carcinoma in situ)

<b>B. Dysplasia / CIS system:</b>

Mild dysplasia
Severe dysplasia (carcinoma in situ)

<b>C. Squamous intraepithelial lesion (SIL) — two-tier system (current):</b>

Low-grade SIL (LSIL): CIN-I (mild dysplasia)
High-grade SIL (HSIL): CIN-II & CIN-III (moderate & severe dysplasia, carcinoma in situ)

Morphology — important features

Cellular immaturity
Cellular disorganization
Nuclear abnormalities
Increased mitotic activity

## 7 · Carcinoma of the Cervix
<b>🔴 Describe the etiology/risk factors and pathogenesis of carcinoma of the cervix.</b>

Risk factors / etiologic factors

Early age at first intercourse
Multiple sexual partners
Male partner with multiple previous sexual partners
Early first pregnancy
Multiparity
Persistent infection with high-risk HPV (types 16, 18 and above) — single most important risk factor
Sexually transmitted diseases (STDs)
Cigarette smoking
Oral contraceptive pill (OCP)
Immunosuppression
Low socio-economic status

Pathogenesis

<b>Sexual activity → HPV exposure → Cervical transformation zone</b> (where immature squamous metaplastic epithelial cells are most susceptible to HPV infection).

High- & low-risk HPVs → Low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL).
High-risk HPVs (type 16, 18 & above) → High-grade SIL (HSIL).
Additional co-factors: smoking, oral contraceptives, high parity, immune status, host gene alterations, time → Invasive squamous carcinoma / Invasive adenocarcinoma.

<b>HPV oncoproteins:</b> High-risk HPV produces <b>E6 & E7 proteins</b> which result in increased destruction of <b>p53</b> and <b>Rb</b>, respectively. Loss of these tumour suppressor proteins increases the risk for CIN.

Persistent infection increases the risk of cervical precancer and subsequent invasive carcinoma. Duration: high-oncogenic-risk HPVs persist longer (average 13 months) vs low-risk HPVs (8 months). High-risk HPVs are also detected in vaginal SCC and in a subset of vulvar, penile, anal, tonsillar, and other oropharyngeal cancers.

Robbins 10e pp.996, 998; Pathoma 2014 p.139

<b>Short note: Transformation zone of the cervix.</b>

Anatomically, the cervix is divided into the <b>exocervix</b> (visible on vaginal exam, lined by squamous epithelium) and the <b>endocervix</b> (lined by columnar mucus-secreting epithelium).

The point where the squamous and columnar epithelium meets is the <b>squamocolumnar junction (SCJ)</b>.

With the onset of puberty, the SCJ undergoes eversion, causing the columnar epithelium to become visible on the exocervix. The exposed columnar cells eventually undergo <b>squamous metaplasia</b>, forming a region called the <b>transformation zone</b>, where tumours most commonly arise.

<b>Importance:</b> Most tumours of the cervix are of epithelial origin and are caused by oncogenic strains of HPV. Immature squamous metaplastic epithelial cells in the transformation zone are most susceptible to HPV infection — this is where cervical precursor lesions and cancers develop.

Robbins 10e p.995; Pathoma 2011

<b>🔴 Give the morphology and spread of cervical carcinoma. What are its complications?</b>

<b>General:</b> one of the most common neoplasms in women; average age 45–50 years. Widespread screening (Pap test) has reduced mortality sharply.

Morphology

Invasive cervical carcinoma may manifest as <b>fungating (exophytic) or infiltrative masses</b>. Histologically:

Squamous cell carcinoma: nests and tongues of malignant squamous epithelium, either keratinizing or non-keratinizing, invading the cervical stroma.
Adenocarcinoma: glandular epithelium composed of malignant endocervical cells with large hyperchromatic nuclei and relatively mucin-depleted cytoplasm → dark-appearing glands.
Adenosquamous carcinoma: intermixed malignant glandular and squamous epithelium.
Neuroendocrine carcinoma: appearance similar to small-cell carcinoma of the lung, positive for high-risk HPVs.

Spread

Direct: local extension into paracervical soft tissue, urinary bladder, ureters (→ hydronephrosis), rectum, and vagina.
Lymphatic: lymphovascular invasion → local and distant lymph node metastases.
Blood-borne: liver, lungs, bone or bone marrow.

Complications

Local extension → ureteral obstruction → pyelonephritis → uraemia.
Distant metastasis.

<b>🔴 How is carcinoma cervix diagnosed? How can it be prevented?</b>

Diagnosis

<b>1. History:</b> unexplained vaginal bleeding (especially post-coital), foul-smelling vaginal discharge, painful coitus (dyspareunia), pelvic pain, weight loss, obstructive uropathy.

<b>2. Clinical examination:</b> cervix is fixed, hard, irregular, friable, bleeds on touch.

<b>3. Investigations:</b>

Visual inspection by acetic acid (VIA)
HPV DNA testing by polymerase chain reaction (PCR)
Pap's smear
Colposcopy and colposcopy-guided biopsy followed by histopathology

Prevention

<b>1. Primary prevention:</b>

Avoidance of risk factors (early marriage, multiple sex partners, smoking).
Counselling / health education for safe sexual behaviour (e.g. condom use).
Prophylactic HPV vaccine (against HPV types 6, 11, 16, and 18) for all school girls (12–18 years) and women (16–25 years), dose schedule 0-2-6 months.

<b>2. Secondary prevention:</b> screening & early detection (begin at age 21 years):

Pap's smear / Pap's test
HPV DNA testing by PCR
Visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA)
Colposcopy

<b>3. Treatment</b> of the pre-malignant lesions.

Robbins 10e p.1000; DeVita 11e p.2090

## 8 · Pap Test
<b>🔴 Short note: Pap test / Pap smear — procedure, schedule, Papanicolaou grading, and importance.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> cytologic preparations of exfoliated cells from the cervical transformation zone stained with the Papanicolaou method. It is a screening procedure for cervical cancer that tests for pre-cancerous or cancerous cells on the cervix.

Procedure

Collection: using a spatula or cytobrush, the transformation zone is circumferentially scraped.
Smearing: specimen smeared on clean glass slides.
Fixation: by 95% alcohol.
Staining & examination: examined under microscope by a cytotechnologist.

Schedule

First smear at 21 years of age or within 3 years of onset of sexual activity, then every 3 years.
After 30: normal cytology + HPV-negative → every 5 years.
Normal cytology but HPV-positive → repeat cytology every 6–12 months.

Papanicolaou grading

<table>
<tr><th>Class</th><th>Meaning</th></tr>
<tr><td>I</td><td>Normal</td></tr>
<tr><td>II</td><td>Inflammatory atypia (infection)</td></tr>
<tr><td>III</td><td>Mild dysplasia (CIN-I), moderate dysplasia (CIN-II)</td></tr>
<tr><td>IV</td><td>Severe dysplasia / carcinoma in situ (CIN-III)</td></tr>
<tr><td>V</td><td>Invasive squamous carcinoma</td></tr>
</table>

<b>Importance:</b> main screening tool for cervical diseases, especially carcinoma cervix. False-negative error rate ~10–20%.

Robbins 10e pp.1000–1001; DeVita 11e p.2094; Manual of Clinical Oncology 7e pp.323–324

<b>What is DUB? What is an anovulatory cycle and its causes?</b>

<b>Dysfunctional uterine bleeding (DUB):</b> uterine bleeding that lacks an underlying structural abnormality — excessive bleeding during or between menstrual periods without a causative uterine lesion (tumour, polyp, infection, hyperplasia, trauma, blood dyscrasia, or pregnancy).

<b>Common causes of DUB:</b> (1) Anovulatory cycle, (2) Ovulatory dysfunctional bleeding (inadequate luteal phase), (3) Hormonal contraceptives, (4) Irregular shedding.

<b>Anovulatory cycle:</b> a menstrual cycle with no ovulation. It is the most frequent cause of DUB during menarche and menopause. Failure of ovulation → prolonged and excessive oestrogenic stimulation without a progestational (luteal) phase.

Causes of anovulation

Endocrine disorders: thyroid disease, adrenal disease, pituitary tumours.
Ovarian lesions: functioning ovarian tumours (granulosa cell tumours), polycystic ovaries.
Generalised metabolic disturbances: obesity, malnutrition, chronic systemic diseases.

Robbins 10e p.1003; Harsh Mohan 7e p.721

## 11 · Endometriosis & Chocolate Cyst
<b>🔴 Define endometriosis. Give its common sites, pathogenesis, clinical features, and complications.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> presence of <b>"ectopic" endometrial tissue</b> (both glands and stroma, or stroma alone) at a site outside of the uterus. Affects approx. 6–10% of women, principally in the <b>3rd and 4th decades</b>.

Common sites (descending order)

Ovaries (most common)
Uterine ligaments
Rectovaginal septum
Cul-de-sac
Pelvic peritoneum
Large and small bowel and appendix
Mucosa of the cervix, vagina, and fallopian tubes
Laparotomy scars

Pathogenesis (major theories)

Regurgitation theory: retrograde menstruation through fallopian tubes → endometrial tissue implanted at abnormal locations.
Benign metastases theory: endometrial tissue spreads to distant sites (bone, lung, brain) via blood and lymphatics.
Metaplastic theory: ectopic endometrium develops in situ from local tissues by metaplasia of the coelomic epithelium (mesothelium).
Extrauterine stem/progenitor cell theory: stem/progenitor cells from bone marrow differentiate into endometrial tissue.

Clinical features / complications

Severe dysmenorrhoea (painful menstruation)
Dyspareunia (pain with intercourse)
Pelvic pain (due to intrapelvic bleeding and periuterine adhesions)
Pain on defecation (rectal wall involvement)
Dysuria (bladder wall involvement)
Menstrual irregularities
Infertility (30–40%) — involvement of fallopian tube mucosa
Development of malignancy (endometrioid and clear cell carcinoma) — uncommon

Robbins 10e pp.1004–1006; Harsh Mohan 7e p.722

<b>🔴 What is a chocolate cyst? What is pseudocyesis?</b>

<b>Chocolate cyst of the ovary (endometrioma):</b> endometriotic lesions bleed periodically in response to cyclic ovarian hormonal stimulation → nodules with red-blue to yellow-brown appearance. Ovaries may become markedly distorted by large cystic masses (3–5 cm) filled with brown fluid from previous haemorrhage → called <b>chocolate cysts</b> or endometriomas. Organising haemorrhage causes extensive fibrous adhesions between tubes, ovaries, and other structures, obliterating the pouch of Douglas.

<b>Pseudocyesis (false pregnancy):</b> the appearance of clinical or subclinical signs and symptoms associated with pregnancy although the individual is not physically carrying a baby.

Robbins 10e p.1006

## 12 · Adenomyosis
<b>🔴 Define adenomyosis. Give its diagnosis, clinical features, and complications.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> presence of <b>endometrial tissue within the uterine wall (myometrium)</b>. Occurs in up to 20% of uteri.

<b>Diagnosis (microscopic):</b> irregular nests of endometrial stroma, with or without glands, within the myometrium, separated from the basalis by at least 2–3 mm.

Clinical features / complications

Menometrorrhagia (irregular and heavy menses)
Colicky dysmenorrhoea
Dyspareunia
Pelvic pain (particularly during menstruation)

<b>Note:</b> adenomyosis can coexist with endometriosis.

Robbins 10e p.1006

## 13 · Endometrial Hyperplasia
<b>🔴 Write the pathology of endometrial hyperplasia. Differentiate non-atypical from atypical hyperplasia.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> abnormal proliferation of endometrial glands relative to the stroma, resulting in an increased gland-to-stroma ratio compared with normal proliferative endometrium.

Cause — prolonged oestrogenic stimulation due to:

Anovulatory cycles
Obesity (peripheral conversion of androgens to oestrogens)
Menopause
Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS)
Functioning granulosa cell tumours of the ovary
Excessive ovarian cortical function (cortical stromal hyperplasia)
Prolonged oestrogen replacement therapy

<b>Presentation:</b> classic presentation is <b>postmenopausal uterine bleeding</b>.

WHO classification (two major categories)

## 14 · Carcinoma of the Endometrium
<b>🔴 What are the risk factors for endometrial carcinoma? How is it diagnosed?</b>

<b>Nice to Know:</b> endometrial carcinoma is the <b>most common invasive cancer of the female genital tract</b>. Peak incidence in <b>55–65 years</b>. Usually produces irregular or postmenopausal vaginal bleeding with excessive leukorrhoea.

Risk factors

Age >40 years (peak 55–65)
Obesity
Diabetes
Hypertension
Infertility
Unopposed oestrogen stimulation
Endometrial hyperplasia

Investigations to confirm diagnosis

Transvaginal ultrasound
Endometrial biopsy → histopathology
Dilatation & curettage (D&C) — if biopsy is non-diagnostic
Pap test (limited sensitivity — as low as 40%)

## 15 · Leiomyoma (Fibroid Uterus)
<b>🔴 Describe the pathology/morphology and complications of leiomyoma of the uterus.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> benign smooth-muscle neoplasms of the uterus — perhaps the <b>most common tumours in women</b>. Present in about <b>75% of females</b> of reproductive age. May occur singly or more often are <b>multiple</b>.

Morphology — Gross

Sharply circumscribed, discrete, round, firm, grey-white tumour of varying sizes from small nodules to massive tumours filling the pelvis.
Found within the myometrium of the corpus.
Locations: intramural (within myometrium), submucosal (beneath endometrium), subserosal (beneath serosa).
Cut section: characteristic whorled pattern.
Large tumours may develop areas of yellow-brown to red softening (red degeneration).

Morphology — Microscopic

Whorled bundles of smooth muscle cells admixed with variable connective tissue.
Individual SMCs: uniform size/shape, abundant cytoplasm, central oval nuclei.
Mitotic figures are scarce.

Complications

Abnormal uterine bleeding
Urinary frequency (bladder compression)
Sudden pain (infarction of large or pedunculated tumour)
Impaired fertility
In pregnant women: spontaneous abortion, fetal malpresentation, uterine inertia, postpartum haemorrhage
Malignant transformation to leiomyosarcoma — extremely rare

<b>Other sites of leiomyoma:</b> arrector pili (pilar leiomyoma — very painful), nipples, scrotum, labia, and less frequently deep soft tissue and gut wall.

Robbins 10e p.1014

## 16 · Ovarian Tumours — WHO Classification
<b>🔴 Classify ovarian tumours (WHO classification).</b>

A. Surface epithelial tumours (~<sup>3</sup>/<sub>4</sub> of ovarian tumours)

<i>Each sub-type has benign, borderline, and malignant variants:</i>

Serous tumours: serous cystadenoma (benign), serous borderline tumour, serous cystadenocarcinoma (malignant) — 50% of ovarian carcinomas
Mucinous tumours (endocervical-like & intestinal type)
Endometrioid tumours
Clear cell tumours
Brenner tumours (transitional cell tumours)

B. Germ cell tumours

<i>Nearly 95% benign; chiefly in females <20 years.</i>

Dysgerminoma (ovarian counterpart of testicular seminoma)
Teratoma: benign (mature/cystic — dermoid cyst), malignant (immature), monodermal/specialized (struma ovarii, carcinoid)
Endodermal sinus (yolk sac) tumour
Choriocarcinoma
Others (embryonal carcinoma, polyembryoma, mixed germ cell tumours)

C. Sex-cord stromal tumours

Granulosa-theca cell tumours: granulosa cell tumours, fibromas, thecomas, fibrothecomas
Sertoli-Leydig cell tumours (androblastoma, arrhenoblastoma)
Steroid (lipid) cell tumours

D. Malignant, not otherwise specified

E. Metatic cancer from non-ovarian primary

Krukenberg tumour & others: colonic, appendiceal, gastric, pancreaticobiliary, breast

<b>Nice to Know:</b>

Benign tumours: well-differentiated epithelial cells, minimal proliferation.
Borderline tumours: increased cell proliferation, no stromal invasion.
Malignant tumours: increased epithelial atypia + stromal invasion.

Robbins 10e p.1017; BRS Pathology 5e pp.304–307; Harsh Mohan 7e p.731

<b>🔴 Classify ovarian cysts (non-neoplastic and neoplastic).</b>

1. Non-neoplastic and functional cysts

Follicular cysts
Luteal cysts
Chocolate cyst (ovarian endometrioma)
Polycystic ovarian disease / PCOS (Stein-Leventhal syndrome)

2. Neoplastic cysts

Surface epithelial tumours: serous or mucinous cystadenoma; serous or mucinous cystadenocarcinoma
Germ cell tumours: mature cystic teratoma (dermoid cyst)

Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS)

A complex endocrine disorder characterised by <b>hyperandrogenism, menstrual abnormalities, polycystic ovaries, chronic anovulation, and decreased fertility</b>. Associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and premature atherosclerosis.

<b>Pathophysiology:</b> excessive androgen production and low FSH levels. Testosterone inhibits FSH, but LH levels are sufficient to cause luteinisation of theca and granulosa cells → androgen secretion → anovulation. Hereditary basis suggested in some cases.

Robbins 10e p.1016; Harsh Mohan 7e p.730

## 18 · Dermoid Cyst (Benign Cystic Teratoma)
<b>🔴 Short note: Dermoid cyst of the ovary — classification, morphology, and complications.</b>

<b>Ovarian teratoma:</b> tumours composed of tissues derived from all three germ cell layers — ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm — in varying combinations.

Types of teratoma

Mature (benign) teratoma — most are cystic, known as dermoid cysts
Immature (malignant) teratoma — rare, chiefly in pre-pubertal adolescents and young women <20; predominantly solid; contain immature/embryonal structures
Monodermal / highly specialized teratoma — struma ovarii, carcinoid tumour

<b>Benign cystic teratoma (dermoid cyst):</b> accounts for ~<b>20% of ovarian tumours</b> and <b>90% of germ cell tumours</b>. Most frequent benign ovarian tumour, usually in young women during reproductive life.

Morphology

<b>Gross:</b>

Bilateral in 10–15% of cases
Unilocular cysts containing hair & cheesy sebaceous material
Cyst wall: thin, opaque grey-white, wrinkled, apparent epidermis with protruding hair shafts
Tooth structures & areas of calcification within the wall

<b>Microscopic:</b>

Cyst wall lined by skin, including hair follicles & skin appendages
Structures from other germ layers: cartilage, bone, GI, neural, respiratory, and thyroid gland tissue

Components

Skin lining with hair follicles, sebaceous glands, sweat glands
Bone, tooth, cartilage, smooth muscle, GI, pancreatic, neural, retinal, respiratory, salivary gland, and thyroid gland tissues

Chance of malignancy (~1%)

Squamous cell carcinoma (most common)
Thyroid carcinoma
Melanoma

Struma ovarii

A rare monodermal teratoma containing mostly thyroid tissue (>50% of overall tissue). ~5% of all ovarian teratomas. May cause hyperthyroidism. Most are benign; malignant in a small percentage.

Robbins 10e pp.1023–1024; BRS Pathology 5e p.306; Harsh Mohan 7e pp.736–737

## 19 · Dysgerminoma
<b>🔴 Give the morphology of dysgerminoma of the ovary.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> germ cell tumour of the ovary — the ovarian counterpart of testicular seminoma. ~2% of all ovarian cancers; ~half of malignant germ cell tumours. About 10% have elevated <b>β-hCG</b> in plasma. <b>All dysgerminomas are malignant and are extremely radiosensitive.</b>

<b>Age:</b> 75% occur in the 2nd & 3rd decades.

Morphology

<b>Gross:</b>

Usually unilateral (80–90%)
Solid tumours, varying in size from small nodules to masses filling the entire abdomen
Cut surface: yellow-white to grey-pink, soft & fleshy with haemorrhage and necrosis foci

<b>Microscopic:</b>

Tumour cells arranged in diffuse sheets, islands, and cords separated by scanty fibrous stroma
Fibrous stroma infiltrated with mature lymphocytes & occasional granulomas
May be found in the wall of an otherwise benign cystic teratoma; conversely, a predominantly dysgerminomatous tumour may contain a small cystic teratoma

Robbins 10e p.1024; Harsh Mohan 7e p.737

<b>What are the hormone-producing tumours of the ovary?</b>

Granulosa-theca cell tumours: secrete oestrogen
Sertoli-Leydig cell tumours (androblastoma, arrhenoblastoma): secrete androgen
Choriocarcinoma: secretes human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)
Others: struma ovarii, carcinoid, hilar cell tumour

Robbins 10e pp.1016–1028; BRS Pathology 5e p.307

## 20 · Krukenberg Tumour
<b>🔴 Short note: Krukenberg tumour.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> a classic metastatic gastrointestinal carcinoma involving the ovaries. Characterised by <b>bilateral metastases</b> composed of <b>mucin-producing cancer cells with a "signet-ring" appearance</b>, most often of <b>gastric origin</b>. May also arise from breast, lung, etc.

Origins of metastatic ovarian tumours

Müllerian origin: uterus, fallopian tube, contralateral ovary, pelvic peritoneum.
Extra-Müllerian origin: breast, GI tract (colon, stomach, biliary tract, pancreas), pseudomyxoma peritonei from appendiceal tumours.

Robbins 10e p.1028; BRS Pathology 5e p.307; Harsh Mohan 7e p.1034

## 21 · Meigs Syndrome
<b>🔴 Short note: Meigs syndrome.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> the triad of <b>ovarian fibroma + ascites + hydrothorax</b> (usually only right side). Genesis is unknown.

<b>Key feature:</b> ascites and pleural effusion <b>resolve spontaneously</b> when the ovarian tumour is removed.

Robbins 10e p.1027; BRS Pathology 5e p.306
