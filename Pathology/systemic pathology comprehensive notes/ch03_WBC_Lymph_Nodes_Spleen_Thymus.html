🧠 Big Picture — WBC, Lymph Nodes, Spleen & Thymus (boss-level overview)

This chapter is a lymphoreticular "roadmap". It pivots on lymph node enlargement (lymphadenopathy) and how to walk it up to a diagnosis — from simple infection to lymphoma.

The central axis: Lymphadenopathy (generalised vs localised) → when it is neoplastic, we meet LYMPHOMA → divide it by its two great families:

🔴 Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) — has the Reed-Sternberg (RS) cell as its hallmark · monomorphic? No — polymorphous background · contiguous spread · extranodal involvement rare · high cure rate.

🔴 Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) — lacks RS cells · monomorphic B-cell (85%) / T–NK-cell (15%) clone · non-contiguous spread · extranodal common (GIT, stomach, Waldeyer's ring) · lower cure rate.

📌 Then branch out: Spleen (functions, splenomegaly, hypersplenism, massive splenomegaly from chronic myeloid leukaemia CML / myelofibrosis / lymphoma) and Thymus (DiGeorge syndrome, follicular/thymic hyperplasia, thymoma = epithelial tumour).

🎯 HL = RS cells + contiguous + curable · NHL = no RS + non-contiguous + incurable/low-cure. HL 👨 young adult, ♂ twice ♀; NS (nodular sclerosis) subtype favours young ♀.

1 · Lymphadenopathy — Generalized

🔴 Define generalized lymphadenopathy and write down its causes. (CU-08Ja, DU-12/07Ja, RU-20/18Nv)

Generalized lymphadenopathy: when 2 or more groups of lymph nodes are enlarged, the condition is called generalized lymphadenopathy.

Causes of generalized lymphadenopathy (important for VIVA)

1. Infection:

  • Viral: Infectious mononucleosis (Epstein–Barr virus / EBV), Cytomegalovirus (CMV), AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency syndrome — HIV)
  • Bacterial: Disseminated tuberculosis, brucellosis, cat scratch disease
  • Spirochaetal: Secondary syphilis
  • Protozoal: Toxoplasmosis
  • Helminthic: Filariasis
  • Fungal: Histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis

2. Malignant disease: Lymphoma · Leukaemia

3. Collagen disease: Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) · Rheumatoid arthritis

4. Miscellaneous: Sarcoidosis · Thyrotoxicosis · Drugs (e.g., phenytoin)

Ref: Medicine Lecture SSMC; de Gruchy/5ᵉ/280

2 · Cervical Lymphadenopathy

What are the causes of cervical lymphadenopathy? A 13-year-old boy came with cervical lymphadenopathy — what is your differential diagnosis? (DU-05M, RU-03M, RU-10Ju)

Causes of cervical (neck) lymphadenopathy

1. Inflammation: Tonsillitis · Pharyngitis · Sinusitis

2. Infections: Tuberculosis · Secondary syphilis · Toxoplasmosis · Filariasis · Infectious mononucleosis

3. Malignant disease: Lymphoma · Leukaemia · Secondary deposits (from nasopharynx, larynx, lungs, thyroid, breast, stomach)

4. Miscellaneous: Sarcoidosis

Ref: Medicine Lecture SSMC & DMC

3 · Approach to Diagnosing Lymphadenopathy

Give your approach to diagnose a case of lymphadenopathy. (RU-13Ju)

Examination

  1. Site
  2. Number (single or multiple)
  3. Size
  4. Consistency:
    • Rubbery → Lymphoma
    • Firm & matted → TB (tuberculosis)
    • Hard → Malignancy
    • Stony hard → calcified lymph node
    • Soft, cystic → cold abscess
  5. Tenderness (suggests acute inflammation)
  6. Discrete or matted (matted in TB)
  7. Fixation to underlying structure or overlying skin (in malignancy)
  8. Skin (sinus, ulcer, signs of acute inflammation)

Investigation

  1. CBC (complete blood count), ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate), PBF (peripheral blood film) — to exclude leukaemia, ↑ eosinophilia in lymphoma, atypical lymphocyte in infectious mononucleosis, and high ESR in TB.
  2. Chest X-ray — TB, BHL (bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy) in lymphoma and lymphatic leukaemia.
  3. If TB suspected → perform tuberculin test.
  4. FNAC (fine needle aspiration cytology) or biopsy of LN (biopsy is preferable).
  5. Bone marrow biopsy (leukaemia and lymphoma).
  6. Other investigations (according to suspicion of cause): USG (ultrasonography) of abdomen — to observe hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, para-aortic and other lymphadenopathy; ANA (antinuclear antibody) in SLE (systemic lupus erythematosus).

4 · Tumours of Lymph Node (Lymphoma)

🔴 Classify tumours of lymph node. (CU-04Ja)

Classification of tumours of lymph node

1. B-cell neoplasms

  • Precursor B-cell neoplasm (neoplasm of immature B-cell)
  • Mature (peripheral) B-cell neoplasm (neoplasm of mature B-cell)

2. T-cell neoplasms

  • Precursor T-cell neoplasm (neoplasm of immature T-cell)
  • Mature (peripheral) T/NK-cell neoplasm (neoplasm of mature T/NK-cell)

3. Hodgkin lymphoma / Hodgkin disease (neoplasms of Reed-Sternberg cells and variants)

  • A. Classical Hodgkin lymphoma: Nodular sclerosis · Mixed cellularity · Lymphocyte rich · Lymphocyte depleted
  • B. Non-classical Hodgkin lymphoma: Lymphocyte predominant

Ref: Robbins/10ᵉ/592; BRS Pathology/5ᵉ/180

🔴 Define lymphoma / malignant lymphoma and classify it. (RMU-24M/22Nv/14/12Ju, CU-14Ja/11Ju/08Ja, RU-21/20M/17Ja)

Lymphoma: the neoplastic proliferation of cells native to the lymph nodes. The cells are lymphocytes and their precursors and histiocytes (histiocyte derived from monocyte).

Classification of lymphoma

1. Hodgkin lymphoma (HL): malignant neoplasm of lymph node characterized by the presence of neoplastic giant cells called Reed-Sternberg (RS) cells and pleomorphic infiltration by lymphocyte, eosinophil, macrophage, plasma cell, neutrophil and follicular dendritic cells.

WHO (World Health Organization) classification of Hodgkin lymphoma:

  • A. Classical Hodgkin lymphoma: Nodular sclerosis · Mixed cellularity · Lymphocyte rich · Lymphocyte depleted
  • B. Non-classical Hodgkin lymphoma (classic RS cells absent or rare): Lymphocyte predominant

2. Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL): malignant neoplasm of lymph node characterized by monomorphic infiltration and absence of RS (Reed-Sternberg) cells.

Classification of Non-Hodgkin lymphoma

  • A. REAL classification (Revised European-American classification of Lymphoid neoplasms) — see below.
  • B. Working classification — see below.

5 · Reed-Sternberg (RS) Cell

🔴 What is an R-S cell? Describe a typical RS cell, its variants/types, and their morphology. (DU-18/17/14/15/23/22/21/20/11/09/08/06, RMU-23/24M, CU-13/10/14Ja, RU-20/19/17/16/12/07/09Ju, SUST-21Nv, CMU-21M)

Reed-Sternberg (R-S) cells: the malignant lymphoid cells of B-cell origin which is the hallmark of Hodgkin's disease. They are large tumour giant cells (~45 μm in diameter).

Classic (typical) RS cell — large tumour giant cell (45 μm)

  • Bi-lobed nucleus, with two halves often appearing as mirror images of each other.
  • Each nucleus contains a large inclusion-like nucleolus surrounded by a clear zone (halo) imparting an "owl-eye" appearance.
  • The nucleus is surrounded by non-malignant T-lymphocytes, plasma cells and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm.

RS cells may also be present in some other conditions:

  • Infectious mononucleosis
  • Non-Hodgkin lymphoma
  • CLL (chronic lymphocytic leukaemia)

Several variants of RS cells

  1. Mononuclear variants: contain a single nucleus with a large inclusion-like nucleolus.
  2. Lacunar cells: have a single multilobate nucleus, multiple small nucleoli, and abundant, pale-staining cytoplasm. In sections of formalin-fixed tissue, the cytoplasm is often torn away, leaving the nucleus lying in an empty space (a lacune). Characteristic of nodular sclerosis HL.
  3. Lympho-histiocytic (L&H) variants ("popcorn cells"): have a delicate multilobed, puffy nucleus with abundant pale cytoplasm resembling popped corn ("popcorn cell"). Found in lymphocyte-predominant Hodgkin lymphoma.

Ref: Robbins/10ᵉ/612; Basic Hematology, Dr. Mainuddin/71; BRS Pathology/5ᵉ/179

6 · Hodgkin's Lymphoma (HL / Hodgkin Disease)

🔴 What is Hodgkin's lymphoma? Give its criteria, clinical features, WHO classification and morphology. (DU-15/13/11/19/17/14/09, RMU-19Nv/24M, SU-19Nv/22/20/11/10/09/06/07/01, CU-13/10/16/15, RU-18/10/09, CMU-24M, BSFMMU'S-23Nv)

Hodgkin lymphoma: malignant neoplasm of lymph node characterized by the presence of neoplastic giant cells called Reed-Sternberg cells (RS cells) and pleomorphic infiltration by lymphocyte, eosinophil, macrophage, plasma cell, neutrophil and follicular dendritic cells.

Criteria

  • Characteristically affects young adults (20–40 age group).
  • Males affected twice as commonly as females.
  • Arises in a single node or group of nodes and spreads first to anatomically contiguous lymphoid tissues.
  • Histological hallmark is presence of RS (Reed-Sternberg) cells.

Clinical features

  • May be asymptomatic.
  • May present with painless peripheral lymphadenopathy (usually rubbery and discrete, but may become matted).
  • Hepatosplenomegaly (enlargement of liver & spleen).
  • Systemic features ("B symptoms"): Fever (Pel-Ebstein fever), night sweats, weight loss, pruritus (itching).

Classification (WHO classification) — V.V.I. for VIVA

A. Classical Hodgkin lymphoma (95% of cases): Nodular sclerosis · Mixed cellularity · Lymphocyte rich · Lymphocyte depletion

B. Non-classical Hodgkin lymphoma (5% of cases) — classic RS cells absent or rare: Lymphocyte predominant

Morphology

  • Neoplastic giant cells (RS cells) are the true neoplastic element in Hodgkin disease.
  • Diagnostic (typical) RS cells are large cells with bi-lobed nucleus, the two halves often mirror images of each other. Each nucleus contains a large inclusion-like nucleolus surrounded by a clear zone (halo) imparting an "owl-eye" appearance. The nucleus is surrounded by non-malignant T-lymphocytes, plasma cells and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm.
  • Positive for CD15 & CD30, but negative for CD45.

Ref: Robbins/10ᵉ/611; Hoffbrand Haematology/6ᵉ/246; de Gruchy/5ᵉ/281

🖼️ Subtypes of Hodgkin's lymphoma (histological classification — key features)

Nodular sclerosis65–70%Most common form; occurs more frequently in young women. Lymph nodes show prominent fibrous bands → nodular architecture. A characteristic lacunar cell variant of the RS cell is often found (single multilobate nucleus, multiple small nucleoli, abundant pale-staining cytoplasm). Background infiltrate composed of T lymphocytes, eosinophils, macrophages and plasma cells.
Mixed cellularity20–25%Classic RS cells are plentiful. Background infiltrate rich in T-lymphocytes, eosinophils, macrophages and plasma cells.
Lymphocyte rich5%Scanty RS cells. Background infiltrate rich in T-lymphocytes with few eosinophils and plasma cells.
Lymphocyte depletedRareFew lymphocytes, numerous R-S cells and extensive necrosis and fibrosis.

📌 N.B. Lymphocyte predominant (non-classical) Hodgkin lymphoma does NOT show classic RS cells and has many features of Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma — it may be treated as such.

7 · Non-Hodgkin's Lymphoma (NHL)

🔴 Classify non-Hodgkin's lymphoma according to the Working classification. Short note on Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma. (SU-14Ja/13Ju/13Ja/08Ju, RU-17Nv/21/20M/17Ja)

Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (NHL): malignant neoplasms of lymphoid cells or other cells native to lymphoid tissue characterized by monoclonal proliferation of lymphoid cells of B-cell (85%) and T/NK-cell (15%) origin.

Criteria

  • May arise at any age (peak incidence is 65–70 years).
  • Males affected twice as commonly as females.
  • Usual presentation is localized or generalized peripheral lymphadenopathy, which are discrete, painless and firm.
  • Spreads rapidly to non-contiguous areas.
  • Involvement of mesenteric, oropharyngeal lymphoid structures (Waldeyer's ring) and epitrochlear lymph nodes is very much common.
  • Extranodal involvement is also common, e.g., bone marrow, GIT (gastrointestinal tract), stomach, lung, thyroid, skin, testis, CNS (central nervous system), etc.
  • Systemic features are generally less common.
  • Histological hallmark is absence of RS (Reed-Sternberg) giant cells.

Etiology

  • Unknown in the majority of cases.
  • Infectious agents: virus (HTLV-1 = human T-lymphotropic virus-1, EBV = Epstein-Barr virus, HHV-8 = human herpesvirus-8, HIV-1 = human immunodeficiency virus-1, HCV = hepatitis C virus), bacteria (H. pylori = Helicobacter pylori → causes gastric lymphoma), protozoa (malaria → causes Burkitt lymphoma).

Classification of NHL

Various classifications exist: (1) REAL classification, (2) Working classification.

Working classification

Divides NHL into three major prognostic subgroups (depending on the rate at which cells divide):

GradeFeatures
1. Low gradeLow cell proliferation rate · Asymptomatic for many years · Slow indolent course · Incurable, but the patient survives for a long time
2. High gradeHigh cell proliferation rate · Early symptoms common · Fatal if untreated · Responds better to treatment (potentially curable disease)
3. Intermediate grade— (between the above two)

Investigations

See the "Investigations of lymphoma" section below.

Ref: Robbins/10ᵉ/611; Basic Hematology, Dr. Mainuddin/74; de Gruchy/5ᵉ/283; Hoffbrand Haematology/6ᵉ/254

Write down the basis of the REAL classification / WHO classification of NHL. (CU-10Ju, CU-20Nv)

REAL classification (Revised European-American classification of Lymphoid neoplasms) / WHO classification of NHL is based on:

  1. Immunophenotype (cell-surface markers) and genetic aberrations
  2. Morphologic (microscopic) and clinical features

REAL classification of NHL (tree diagram)

Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma (NHL) → two arms: B-cell lymphoma and T-cell lymphoma.

B-cell lymphoma further divides into:

  • Precursor B-cell lymphoma — neoplasm of immature B-cells
  • Mature B-cell lymphoma → Follicular lymphoma · Mantle cell lymphoma · Extranodal marginal zone lymphoma · Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma · Burkitt lymphoma

T-cell lymphoma further divides into:

  • Precursor T-cell lymphoma — neoplasm of immature T-cells
  • Mature T-cell lymphoma → Adult T-cell lymphoma/leukaemia · Cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (mycosis fungoides and Sézary syndrome) · Anaplastic large-cell lymphoma

Ref: Robbins/10ᵉ/592; BRS Pathology/5ᵉ/180

9 · Investigations of Lymphoma

Write down the possible investigations to evaluate a suspected case of lymphoma. How will you diagnose a case of non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (NHL)? (CU-18Nv, CU-14Ja)

Possible investigations to evaluate a suspected case of lymphoma:

  1. CBC (complete blood count) with ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) and PBF (peripheral blood film)
  2. Chest X-ray
  3. USG (ultrasonography) of whole abdomen
  4. CT (computed tomography) scan of chest, abdomen, pelvis, neck — necessary for staging
  5. PET (positron emission tomography) scan
  6. FNAC (fine needle aspiration cytology) or biopsy of lymph nodes — biopsy is preferable (excisional or image-guided core needle biopsy), followed by histopathology and IHC (immunohistochemistry) — confirmatory
  7. Bone marrow biopsy (preferably trephine biopsy from the unilateral iliac crest)
  8. Liver function tests
  9. Renal function tests

Ref: de Gruchy/5ᵉ/285; Hoffbrand Haematology/6ᵉ/257; Long Cases in Clinical Medicine/1ᵉ/644,648

📌 Nice to Know — Clinical staging of Hodgkin's & non-Hodgkin's lymphoma (Ann Arbor classification).
StageDistribution of disease
IInvolvement of a single lymph node region (I), or involvement of a single extralymphatic organ or site (IE)
IIInvolvement of two or more lymph node regions on the same side of the diaphragm alone (II), or localized involvement of an extralymphatic organ or site (IIE)
IIIInvolvement of lymph node regions on both sides of the diaphragm without (III) or with (IIIE) localized involvement of an extralymphatic organ or site
IVDiffuse involvement of one or more extralymphatic organs or sites, with or without lymphatic involvement

📌 All stages are further divided on the basis of the absence (A) or presence (B) of the following symptoms: unexplained fever, drenching night sweats, and/or unexplained weight loss of >10% of normal body weight.

Ref: Robbins/10ᵉ/615

🔴 A male aged 20 years presented with multiple, painless, enlarged lymph nodes in cervical and axillary regions. What is your provisional diagnosis? What tests can you do on the lymph nodes to establish the diagnosis? (DU-19M)

Provisional diagnosis: Lymphoma (a young adult male with multiple painless cervical & axillary nodes → classical presentation; remember HL typically affects young adults 20–40, ♂ twice ♀).

Tests that can be done on the lymph nodes to establish the diagnosis: Lymph node biopsy followed by histopathology & immuno-histochemistry (IHC) (if needed).

10 · Spleen

What are the functions of the spleen? (with the normal splenic architecture)

Functions of the spleen:

  1. Phagocytosis of blood cells and particulate matter
  2. Antibody production
  3. Hematopoiesis (blood cell formation)
  4. Sequestration of formed blood elements

Normal splenic architecture (for the diagram): the spleen has a hilum (indentation through which vessels enter/exit), a capsule, white pulp containing lymphoid follicles with germinal centres, and red pulp of splenic cords & sinusoids, all supported by trabeculae extending from the capsule.

Ref: Robbins/10ᵉ/629

🔴 Mention the causes of splenomegaly. (Robbins)

Causes of splenomegaly

1. Infections:

  • Nonspecific splenitis of various blood-borne infections (particularly infectious endocarditis)
  • Infectious mononucleosis · Tuberculosis · Typhoid fever · Brucellosis · Cytomegalovirus (CMV) · Syphilis · Malaria · Histoplasmosis · Toxoplasmosis · Kala-azar · Trypanosomiasis · Schistosomiasis · Leishmaniasis · Echinococcosis

2. Congestive states related to portal hypertension:

  • Cirrhosis of liver · Portal or splenic vein thrombosis · Cardiac failure

3. Lympho-haematogenous disorders:

  • Hodgkin lymphoma · Non-Hodgkin lymphoma · Multiple myeloma · Myeloproliferative disorders · Haemolytic anaemias

4. Immunologic-inflammatory conditions:

  • Rheumatoid arthritis · Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)

5. Storage diseases:

  • Gaucher disease · Niemann-Pick disease · Mucopolysaccharidoses

6. Miscellaneous:

  • Amyloidosis · Primary neoplasms and cysts · Secondary neoplasms

Ref: Robbins/10ᵉ/630

What do you mean by hypersplenism?

Hypersplenism: Enlargement of the spleen can cause a syndrome known as hypersplenism, characterized by anaemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, alone or in combination.

Probable cause of the cytopenias: increased sequestration of formed elements and the consequent enhanced phagocytosis by the splenic macrophages.

Ref: Robbins/10ᵉ/629

What are the causes of massive splenomegaly?

Causes of massive (huge) splenomegaly:

  1. Chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML)
  2. Myelofibrosis
  3. Hemolytic anaemia
  4. Chronic kala-azar
  5. Chronic malaria
  6. Lymphoma

Ref: Medicine Lecture SSMC

11 · Thymus

Name some disorders of the thymus.

Disorders of the thymus:

1. Developmental disorders:

  • Thymic hypoplasia or aplasia — seen in DiGeorge syndrome, accompanied by parathyroid developmental failure (hence hypocalcaemia + T-cell deficiency).
  • Isolated thymic cysts

2. Thymic hyperplasia: The term "thymic hyperplasia" is a bit misleading, as it usually applies to the appearance of lymphoid follicles within the thymus, a state referred to as thymic follicular hyperplasia (classically associated with myasthenia gravis).

3. Thymic neoplasms:

  • Germ cell tumours
  • Lymphomas
  • Carcinoids
  • Thymomas: tumours of thymic epithelial cells (associated with myasthenia gravis, pure red-cell aplasia, hypogammaglobulinaemia)

Ref: Robbins/10ᵉ/631

Is a T-cell lymphoma = False (it is a B-cell lymphoma) · occurs mainly in children and young adults = True · can be endemic = True (endemic African form) · mass involving mandible if sporadic = False (mandible involvement is typical of the ENDEMIC form, not sporadic) · is very aggressive = True (high-grade).
The following are correctly matched: Reed-Sternberg cell — non-Hodgkin lymphoma (T/F), polycythemia — renal cell carcinoma (T/F), saddle thrombus — myocardial infarction (T/F), increased albumin-globulin ratio — cirrhosis of liver (T/F), necrotizing inflammation — bronchiectasis (T/F)? (RU-13Ju)
Reed-Sternberg cell — non-Hodgkin lymphoma = False (RS cell is the hallmark of HODGKIN lymphoma) · polycythemia — renal cell carcinoma = True (paraneoplastic erythropoietin) · saddle thrombus — myocardial infarction = False · increased albumin-globulin ratio — cirrhosis = False (albumin falls, globulin rises → ratio DECREASES / reversed) · necrotizing inflammation — bronchiectasis = False.
🎯 One-liner mnemonics:
Hodgkin = "RS + young adult + contiguous + curable". NHL = "no RS + old (65–70) + non-contiguous + low cure". Consistency: rubbery = lymphoma, firm & matted = TB, hard = malignancy, stony hard = calcified, soft cystic = cold abscess.
🎯 "Owl-eye" cell of Hodgkin disease = ? · "Popcorn cell" (L&H) variant of RS cell found in which HL subtype? · "Lacunar cell" characteristic of which subtype?
Owl-eye nucleus = classic Reed-Sternberg cell · popcorn/L&H cell = lymphocyte-predominant (non-classical) HL · lacunar cell = nodular sclerosis HL.
🎯 Which is the most common subtype of HL? Which subtype favours young women? Which HL subtype is CD15+ / CD30+ / CD45−?
Most common = nodular sclerosis (NS) · favours young women = NS · HL neoplastic RS cells are CD15+ & CD30+, CD45−.
🎯 Conditions where RS-like cells may also be found (apart from HL)?
Infectious mononucleosis, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and CLL (chronic lymphocytic leukaemia).
🎯 What causes splenomegaly in the "storage disease" group?
Gaucher disease, Niemann-Pick disease, mucopolysaccharidoses.
🎯 What is the molecular/cellular basis of the cytopenias in hypersplenism?
Increased sequestration of formed elements + enhanced phagocytosis by splenic macrophages → anaemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia.
