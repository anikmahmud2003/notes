🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of the Female Genital System

The female genital tract spans vulva → vagina → cervix → uterus (endometrium + myometrium) → fallopian tubes → ovaries. Disease follows a logical anatomical progression:

🔴 Infections (lower tract: HSV, HPV; PID: gonorrhoea/chlamydia) → Vulvar disorders (lichen sclerosus, VIN, vulvar carcinoma, extramammary Paget disease) → Vaginal lesions (VAIN, rhabdomyosarcoma) → Cervix (CIN I→II→III → invasive carcinoma, almost always HPV-driven) → Endometrium (AUB/DUB, endometriosis, adenomyosis, endometrial hyperplasia → endometrial carcinoma) → Myometrium (leiomyoma = "fibroid," leiomyosarcoma) → Ovaries (WHO classification: surface epithelial, germ cell, sex-cord stromal, metastatic tumours; Krukenberg tumour, Meigs syndrome).

📌 Key themes: HPV = oncogenic driver of cervical (and vaginal/vulvar) cancer; unopposed oestrogen drives endometrial hyperplasia/cancer; leiomyoma is the most common gynaecological tumour; ovarian germ cell tumours dominate in young women; Krukenberg = bilateral GI metastases with signet-ring cells.

Robbins 10e, Ch 23 (Female Genital Tract), pp. 985–1028 · Arif Guide 2025 Vol-2, pp.264–291

1 · Infections of the Female Genital Tract

What are the major infections of the female genital tract? Mention their clinical significance.

Bacteria — Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis are the major causes of female infertility. Streptococcus infections are implicated in preterm deliveries, stillbirths, and neonatal sepsis.

Viruses — especially herpes simplex virus (HSV) and human papillomavirus (HPV), account for considerable morbidity; HSVs cause painful genital ulcerations, whereas HPVs are involved in the pathogenesis of cervical, vaginal, and vulvar cancers. Molluscum contagiosum is a cutaneous or mucosal lesion caused by poxvirus.

Robbins 10e pp.986–988

2 · Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)

🔴 Define PID. Write down its etiology, pathogenesis, and complications.

Definition: PID is an infection that begins in the vulva or vagina and spreads upward to involve most of the structures in the female genital system, resulting in pelvic pain, adnexal tenderness, fever, and vaginal discharge.

Etiology

  1. Gonococcus infection (most common cause)
  2. Chlamydia infection
  3. Staphylococci
  4. Streptococci
  5. Coliform bacteria
  6. Clostridium perfringens

Pathogenesis

  • Gonococcal PID: inflammatory changes appear 2–7 days after inoculation. Endocervical mucosa is the most common site of initial involvement. From here, organisms may spread upward to involve the fallopian tubes and tubo-ovarian region.
  • Non-gonococcal PID: bacterial infections that follow induced abortion, dilation and curettage (D&C), and other surgical procedures spread upward from the uterus through the lymphatics and venous channels rather than on mucosal surfaces. These infections tend to produce more inflammation within the deeper layers of the organs than gonococcal infections.

Complications

  • Acute: peritonitis; bacteraemia (which in turn may result in endocarditis, meningitis, and suppurative arthritis).
  • Chronic sequelae: infertility and tubal obstruction; ectopic pregnancy; pelvic pain; intestinal obstruction (due to adhesions between the small bowel and the pelvic organs).

Robbins 10e pp.988–989

4 · Vaginal Lesions

Classify the lesions of the vagina (congenital, benign, pre-malignant, malignant).

Lesions of the vagina

  1. Congenital anomalies: atresia and total absence; septate or double vagina.
  2. Benign neoplasms: stromal tumours (stromal polyps), leiomyomas, haemangiomas.
  3. Pre-malignant: vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia (VAIN).
  4. Malignant — Primary: squamous cell carcinoma (SCC); embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (sarcoma botryoides).
  5. Malignant — Secondary: carcinoma spreading from the cervix (most common).

  • Erosion
  • Cervicitis (acute & chronic)
  • Endocervical polyps

B. Pre-malignant and malignant neoplastic disorders

6 · Cervical Intraepithelial Neoplasia (CIN)

🔴 What is CIN? Describe its characteristics, classifications, and morphology.

Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN): a spectrum of precancerous changes in cervical epithelium, graded on progressively severe degree of changes. It includes dysplasia and carcinoma in situ (CIS).

Characteristics

  1. They represent a continuum of morphologic change with indistinct boundaries.
  2. They do not invariably progress to cancer and may spontaneously regress, with the risk of persistence or progression increasing with the severity of the precancerous change.
  3. They are associated with human papillomavirus (HPV), and high-risk HPV (types 16, 18 & above) are found in increasing frequency in high-grade CIN.
  4. Progression to invasive carcinoma (when it occurs) may take place in a few months to more than a decade.

Classification systems

A. CIN system (based on extent of epithelial involvement):

  • CIN-I: Mild dysplasia
  • CIN-II: Moderate dysplasia
  • CIN-III: Severe dysplasia (carcinoma in situ)

B. Dysplasia / CIS system:

  • Mild dysplasia
  • Severe dysplasia (carcinoma in situ)

C. Squamous intraepithelial lesion (SIL) — two-tier system (current):

  • Low-grade SIL (LSIL): CIN-I (mild dysplasia)
  • High-grade SIL (HSIL): CIN-II & CIN-III (moderate & severe dysplasia, carcinoma in situ)

Morphology — important features

  • Cellular immaturity
  • Cellular disorganization
  • Nuclear abnormalities
  • Increased mitotic activity

7 · Carcinoma of the Cervix

🔴 Describe the etiology/risk factors and pathogenesis of carcinoma of the cervix.

Risk factors / etiologic factors

  1. Early age at first intercourse
  2. Multiple sexual partners
  3. Male partner with multiple previous sexual partners
  4. Early first pregnancy
  5. Multiparity
  6. Persistent infection with high-risk HPV (types 16, 18 and above) — single most important risk factor
  7. Sexually transmitted diseases (STDs)
  8. Cigarette smoking
  9. Oral contraceptive pill (OCP)
  10. Immunosuppression
  11. Low socio-economic status

Pathogenesis

Sexual activity → HPV exposure → Cervical transformation zone (where immature squamous metaplastic epithelial cells are most susceptible to HPV infection).

  • High- & low-risk HPVs → Low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL).
  • High-risk HPVs (type 16, 18 & above) → High-grade SIL (HSIL).
  • Additional co-factors: smoking, oral contraceptives, high parity, immune status, host gene alterations, time → Invasive squamous carcinoma / Invasive adenocarcinoma.

HPV oncoproteins: High-risk HPV produces E6 & E7 proteins which result in increased destruction of p53 and Rb, respectively. Loss of these tumour suppressor proteins increases the risk for CIN.

Persistent infection increases the risk of cervical precancer and subsequent invasive carcinoma. Duration: high-oncogenic-risk HPVs persist longer (average 13 months) vs low-risk HPVs (8 months). High-risk HPVs are also detected in vaginal SCC and in a subset of vulvar, penile, anal, tonsillar, and other oropharyngeal cancers.

Robbins 10e pp.996, 998; Pathoma 2014 p.139

Short note: Transformation zone of the cervix.

Anatomically, the cervix is divided into the exocervix (visible on vaginal exam, lined by squamous epithelium) and the endocervix (lined by columnar mucus-secreting epithelium).

The point where the squamous and columnar epithelium meets is the squamocolumnar junction (SCJ).

With the onset of puberty, the SCJ undergoes eversion, causing the columnar epithelium to become visible on the exocervix. The exposed columnar cells eventually undergo squamous metaplasia, forming a region called the transformation zone, where tumours most commonly arise.

Importance: Most tumours of the cervix are of epithelial origin and are caused by oncogenic strains of HPV. Immature squamous metaplastic epithelial cells in the transformation zone are most susceptible to HPV infection — this is where cervical precursor lesions and cancers develop.

Robbins 10e p.995; Pathoma 2011

🔴 Give the morphology and spread of cervical carcinoma. What are its complications?

General: one of the most common neoplasms in women; average age 45–50 years. Widespread screening (Pap test) has reduced mortality sharply.

Morphology

Invasive cervical carcinoma may manifest as fungating (exophytic) or infiltrative masses. Histologically:

  • Squamous cell carcinoma: nests and tongues of malignant squamous epithelium, either keratinizing or non-keratinizing, invading the cervical stroma.
  • Adenocarcinoma: glandular epithelium composed of malignant endocervical cells with large hyperchromatic nuclei and relatively mucin-depleted cytoplasm → dark-appearing glands.
  • Adenosquamous carcinoma: intermixed malignant glandular and squamous epithelium.
  • Neuroendocrine carcinoma: appearance similar to small-cell carcinoma of the lung, positive for high-risk HPVs.

Spread

  • Direct: local extension into paracervical soft tissue, urinary bladder, ureters (→ hydronephrosis), rectum, and vagina.
  • Lymphatic: lymphovascular invasion → local and distant lymph node metastases.
  • Blood-borne: liver, lungs, bone or bone marrow.

Complications

  1. Local extension → ureteral obstruction → pyelonephritis → uraemia.
  2. Distant metastasis.

Staging (FIGO)

StageDefinition
IConfined to the cervix (Ia: microscopic <7 mm; Ib: clinically visible)
IIExtends beyond the cervix but not to the pelvic wall or lower 1/3 vagina
IIIExtended to the pelvic wall & lower 1/3 vagina
IVBeyond the true pelvis or involved bladder/rectal mucosa (includes metastatic disease)

Robbins 10e pp.998–1000; Manual of Clinical Oncology 7e pp.322–323

🔴 How is carcinoma cervix diagnosed? How can it be prevented?

Diagnosis

1. History: unexplained vaginal bleeding (especially post-coital), foul-smelling vaginal discharge, painful coitus (dyspareunia), pelvic pain, weight loss, obstructive uropathy.

2. Clinical examination: cervix is fixed, hard, irregular, friable, bleeds on touch.

3. Investigations:

  • Visual inspection by acetic acid (VIA)
  • HPV DNA testing by polymerase chain reaction (PCR)
  • Pap's smear
  • Colposcopy and colposcopy-guided biopsy followed by histopathology

Prevention

1. Primary prevention:

  • Avoidance of risk factors (early marriage, multiple sex partners, smoking).
  • Counselling / health education for safe sexual behaviour (e.g. condom use).
  • Prophylactic HPV vaccine (against HPV types 6, 11, 16, and 18) for all school girls (12–18 years) and women (16–25 years), dose schedule 0-2-6 months.

2. Secondary prevention: screening & early detection (begin at age 21 years):

  • Pap's smear / Pap's test
  • HPV DNA testing by PCR
  • Visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA)
  • Colposcopy

3. Treatment of the pre-malignant lesions.

Robbins 10e p.1000; DeVita 11e p.2090

8 · Pap Test

🔴 Short note: Pap test / Pap smear — procedure, schedule, Papanicolaou grading, and importance.

Definition: cytologic preparations of exfoliated cells from the cervical transformation zone stained with the Papanicolaou method. It is a screening procedure for cervical cancer that tests for pre-cancerous or cancerous cells on the cervix.

Procedure

  1. Collection: using a spatula or cytobrush, the transformation zone is circumferentially scraped.
  2. Smearing: specimen smeared on clean glass slides.
  3. Fixation: by 95% alcohol.
  4. Staining & examination: examined under microscope by a cytotechnologist.

Schedule

  • First smear at 21 years of age or within 3 years of onset of sexual activity, then every 3 years.
  • After 30: normal cytology + HPV-negative → every 5 years.
  • Normal cytology but HPV-positive → repeat cytology every 6–12 months.

Papanicolaou grading

ClassMeaning
INormal
IIInflammatory atypia (infection)
IIIMild dysplasia (CIN-I), moderate dysplasia (CIN-II)
IVSevere dysplasia / carcinoma in situ (CIN-III)
VInvasive squamous carcinoma

Importance: main screening tool for cervical diseases, especially carcinoma cervix. False-negative error rate ~10–20%.

Robbins 10e pp.1000–1001; DeVita 11e p.2094; Manual of Clinical Oncology 7e pp.323–324

Diseases of uterus

  1. Functional endometrial disorders (dysfunctional uterine bleeding)
  2. Inflammatory disorders:
    • Acute endometritis — retained products of conception; group-A haemolytic streptococci, staphylococci.

What is DUB? What is an anovulatory cycle and its causes?

Dysfunctional uterine bleeding (DUB): uterine bleeding that lacks an underlying structural abnormality — excessive bleeding during or between menstrual periods without a causative uterine lesion (tumour, polyp, infection, hyperplasia, trauma, blood dyscrasia, or pregnancy).

Common causes of DUB: (1) Anovulatory cycle, (2) Ovulatory dysfunctional bleeding (inadequate luteal phase), (3) Hormonal contraceptives, (4) Irregular shedding.

Anovulatory cycle: a menstrual cycle with no ovulation. It is the most frequent cause of DUB during menarche and menopause. Failure of ovulation → prolonged and excessive oestrogenic stimulation without a progestational (luteal) phase.

Causes of anovulation

  • Endocrine disorders: thyroid disease, adrenal disease, pituitary tumours.
  • Ovarian lesions: functioning ovarian tumours (granulosa cell tumours), polycystic ovaries.
  • Generalised metabolic disturbances: obesity, malnutrition, chronic systemic diseases.

Robbins 10e p.1003; Harsh Mohan 7e p.721

11 · Endometriosis & Chocolate Cyst

🔴 Define endometriosis. Give its common sites, pathogenesis, clinical features, and complications.

Definition: presence of "ectopic" endometrial tissue (both glands and stroma, or stroma alone) at a site outside of the uterus. Affects approx. 6–10% of women, principally in the 3rd and 4th decades.

Common sites (descending order)

  1. Ovaries (most common)
  2. Uterine ligaments
  3. Rectovaginal septum
  4. Cul-de-sac
  5. Pelvic peritoneum
  6. Large and small bowel and appendix
  7. Mucosa of the cervix, vagina, and fallopian tubes
  8. Laparotomy scars

Pathogenesis (major theories)

  1. Regurgitation theory: retrograde menstruation through fallopian tubes → endometrial tissue implanted at abnormal locations.
  2. Benign metastases theory: endometrial tissue spreads to distant sites (bone, lung, brain) via blood and lymphatics.
  3. Metaplastic theory: ectopic endometrium develops in situ from local tissues by metaplasia of the coelomic epithelium (mesothelium).
  4. Extrauterine stem/progenitor cell theory: stem/progenitor cells from bone marrow differentiate into endometrial tissue.

Clinical features / complications

  • Severe dysmenorrhoea (painful menstruation)
  • Dyspareunia (pain with intercourse)
  • Pelvic pain (due to intrapelvic bleeding and periuterine adhesions)
  • Pain on defecation (rectal wall involvement)
  • Dysuria (bladder wall involvement)
  • Menstrual irregularities
  • Infertility (30–40%) — involvement of fallopian tube mucosa
  • Development of malignancy (endometrioid and clear cell carcinoma) — uncommon

Robbins 10e pp.1004–1006; Harsh Mohan 7e p.722

🔴 What is a chocolate cyst? What is pseudocyesis?

Chocolate cyst of the ovary (endometrioma): endometriotic lesions bleed periodically in response to cyclic ovarian hormonal stimulation → nodules with red-blue to yellow-brown appearance. Ovaries may become markedly distorted by large cystic masses (3–5 cm) filled with brown fluid from previous haemorrhage → called chocolate cysts or endometriomas. Organising haemorrhage causes extensive fibrous adhesions between tubes, ovaries, and other structures, obliterating the pouch of Douglas.

Pseudocyesis (false pregnancy): the appearance of clinical or subclinical signs and symptoms associated with pregnancy although the individual is not physically carrying a baby.

Robbins 10e p.1006

12 · Adenomyosis

🔴 Define adenomyosis. Give its diagnosis, clinical features, and complications.

Definition: presence of endometrial tissue within the uterine wall (myometrium). Occurs in up to 20% of uteri.

Diagnosis (microscopic): irregular nests of endometrial stroma, with or without glands, within the myometrium, separated from the basalis by at least 2–3 mm.

Clinical features / complications

  • Menometrorrhagia (irregular and heavy menses)
  • Colicky dysmenorrhoea
  • Dyspareunia
  • Pelvic pain (particularly during menstruation)

Note: adenomyosis can coexist with endometriosis.

Robbins 10e p.1006

13 · Endometrial Hyperplasia

🔴 Write the pathology of endometrial hyperplasia. Differentiate non-atypical from atypical hyperplasia.

Definition: abnormal proliferation of endometrial glands relative to the stroma, resulting in an increased gland-to-stroma ratio compared with normal proliferative endometrium.

Cause — prolonged oestrogenic stimulation due to:

  • Anovulatory cycles
  • Obesity (peripheral conversion of androgens to oestrogens)
  • Menopause
  • Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS)
  • Functioning granulosa cell tumours of the ovary
  • Excessive ovarian cortical function (cortical stromal hyperplasia)
  • Prolonged oestrogen replacement therapy

Presentation: classic presentation is postmenopausal uterine bleeding.

WHO classification (two major categories)

TypeFeaturesMalignant potential
Non-atypical (typical) hyperplasiaNo cellular atypia; increase in gland-to-stroma ratio; glands vary in size/shape, may be dilatedLow (~1–3%)
Atypical hyperplasia (endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia / carcinoma in situ)Atypical cells: loss of polarity, large size, irregular/hyperchromatic nuclei, prominent nucleoli, altered N:C ratio. Glands back-to-back, complex branching outlines.High (23–48%) — precancerous condition

Major complication: frequent precursor to the most common type of endometrial carcinoma.

Robbins 10e p.1006; Harsh Mohan 7e p.723; BRS Pathology 5e p.302

14 · Carcinoma of the Endometrium

🔴 What are the risk factors for endometrial carcinoma? How is it diagnosed?

Nice to Know: endometrial carcinoma is the most common invasive cancer of the female genital tract. Peak incidence in 55–65 years. Usually produces irregular or postmenopausal vaginal bleeding with excessive leukorrhoea.

Risk factors

  • Age >40 years (peak 55–65)
  • Obesity
  • Diabetes
  • Hypertension
  • Infertility
  • Unopposed oestrogen stimulation
  • Endometrial hyperplasia

Investigations to confirm diagnosis

  • Transvaginal ultrasound
  • Endometrial biopsy → histopathology
  • Dilatation & curettage (D&C) — if biopsy is non-diagnostic
  • Pap test (limited sensitivity — as low as 40%)
  • Chest X-ray, CT scan / MRI of abdomen & pelvis — for staging
  • Tumour marker: Serum CA-125 (elevated in 60% of cases)

Robbins 9e p.1020; Robbins 10e pp.1014, 1018

15 · Leiomyoma (Fibroid Uterus)

🔴 Describe the pathology/morphology and complications of leiomyoma of the uterus.

Definition: benign smooth-muscle neoplasms of the uterus — perhaps the most common tumours in women. Present in about 75% of females of reproductive age. May occur singly or more often are multiple.

Morphology — Gross

  • Sharply circumscribed, discrete, round, firm, grey-white tumour of varying sizes from small nodules to massive tumours filling the pelvis.
  • Found within the myometrium of the corpus.
  • Locations: intramural (within myometrium), submucosal (beneath endometrium), subserosal (beneath serosa).
  • Cut section: characteristic whorled pattern.
  • Large tumours may develop areas of yellow-brown to red softening (red degeneration).

Morphology — Microscopic

  • Whorled bundles of smooth muscle cells admixed with variable connective tissue.
  • Individual SMCs: uniform size/shape, abundant cytoplasm, central oval nuclei.
  • Mitotic figures are scarce.

Complications

  1. Abnormal uterine bleeding
  2. Urinary frequency (bladder compression)
  3. Sudden pain (infarction of large or pedunculated tumour)
  4. Impaired fertility
  5. In pregnant women: spontaneous abortion, fetal malpresentation, uterine inertia, postpartum haemorrhage
  6. Malignant transformation to leiomyosarcoma — extremely rare

Other sites of leiomyoma: arrector pili (pilar leiomyoma — very painful), nipples, scrotum, labia, and less frequently deep soft tissue and gut wall.

Robbins 10e p.1014

16 · Ovarian Tumours — WHO Classification

🔴 Classify ovarian tumours (WHO classification).

A. Surface epithelial tumours (~3/4 of ovarian tumours)

Each sub-type has benign, borderline, and malignant variants:

  • Serous tumours: serous cystadenoma (benign), serous borderline tumour, serous cystadenocarcinoma (malignant) — 50% of ovarian carcinomas
  • Mucinous tumours (endocervical-like & intestinal type)
  • Endometrioid tumours
  • Clear cell tumours
  • Brenner tumours (transitional cell tumours)

B. Germ cell tumours

Nearly 95% benign; chiefly in females <20 years.

  • Dysgerminoma (ovarian counterpart of testicular seminoma)
  • Teratoma: benign (mature/cystic — dermoid cyst), malignant (immature), monodermal/specialized (struma ovarii, carcinoid)
  • Endodermal sinus (yolk sac) tumour
  • Choriocarcinoma
  • Others (embryonal carcinoma, polyembryoma, mixed germ cell tumours)

C. Sex-cord stromal tumours

  • Granulosa-theca cell tumours: granulosa cell tumours, fibromas, thecomas, fibrothecomas
  • Sertoli-Leydig cell tumours (androblastoma, arrhenoblastoma)
  • Steroid (lipid) cell tumours

D. Malignant, not otherwise specified

E. Metatic cancer from non-ovarian primary

  • Krukenberg tumour & others: colonic, appendiceal, gastric, pancreaticobiliary, breast

Nice to Know:

  • Benign tumours: well-differentiated epithelial cells, minimal proliferation.
  • Borderline tumours: increased cell proliferation, no stromal invasion.
  • Malignant tumours: increased epithelial atypia + stromal invasion.

Robbins 10e p.1017; BRS Pathology 5e pp.304–307; Harsh Mohan 7e p.731

🔴 Classify ovarian cysts (non-neoplastic and neoplastic).

1. Non-neoplastic and functional cysts

  • Follicular cysts
  • Luteal cysts
  • Chocolate cyst (ovarian endometrioma)
  • Polycystic ovarian disease / PCOS (Stein-Leventhal syndrome)

2. Neoplastic cysts

  • Surface epithelial tumours: serous or mucinous cystadenoma; serous or mucinous cystadenocarcinoma
  • Germ cell tumours: mature cystic teratoma (dermoid cyst)

Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS)

A complex endocrine disorder characterised by hyperandrogenism, menstrual abnormalities, polycystic ovaries, chronic anovulation, and decreased fertility. Associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and premature atherosclerosis.

Pathophysiology: excessive androgen production and low FSH levels. Testosterone inhibits FSH, but LH levels are sufficient to cause luteinisation of theca and granulosa cells → androgen secretion → anovulation. Hereditary basis suggested in some cases.

18 · Dermoid Cyst (Benign Cystic Teratoma)

🔴 Short note: Dermoid cyst of the ovary — classification, morphology, and complications.

Ovarian teratoma: tumours composed of tissues derived from all three germ cell layers — ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm — in varying combinations.

Types of teratoma

  1. Mature (benign) teratoma — most are cystic, known as dermoid cysts
  2. Immature (malignant) teratoma — rare, chiefly in pre-pubertal adolescents and young women <20; predominantly solid; contain immature/embryonal structures
  3. Monodermal / highly specialized teratoma — struma ovarii, carcinoid tumour

Benign cystic teratoma (dermoid cyst): accounts for ~20% of ovarian tumours and 90% of germ cell tumours. Most frequent benign ovarian tumour, usually in young women during reproductive life.

Morphology

Gross:

  • Bilateral in 10–15% of cases
  • Unilocular cysts containing hair & cheesy sebaceous material
  • Cyst wall: thin, opaque grey-white, wrinkled, apparent epidermis with protruding hair shafts
  • Tooth structures & areas of calcification within the wall

Microscopic:

  • Cyst wall lined by skin, including hair follicles & skin appendages
  • Structures from other germ layers: cartilage, bone, GI, neural, respiratory, and thyroid gland tissue

Components

  • Skin lining with hair follicles, sebaceous glands, sweat glands
  • Bone, tooth, cartilage, smooth muscle, GI, pancreatic, neural, retinal, respiratory, salivary gland, and thyroid gland tissues

Chance of malignancy (~1%)

  • Squamous cell carcinoma (most common)
  • Thyroid carcinoma
  • Melanoma

Struma ovarii

A rare monodermal teratoma containing mostly thyroid tissue (>50% of overall tissue). ~5% of all ovarian teratomas. May cause hyperthyroidism. Most are benign; malignant in a small percentage.

Robbins 10e pp.1023–1024; BRS Pathology 5e p.306; Harsh Mohan 7e pp.736–737

19 · Dysgerminoma

🔴 Give the morphology of dysgerminoma of the ovary.

Definition: germ cell tumour of the ovary — the ovarian counterpart of testicular seminoma. ~2% of all ovarian cancers; ~half of malignant germ cell tumours. About 10% have elevated β-hCG in plasma. All dysgerminomas are malignant and are extremely radiosensitive.

Age: 75% occur in the 2nd & 3rd decades.

Morphology

Gross:

  • Usually unilateral (80–90%)
  • Solid tumours, varying in size from small nodules to masses filling the entire abdomen
  • Cut surface: yellow-white to grey-pink, soft & fleshy with haemorrhage and necrosis foci

Microscopic:

  • Tumour cells arranged in diffuse sheets, islands, and cords separated by scanty fibrous stroma
  • Fibrous stroma infiltrated with mature lymphocytes & occasional granulomas
  • May be found in the wall of an otherwise benign cystic teratoma; conversely, a predominantly dysgerminomatous tumour may contain a small cystic teratoma

Robbins 10e p.1024; Harsh Mohan 7e p.737

What are the hormone-producing tumours of the ovary?
  1. Granulosa-theca cell tumours: secrete oestrogen
  2. Sertoli-Leydig cell tumours (androblastoma, arrhenoblastoma): secrete androgen
  3. Choriocarcinoma: secretes human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)
  4. Others: struma ovarii, carcinoid, hilar cell tumour

Robbins 10e pp.1016–1028; BRS Pathology 5e p.307

20 · Krukenberg Tumour

🔴 Short note: Krukenberg tumour.

Definition: a classic metastatic gastrointestinal carcinoma involving the ovaries. Characterised by bilateral metastases composed of mucin-producing cancer cells with a "signet-ring" appearance, most often of gastric origin. May also arise from breast, lung, etc.

Origins of metastatic ovarian tumours

  • Müllerian origin: uterus, fallopian tube, contralateral ovary, pelvic peritoneum.
  • Extra-Müllerian origin: breast, GI tract (colon, stomach, biliary tract, pancreas), pseudomyxoma peritonei from appendiceal tumours.

Robbins 10e p.1028; BRS Pathology 5e p.307; Harsh Mohan 7e p.1034

21 · Meigs Syndrome

🔴 Short note: Meigs syndrome.

Definition: the triad of ovarian fibroma + ascites + hydrothorax (usually only right side). Genesis is unknown.

Key feature: ascites and pleural effusion resolve spontaneously when the ovarian tumour is removed.

Robbins 10e p.1027; BRS Pathology 5e p.306

🎯 What is the transformation zone? Where do most cervical cancers arise?
Transformation zone = area of the cervix where columnar epithelium abuts squamous epithelium, formed by squamous metaplasia of everted columnar cells. Most cervical cancers arise here because immature metaplastic cells are most susceptible to HPV infection.
🎯 PCOS = ? Name its principal biochemical abnormality.
Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) = hyperandrogenism + menstrual abnormalities + polycystic ovaries + chronic anovulation. Principal abnormality: excessive androgen production + low FSH (inhibited by testosterone) with relatively adequate LH → anovulation.
🎯 The submucous leiomyoma is most likely to cause which complication?
Menorrhagia (heavy menstrual bleeding) — because submucous location distorts the endometrial cavity and interferes with normal endometrial shedding/hemostasis.
🎯 What percentage of ovarian tumours are surface epithelial in origin? What percentage are germ cell tumours?
Surface epithelial = ~3/4 (75%) of all ovarian tumours · Germ cell = ~95% benign, mostly in young women <20 years.
🎯 Vitamin deficiency / obesity → peripheral conversion of androgens to oestrogens → endometrial hyperplasia. Name the pathway.
Aromatase pathway in peripheral adipose tissue converts androstenedione → oestrone → oestradiol, providing unopposed oestrogenic stimulation of the endometrium, especially in postmenopausal obese women.
