🧠 Big Picture — Lipids, Lipoproteins & Atherosclerosis Risk

Lipids are water-insoluble molecules that must travel in blood as lipoproteins (lipid + apolipoprotein complexes). The clinical lipid profile measures: Total Cholesterol (TC), Triacylglycerols (TAG/TG), Low-Density Lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and High-Density Lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C).

🔴 LDL = "bad cholesterol" — carries cholesterol from liver → peripheral tissues → promotes atherosclerosis. 🔴 HDL = "good cholesterol" — carries cholesterol from periphery → liver → protects against atherosclerosis (reverse cholesterol transport).

📌 Four lipoprotein classes by density: Chylomicrons → VLDL → LDL → HDL (density ↑, size ↓). VLDL and LDL are atherogenic; HDL is anti-atherogenic.

🎯 Dyslipidaemia = abnormal lipid profile → major modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and coronary artery disease (CAD). Diagnosed by fasting lipid profile; managed by diet, exercise, and drugs (statins, fibrates).

1 · What Is a Lipid Profile?

❓ What is a lipid profile? Give its definition and objectives.

Lipid profile means the pattern or status of lipids in plasma.

More precisely: a group of laboratory tests done to assess the lipid status of an individual and to evaluate risk factors for atherosclerosis and coronary artery disease (CAD).

Frequently examined: DU-13/07Ja, BUP-22Nv, CMU-21/18M, DU-17M, RU-10Ju, CU-19Nv, DU-23Nv/21Oct/18Nv/17/14/11/07Ja/06S, SMU-23M/21Nv, RU-17Jw/Ja/16Ja/13Ju, CU-15Ju/Ja/10/09Ju/08Ja.

4 · Patient Preparation & Procedure

❓ How do you prepare a patient for lipid profile testing? Describe the procedure.

Preparation (4 rules)

  1. 10–12 hours fasting prior to the test.
  2. Usual diet in the previous 3 days — no dietary change allowed.
  3. Usual activities in the previous 3 days.
  4. Individual must be free from trauma, surgery, acute infection, and pregnancy.

Procedure

  1. Collect a fasting morning blood sample.
  2. Blood is taken in a plain (red-top) test tube and allowed to clot.
  3. Serum is separated and lipid profile components are measured.

5 · Which Fraction Is "Better"? — TG

❓ Which fraction of the lipid profile is better and why?

Triacylglycerol (TG) is the better fraction of the lipid profile because it is one of the major treatable risk factors for cardiovascular disease, whilst severe hypertriglyceridaemia also predisposes to acute pancreatitis.

Ref: Davidson's 21st/449

6 · Indications of Lipid Profile

❓ Enumerate the indications/objectives of lipid profile.
  1. Diagnosis of dyslipidaemia (abnormal lipid levels).
  2. Assessment of prognosis for a known dyslipidaemic patient.
  3. Assessment of risk for atherosclerosis and coronary artery disease (CAD).

Arif Guide 2025 Vol-2 p.487

7 · Clinical Importance & Screening

❓ What is the clinical importance of lipid profile in cardiovascular disease screening?

Screening for cardiovascular disease (CVD):

  • Increased LDL-C is directly related to cardiovascular disease (more LDL = more risk).
  • Increased HDL-C is inversely related to cardiovascular disease (more HDL = less risk).

Ref: Davidson's 21st/451, 1296; Harper's 25th/869

8 · Dyslipidaemia & Hyperlipidaemia

❓ Define dyslipidaemia, hyperlipidaemia, and hypercholesterolaemia.

Dyslipidaemia: abnormalities in the lipid profile (any or all of TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C abnormal).

Hyperlipidaemia: excess lipid in blood (above normal range).

Hypercholesterolaemia: increased cholesterol level in blood above normal (> 300 mg/dL).

Arif Guide 2025 Vol-2 p.488

9 · Causes of Hyperlipidaemia

❓ What are the causes of hyperlipidaemia? Classify as primary and secondary, for both hypercholesterolaemia and hypertriglyceridaemia.

A. Hypercholesterolaemia (↑ Cholesterol)

1. Primary: Familial hypercholesterolaemia.

2. Secondary causes:

  • Hypothyroidism
  • Pregnancy
  • Cholestatic liver disease
  • Drugs — diuretics, corticosteroids, androgens
  • Nephrotic syndrome
  • Anorexia nervosa
  • Porphyria
  • Hyperparathyroidism

B. Hypertriglyceridaemia (↑ TG)

1. Primary: Familial hypertriglyceridaemia.

2. Secondary causes:

  • Diabetes mellitus (type 2)
  • Chronic renal disease
  • Abdominal obesity
  • Excess alcohol
  • Hepatocellular disease
  • Drugs — β-blockers, retinoids, corticosteroids
🎯 VIVA favourite: "Nephrotic syndrome, DM type 2, hypothyroidism, obesity, alcohol" — top 5 causes to remember.

10 · Diagnostic Approach to Dyslipidaemia

❓ How will you diagnose a case of dyslipidaemia?

A. History

  • Obesity
  • Diabetes
  • Hypertension
  • Nephrotic syndrome
  • Occasional severe abdominal pain (history of pancreatitis — suggests severe hypertriglyceridaemia)
  • Family history of dyslipidaemia
  • Hypothyroidism
  • Drugs — OCP (oral contraceptive pill), steroid, thiazide, β-blocker, etc.
  • Alcohol intake
  • Dietary habits

B. Physical Examination

  • Corneal arcus — lipid deposit in cornea
  • Xanthelasma — yellow plaques around eyelids
  • Eruptive xanthoma — small yellow papules on skin (↑TG)
  • Tendon xanthoma — firm nodules over tendons (familial hypercholesterolaemia)

C. Laboratory Investigation

Estimation of serum lipid profile (fasting sample).

Ref: Davidson's 21st/451–456

11 · Lipoprotein — Structure & Classification

❓ What is a lipoprotein? Describe its structure and classification.

Lipoprotein: a complex lipid made up of lipid + protein (called apolipoproteins or apoproteins) — the transport vehicle for lipids in blood.

Structure of a lipoprotein

  1. Lipid core: contains non-polar triglyceride (TAG) and cholesteryl ester.
  2. Single surface layer: phospholipid, free cholesterol, and apoproteins. Phospholipid and cholesterol are oriented so their polar groups face outward to the aqueous medium, making the particle water-soluble.

Constituents of a lipoprotein

  1. Triacylglycerol (TAG)
  2. Phospholipid
  3. Free cholesterol
  4. Cholesteryl ester
  5. Apoprotein

Classification — Four major groups

LipoproteinOther Name
1. Chylomicrons—
2. Very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL)Pre-β-lipoproteins
3. Low-density lipoproteins (LDL)β-lipoproteins
4. High-density lipoproteins (HDL)α-lipoproteins

12 · Functions of Lipoproteins

❓ What are the functions of each class of lipoprotein?

Since lipids are insoluble in water, they cannot travel alone in blood plasma. Lipoproteins are water-soluble and act as transport vehicles for lipids.

LipoproteinOriginMain LipidFunction
ChylomicronsIntestineTAGTransport exogenous (dietary) TAG from intestine to liver & peripheral tissue
VLDLLiverTAGTransport endogenous (liver-synthesised) TAG to peripheral tissue; precursor of LDL
LDLFrom VLDL in bloodCholesterolTransport cholesterol from liver → peripheral tissues
HDLLiver & intestinePhospholipidsTransport cholesterol from peripheral tissues → liver (reverse cholesterol transport) for degradation & excretion

Clinical importance of lipoprotein function

  • High LDL → increased cholesterol deposition in arterial wall → atherosclerosis.
  • High HDL → removes cholesterol from peripheral tissues → prevents atherosclerosis.
  • Imbalance in rate of TG formation in liver vs VLDL secretion → fatty liver.

Ref: Harper's 28th/213–215; Lippincott's 5th/227–228

14 · Atherogenic vs Anti-Atherogenic Lipoproteins

❓ Name the atherogenic and anti-atherogenic lipoproteins.

Atherogenic lipoproteins (promote atherosclerosis)

  • VLDL (Very-Low-Density Lipoprotein)
  • LDL (Low-Density Lipoprotein)
  • VLDL remnant
  • Chylomicron remnant
  • Lp(a) — Lipoprotein(a)

Anti-atherogenic lipoprotein (protects against atherosclerosis)

  • HDL (High-Density Lipoprotein)

15 · LDL — Low-Density Lipoprotein

❓ Write a short note on LDL. What is its significance?

LDL is one of the four major groups of lipoproteins, representing a final stage in the catabolism of VLDL.

PropertyDetail
Synonymβ-lipoprotein
Origin/sourceFrom VLDL (in plasma)
Diameter20–25 nm
Density1.019–1.063 g/mL
CompositionProtein 21%, Lipid 79%

Lipid components: high concentration of cholesterol, phospholipid & cholesteryl ester; low concentration of triacylglycerol (TAG).

Function & Clinical Significance

  • LDL transports cholesterol from liver → peripheral tissues.
  • It may be responsible for atherosclerosis, which may lead to:
    • Myocardial infarction (MI)
    • Stroke (cerebral infarction)
    • Peripheral arterial thrombosis → gangrene
  • So, increased LDL is harmful to our body.

Ref: Lippincott's 3rd/229–232; Harper's 28th/212–219

16 · HDL — High-Density Lipoprotein

❓ Write a short note on HDL. Why is it called "good cholesterol"?

HDL is a lipoprotein having the highest density.

PropertyDetail
Synonymα-lipoprotein
OriginLiver & intestine
Main lipid componentPhospholipid
ApoproteinsA, C, E, A-I
Blood level> 40 mg/dL

Subfractions

  • HDL₂ — Cholesterol
  • HDL₃ — Cholesteryl ester
  • Pre-β-HDL — TAG

Role of HDL-Cholesterol

  • HDL transports cholesterol from peripheral tissues → liver (Reverse Cholesterol Transporter).
  • In the liver, this cholesterol can be:
    • Repackaged into lipoproteins
    • Converted into bile acids
    • Secreted into the bile in unchanged form for removal from the body
  • More HDL-C = less cholesterol deposition in peripheral tissue = less chance of atherosclerosis.
  • HDL is therefore known as anti-atherogenic or "good cholesterol".

Clinical Significance

  • A high level of HDL-C seems to protect against cardiovascular diseases.
  • Low HDL-C (< 40 mg/dL) increases the risk for heart disease.
  • HDL particle is considered as protection to the body's cardiovascular health.

Ref: Harper's 29th/242; Lippincott's 5th/234–237

❓ What is good and bad cholesterol? Why are they called so?

HDL is "good cholesterol" because HDL removes cholesterol from peripheral tissues to the liver, where it is metabolised and excreted in bile — thus it reduces tissue atherogenicity.

LDL is "bad cholesterol" because LDL carries cholesterol from the liver to the peripheral tissues — thus it increases tissue atherogenicity.

Ref: Davidson's 21st/451
