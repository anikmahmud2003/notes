# Ch 07 — Liver and Biliary Tract     <i>Full-detail flashcards (Q→A) · All abbreviations expanded · Robbins 4-pillar answers</i>
### 🧠 Big Picture — Liver & Biliary Tract (boss-level overview)
The liver is the body's metabolic factory — it detoxifies, synthesises proteins, stores glycogen, produces bile, and regulates lipid/carbohydrate metabolism. When 80–90% of functional capacity is lost, <b>liver failure</b> ensues (acute or chronic).
The unifying thread: <b>any chronic liver injury</b> (viral hepatitis B/C, alcohol, fat, autoimmune, metabolic) → <b>inflammation → fibrosis → cirrhosis</b> (regenerative nodules surrounded by fibrous bands) → <b>portal hypertension</b> (ascites, varices, splenomegaly) → <b>decompensation</b> (jaundice, encephalopathy, coagulopathy) → <b>death or hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)</b>.
Key classification (VIVA favourite): <b>1.</b> Liver failure (acute/chronic/acute-on-chronic) · <b>2.</b> Infectious disorders (viral hepatitis A–E, bacterial, parasitic) · <b>3.</b> Autoimmune hepatitis · <b>4.</b> Drug/toxin-induced injury · <b>5.</b> Fatty liver disease (alcoholic + NAFLD/NASH) · <b>6.</b> Inherited diseases (haemochromatosis, Wilson disease, α₁-antitrypsin deficiency) · <b>7.</b> Cholestatic diseases (PBC, PSC) · <b>8.</b> Circulatory disorders (Budd-Chiari) · <b>9.</b> Tumours (benign + malignant) · <b>10.</b> Gallbladder & biliary tract diseases (gallstones, cholecystitis, carcinoma).

## 1 · Overview — Diseases of the Liver
<b>🔴 Name the diseases of the liver. Classify hepatic tumours/neoplasms.</b>

<b>Important pathologic lesions of the liver:</b>

Liver failure: Acute liver failure · Chronic liver failure and cirrhosis · Portal hypertension (PHT) · Acute-on-chronic liver failure
Infectious disorders: Viral hepatitis (hepatotrophic viruses: HAV, HBV, HCV, HDV, HEV; non-hepatotrophic: EBV, CMV, HSV, adenovirus, yellow fever) · Bacterial, fungal, and parasitic infections
Autoimmune hepatitis
Drug- and toxin-induced liver injury
Fatty liver disease: Alcoholic liver disease · Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)
Inherited liver disease: Haemochromatosis · Wilson disease · α₁-antitrypsin deficiency
Cholestatic disease: Physiologic jaundice of the newborn · Hereditary hyperbilirubinaemia · Cholestasis of sepsis · Primary hepatolithiasis · Neonatal cholestasis · Autoimmune cholangiopathies · Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) · Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)
Circulatory disorders: Portal vein obstruction & thrombosis · Hepatic vein thrombosis (Budd-Chiari syndrome)
Hepatic disease associated with pregnancy: Pre-eclampsia & eclampsia · Acute fatty liver of pregnancy · Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy
Tumours (Very Important for VIVA):
Benign: Cavernous haemangioma · Hepatocellular adenoma
Malignant (primary): Hepatoblastoma (childhood) · Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) · Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma · Combined hepatocellular-cholangiocarcinoma · Angiosarcoma · Epithelioid haemangioendothelioma · Primary hepatic lymphoma (NHL)
Metastatic (more common than primary): Colon, breast, lung, pancreas, leukaemias, melanomas, lymphomas

Robbins 10e pp.822–872

<b>🔴 Mention the patterns of hepatic injury.</b>

<b>1. Reversible injury:</b> Accumulation of fat (steatosis) and bilirubin (cholestasis), as well as <b>ballooning</b> — cell swelling, cytoplasmic clearing, and clumping of intermediate filaments, which when prominent may form <b>Mallory hyaline</b>.

<b>2. Irreversible injury:</b> Hepatocytes die by <b>necrosis</b> or <b>apoptosis</b>.

Robbins 10e p.824

<b>🔴 Name the inflammatory liver diseases. What are the causes of hepatitis?</b>

<b>A. Infectious disorders:</b>

Viral hepatitis: Hepatotrophic viruses (HAV, HBV, HCV, HDV, HEV); Non-hepatotrophic viruses (EBV, CMV, HSV, adenovirus, yellow fever virus — often in association with systemic infection)
Bacterial infections: Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella typhi, T. pallidum, Bartonella henselae, M. tuberculosis
Parasitic & helminthic: Malaria, schistosomiasis, strongyloidiasis, cryptosporidiosis, leishmaniasis, echinococcosis, liver flukes (Fasciola hepatica, Opisthorchis spp., Clonorchis sinensis), hydatid cyst, amebic liver abscess
Fungal: Histoplasmosis, candidiasis

<b>B. Autoimmune hepatitis</b>

<b>Other causes of hepatitis:</b> Drugs (acetaminophen, isoniazid, α-methyldopa, methotrexate), toxins (aflatoxin/mushroom poisoning), alcohol, metabolic (NAFLD, haemochromatosis, Wilson disease, α₁-antitrypsin deficiency, neonatal hepatitis), cholestasis (biliary stricture, sclerosing cholangitis), congestion (CCF, Budd-Chiari syndrome).

Robbins 10e pp.823–839

## 2 · Liver Failure
<b>🔴 Short note: Liver failure. Mention clinical features and complications.</b>

<b>Liver failure:</b> The most severe form of liver disease — may be <b>acute</b> (sudden massive hepatic destruction) or (more commonly) <b>chronic</b>, following years of insidious, progressive liver injury. <b>Acute-on-chronic liver failure</b> occurs when an unrelated acute injury is superimposed on late-stage chronic disease.

<b>80–90% of functional capacity must be lost before hepatic failure appears.</b>

Clinical features

Jaundice
Hypoalbuminaemia (peripheral oedema)
Hyperammonaemia (cerebral dysfunction)
Fetor hepaticus (body odour "musty" or "sweet and sour")
Palmar erythema (local vasodilatation)
Spider angiomas of the skin
In males — hypogonadism and gynaecomastia

Complications

Coagulopathy
Hepatic encephalopathy
Portal hypertension
Bleeding oesophageal varices
Hepatorenal syndrome
Portopulmonary hypertension

🎯 Without liver transplantation, mortality in hepatic failure is ~80%.

Robbins 10e pp.826, 831

<b>🔴 Define acute liver failure. Mention causes (ABCDE mnemonic) and clinical features.</b>

<b>Acute (fulminant) liver failure:</b> An acute liver illness associated with <b>encephalopathy and coagulopathy</b> that occurs within <b>26 weeks</b> of the initial liver injury <b>in the absence of pre-existing liver disease</b>.

Causes — ABCDE Mnemonic

A: Acetaminophen, hepatitis A, autoimmune hepatitis
B: Hepatitis B
C: Hepatitis C, cryptogenic
D: Drugs/toxins, hepatitis D
E: Hepatitis E, esoteric causes (Wilson disease, Budd-Chiari syndrome)
F: Fatty change of the microvesicular type (fatty liver of pregnancy, valproate, tetracycline, Reye syndrome)

Rare causes: abnormalities of blood flow, metabolic disorders, malignancies (leukaemia/lymphoma 33%, breast cancer 30%, colon cancer 7%). Aetiology cannot be established in ~15% adults and ~50% paediatric cases.

Clinical features & complications

Nausea, vomiting, and jaundice
Marked elevation of serum liver transaminases
Initial liver enlargement followed by shrinkage
Cholestasis — yellow discoloration of skin and sclera (jaundice and icterus)
Hepatic encephalopathy
Clotting factor deficiencies and coagulopathy — easy bruisability, life-threatening intracranial bleeding, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
Portal hypertension (more commonly with chronic liver failure)
Hepatorenal syndrome
Progressive multiorgan system failure → death (if transplantation not possible)

Robbins 10e pp.826, 831

<b>🔴 Short note: Hepatic encephalopathy. (Very Important for VIVA)</b>

<b>Hepatic encephalopathy:</b> A spectrum of disturbances in consciousness, ranging from subtle behavioural abnormalities to marked confusion and stupor, to deep coma and death. May progress over days, weeks, or months following acute hepatic injury.

Associated fluctuating neurologic signs

Rigidity and hyperreflexia
Asterixis / flapping tremor (a particularly characteristic sign — nonrhythmic, rapid extension-flexion movements of the head and extremities, best seen when arms are held in extension with dorsiflexed wrists)

Pathogenesis

Caused by <b>elevated ammonia levels</b>, which correlate with impaired neuronal function and cerebral oedema. The principal source of ammonia is the <b>gastrointestinal tract</b> (produced by microorganisms and enterocytes during glutamine metabolism). Normally, ammonia is transported in the portal vein to the liver, where it is metabolised in the urea cycle; in severe liver disease, this detoxification mechanism fails.

Robbins 10e p.827

<b>🔴 Short note: Hepatorenal syndrome. (Very Important for VIVA)</b>

<b>Hepatorenal syndrome:</b> A form of <b>renal failure</b> occurring in individuals with liver failure in whom there is <b>no intrinsic morphologic or functional cause</b> for kidney dysfunction.

Onset is marked by a drop in urine output and increasing levels of urea and creatinine in the blood. Sodium retention, impaired free-water excretion, and decreased renal perfusion and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) are the main renal functional abnormalities.

The decline in renal function is reversible if liver function is restored — e.g. by liver transplantation.

Robbins 10e p.827

## 3 · Viral Hepatitis
<b>🔴 What are hepatotrophic viruses?</b>

<b>Hepatotrophic viruses:</b> The group of viruses (<b>HAV, HBV, HCV, HDV, HEV</b>) that have a <b>specific affinity for the liver</b>.

<b>Why are they named so? Enumerate them.</b>

<b>HAV:</b> Spread by ingestion of contaminated water/foods; endemic in countries with poor sanitation. Sexual transmission may occur but maternal-fetal transmission does not. Blood-borne transmission is rare (viremia is transient). 99% recover within 3 months; disease resolution in nearly all by 6 months. Acute liver failure in 0.1–0.3%. Extrahepatic manifestations include rash, arthralgia, leucocytoclastic vasculitis, glomerulonephritis, cryoglobulinaemia.

Robbins 10e pp.831, 832, 836

<b>🔴 Give the possible outcomes of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection.</b>

Acute hepatitis followed by recovery and clearance of the virus (99% of acute hepatitis in adults)
Death from fulminant hepatitis (<1%)
Chronic hepatitis with or without progression to cirrhosis
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)
An asymptomatic "healthy" carrier state

<b>Patients with long-standing HBV- or HCV-related cirrhosis are at increased risk for HCC.</b>

Robbins 10e pp.832, 838; Park 23e p.215

<b>🔴 Mention the serologic markers of HBV infection.</b>

<b>a. Antigens:</b> HBsAg (hepatitis B surface antigen), HBeAg (hepatitis B e antigen)

<b>b. Antibodies:</b> Anti-HBc IgM (anti-hepatitis B core IgM), Anti-HBs (anti-hepatitis B surface), Anti-HBe IgM

<b>Chronic hepatitis serological markers:</b>

Hepatitis B: HBsAg (+), Anti-HBc IgM (+), Anti-HBe IgM (+)
Hepatitis C: Anti-HCV IgM & IgG Ab
Hepatitis D: Anti-HDV IgM & IgG Ab

Robbins 10e p.833

<b>🔴 Give the clinicopathologic syndromes of viral hepatitis.</b>

Acute asymptomatic infection with recovery
Acute symptomatic infection (anicteric or icteric) with recovery
Acute liver failure with massive to submassive hepatic necrosis (~10% of cases)
Chronic hepatitis (hepatic disease for >6 months), with or without progression to cirrhosis
Carrier state

Robbins 10e pp.836–837

<b>🔴 Write down the morphologic changes in acute viral hepatitis (macroscopic + microscopic).</b>

<b>Macroscopic:</b>

Mild acute viral hepatitis: Liver may be normal in size or enlarged (inflammation)
Acute liver failure with massive necrosis: Liver may be shrunken

<b>Microscopic:</b>

Portal and lobular inflammatory infiltrate — predominantly lymphocytes, variably admixed with plasma cells and eosinophils
Hepatocyte injury → necrosis or apoptosis, with pigmented macrophages scavenging dead cell debris
Confluent necrosis (necrosis of groups of hepatocytes) in severe cases → may progress to panlobular (panacinar) necrosis or bridging necrosis (connecting vascular structures)
Liver failure can develop with massive hepatic necrosis

Robbins 10e p.837

<b>🔴 Discuss chronic viral hepatitis: causes, pathogenesis, morphology, clinical features, and complications.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Symptomatic, biochemical, or serological evidence of continuing or relapsing hepatic disease for <b>>6 months</b>.

Causes

Hepatitis viruses: HBV, HCV, HBV+HDV
Chronic alcoholism
Wilson disease
α₁-antitrypsin deficiency
Drugs: isoniazid, α-methyldopa, methotrexate
Autoimmunity

Pathogenesis

Continued loss of hepatocytes due to marked inflammatory infiltrate in portal tract and hepatic parenchyma
Progressive fibrosis

Morphology

<b>Macroscopic:</b> Liver architecture is usually well preserved.

<b>Microscopic:</b>

Mononuclear portal infiltration
Inflammatory cells cross the limiting plate → injury of periportal hepatocytes (interface activity)
Variable degree of lobular inflammation
Fibrosis develops → initially portal and periportal → fibrous septa → portoportal bridging fibrosis → eventually cirrhosis

Clinical features

Fatigue, malaise, loss of appetite, occasional bouts of mild jaundice, spider angiomas, palmar erythema, mild hepatomegaly, vasculitis/glomerulonephritis/cryoglobulinaemia (in chronic HBV/HCV).

Laboratory findings

Persistent elevation of serum transaminases (ALT/AST), prolongation of prothrombin time (PT), hyperglobulinaemia, hyperbilirubinaemia, mild elevation of alkaline phosphatase (ALP).

Complications

Cirrhosis of liver
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)

Robbins 10e p.837

<b>🔴 What is fulminant hepatitis / fulminant liver failure?</b>

<b>Fulminant hepatitis / fulminant liver failure:</b> Very rapid onset acute liver failure, typically the result of <b>massive hepatic necrosis</b> most often induced by drugs or toxins.

<b>Causes:</b>

Accidental or deliberate ingestion of acetaminophen (paracetamol) — accounts for almost 50% of adult cases
Autoimmune hepatitis
Other drugs/toxins
Acute hepatitis A, B, and E infections

Robbins 10e p.826

<b>What is carrier state of hepatitis?</b>

<b>Carrier state:</b> An individual who harbours and can transmit an organism but has no symptoms. For hepatotropic viruses, two scenarios:

Individuals who harbour the virus but have no liver disease
Individuals who harbour the virus and have asymptomatic nonprogressive liver damage

For HBV: "healthy carrier" = individual with HBsAg and anti-HBe, but without HBeAg. Normal aminotransferases, low or undetectable serum HBV DNA, and lack of significant inflammation or injury on biopsy.

Robbins 10e p.837

<b>What alterations occur in liver function tests (LFTs) in acute viral hepatitis?</b>

Serum bilirubin: High
ALT & AST: Very high
Alkaline phosphatase: Slightly high
Viral markers: Positive

## 4 · Cirrhosis of Liver
<b>🔴 Define chronic liver disease (CLD). Define cirrhosis of liver. (Very Important for VIVA)</b>

<b>Chronic liver disease (CLD):</b> A series of liver disorders of varying causes and severity in which hepatic inflammation and necrosis continue for <b>at least 6 months</b>. Or: hepatic injury, inflammation and/or fibrosis occurring for >6 months.

<b>Cirrhosis of liver:</b> The <b>end-stage of chronic liver disease</b> characterised by:

Bridging fibrous septae — delicate bands or broad scars linking portal tracts with each other and with terminal hepatic veins
Parenchymal nodules containing proliferating hepatocytes encircled by fibrosis, ranging from micronodules (<3 mm) to macronodules (several cm)
Disruption of the architecture of the entire liver

🎯 Although cirrhosis and chronic liver failure are often associated, they are <b>not synonymous</b> — not all cirrhosis leads to chronic liver failure, and not all end-stage chronic liver disease is cirrhotic.

Robbins 10e p.828; Robbins 11e (South Asia) p.536

<b>🔴 Classify cirrhosis of liver (etiological + morphological).</b>

Etiological classification

Alcoholic liver disease (60–70% in Western countries)
Viral hepatitis: Chronic HBV & HCV infection — 10% (most common in Bangladesh)
Biliary cirrhosis: 5–10%
Primary haemochromatosis: 5%
Wilson disease: Rare
α₁-antitrypsin deficiency: Rare
Cryptogenic cirrhosis: 10–15% (cause not found)
Drug-induced cirrhosis (e.g. α-methyldopa)
Cardiac cirrhosis: Due to long-standing heart failure

Morphological classification

<b>🔴 Mention the etiology/causes/risk factors of cirrhosis of liver.</b>

Chronic viral hepatitis (B or C) — most common cause in Bangladesh
Alcohol — most common in Western countries
Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)
Immune: Primary sclerosing cholangitis, autoimmune hepatitis
Biliary: Primary biliary cirrhosis (PBC), secondary biliary cirrhosis, cystic fibrosis
Genetic: Haemochromatosis, Wilson disease, α₁-antitrypsin deficiency
Cryptogenic cirrhosis (unknown — 15%)
Chronic venous outflow obstruction
Any chronic liver disease

Robbins 10e p.828; Davidson's 23e p.866

<b>🔴 Describe the pathogenesis of cirrhosis of liver.</b>

The central pathogenic processes are: <b>death of hepatocytes, extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, and vascular reorganisation</b>.

Chronic inflammation of liver → inflammatory cytokines (TNF, lymphotoxin, TGF-β, IL-1, PDGF) produced by stimulated Kupffer cells, endothelial cells, hepatocytes, and bile duct epithelium
Transformation of ITO cells (peri-sinusoidal stellate cells) into myofibroblast-like cells (collagen-secreting cells)
Collagen deposition (type I & III) in the space of Disse → fibrotic septal tracts
Vascular architecture disrupted by parenchymal damage and scarring
Continued collagen deposition → loss of fenestrations of sinusoidal endothelial cells
Eventual fibrosis → remaining hepatocytes stimulated to regenerate → proliferate as spherical nodules within fibrous septa
Result: fibrosis + loss of architecture + regenerative nodules = CIRRHOSIS

Robbins 10e p.828

<b>Why is there jaundice in cirrhosis?</b>

Disruption of the interface between the parenchyma and portal tracts may obliterate biliary channels, leading to the development of jaundice.

Robbins 9e p.828

<b>🔴 Discuss the pathology/morphology of cirrhosis of liver (gross + microscopic).</b>

Macroscopic

Liver is enlarged in early stage; in late stage becomes smaller (loss of cells and fibrous contraction)
Liver is much firmer than normal
Nodularity is the most characteristic feature

Microscopic

Loss of normal liver architecture
Regenerative nodules found within fibrous bands
Enlargement of liver cells with atypical nuclei
Vasculature greatly distorted
Fibrous bands obstruct portal venous radicles → abnormal communication between portal vein and hepatic arteriole

Robbins 10e p.828

<b>🔴 Enumerate the clinical features of cirrhosis of liver.</b>

1. Features of portal hypertension

Splenomegaly
Ascites & oedema
Collateral vessel formation (e.g. caput medusae)
Variceal bleeding (oesophageal, gastric & rectal varices)
Fetor hepaticus (portosystemic shunting)

2. Features of hepatic insult

Jaundice
Ascites
Circulatory changes: Spider naevi / spider angioma / spider telangiectasia, palmar erythema, cyanosis
Endocrine changes: ↓Libido, ↓body hair (both sexes). In males: gynaecomastia, testicular atrophy, impotence. In females: breast atrophy, menstrual abnormality, amenorrhoea
Haemorrhagic tendency: Bruises, purpura, epistaxis
Hepatic (portosystemic) encephalopathy
Other: Pruritus (itching), pigmentation, clubbing of fingers and toes, Dupuytren's contracture

🎯 Spider telangiectasia, gynaecomastia, and parotid enlargement are most common in <b>alcoholic cirrhosis</b>.

Davidson's 23e p.867; Robbins 10e p.828

<b>🔴 Mention the complications/causes of death in cirrhosis of liver (features of decompensated liver disease).</b>

Jaundice
Ascites
Hepatic encephalopathy
Bleeding from oesophageal varices
Hepatorenal syndrome
Hepatopulmonary syndrome
Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)
Development of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)

🎯 Chronic liver failure develops when the metabolic capacity of the liver is exceeded.

Davidson's 23e p.867; Robbins 10e p.828; Harsh Mohan 7e p.588

## 5 · Portal Hypertension
<b>🔴 What are the causes of portal hypertension? (Prehepatic, intrahepatic, posthepatic)</b>

<b>1. Prehepatic causes:</b>

Obstructive thrombosis of portal vein
Structural abnormalities (narrowing of portal vein before it ramifies in liver)

<b>2. Intrahepatic causes:</b>

Cirrhosis from any cause
Nodular regenerative hyperplasia
Primary biliary cirrhosis (even without cirrhosis)
Schistosomiasis
Massive fatty change
Diffuse fibrosing granulomatous disease (e.g. sarcoidosis)
Infiltrative malignancy (primary or metastatic)
Focal malignancy with portal vein invasion (particularly HCC)
Amyloidosis

<b>3. Posthepatic causes:</b>

Severe right-sided heart failure
Constrictive pericarditis
Hepatic vein outflow obstruction

Robbins 10e pp.828, 829

<b>🔴 What are the complications/clinical consequences of portal hypertension?</b>

The <b>four major clinical consequences</b> of portal hypertension are:

Ascites
Formation of porto-systemic venous shunts: haemorrhoids, oesophageal varices, periumbilical caput medusae
Congestive splenomegaly
Hepatic encephalopathy

Robbins 10e p.829

## 6 · Ascites in Cirrhosis

<b>🔴 Describe the mechanism/pathogenesis of ascites in cirrhosis of liver.</b>

<b>1. Splanchnic vasodilatation:</b> Cirrhosis (fibrosis) → pronounced splanchnic vasodilatation → falling systemic arterial pressure → reduced renal blood flow → activation of renin-angiotensin system with secondary hyperaldosteronism → ↑Na⁺ and H₂O retention → ↑plasma hydrostatic pressure → transudation of fluid into peritoneal cavity → ascites.

<b>2. Percolation of hepatic lymph:</b> Cirrhosis → hepatic lymphatic flow (20 L/day vs normal 800–1000 ml/day) exceeds thoracic duct return capacity → percolation of excess hepatic lymph (rich in proteins) into peritoneal cavity → ascites.

<b>3. Intestinal fluid leakage:</b> Cirrhosis → portal hypertension → increased perfusion pressure in intestinal capillaries → additional fluid movement into abdomen → ascites.

<b>4. Secondary hyperaldosteronism:</b> Cirrhosis + impaired hepatic metabolism of aldosterone → ↑aldosterone → ↑Na⁺ and H₂O retention → ↑plasma hydrostatic pressure → transudation → ascites.

<b>5. Hypoalbuminaemia:</b> Cirrhosis → impaired hepatic function → decreased albumin synthesis → hypoalbuminaemia → decreased plasma oncotic pressure → transudation → ascites.

Robbins 10e p.829; Davidson's 23e p.863

<b>Why does hypoproteinaemic oedema occur in cirrhosis of liver?</b>

The liver is the principal site of protein (mainly albumin) synthesis. In cirrhosis, the normal architecture is altered, hepatocytes undergo necrosis and apoptosis. Protein (albumin) synthesis is decreased → plasma oncotic pressure is decreased → hypoproteinaemic oedema.

Robbins 10e p.829; Davidson's 23e p.863

## 7 · Alcoholic Liver Disease
<b>🔴 Give an outline of the spectrum of liver diseases associated with alcohol abuse.</b>

<b>Alcoholic liver disease:</b> A chronic disorder giving rise to steatosis, alcoholic hepatitis, progressive steato-fibrosis, and marked derangement of vascular perfusion leading eventually to cirrhosis.

Excessive alcohol (ethanol) consumption is a major cause of liver disease in Western countries. Threshold: <b>≥80 g/day</b> (may be lower in women).

Three overlapping forms of alcoholic liver injury

Steatosis (fatty change)
Alcoholic steatohepatitis
Fibrosis → cirrhosis

<b>Only 10–15% of alcoholics develop cirrhosis.</b>

Factors influencing development

Gender: Women more susceptible (though majority of patients are men)
Ethnic/genetic differences: Higher rates in African Americans vs Caucasians despite similar alcohol consumption; twin studies show strong family association
Co-morbid conditions: Iron overload, NASH, HCV/HBV infection synergise with alcohol

Laboratory findings

Elevated serum aminotransferases (AST:ALT ratio ≥2:1)
Hyperbilirubinaemia
Variable elevation of serum ALP
Hypoproteinaemia (globulins, albumin, clotting factors)
Anaemia

Morphology

<b>Macroscopic:</b> Fatty liver is enlarged (4–6 kg), soft, yellow, and greasy. Steatosis may be microvesicular or macrovesicular.

<b>Microscopic:</b> <b>Ballooned hepatocytes</b> (injured, swollen, cleared cytoplasm, cytoskeletal damage → <b>Mallory hyaline</b>), inflammation and necrosis, <b>perivenular/pericellular fibrosis</b>.

Common proximate causes of death in advanced disease

Hepatic coma
Massive gastrointestinal haemorrhage
Inter-current infection
Hepatorenal syndrome
Hepatocellular carcinoma (1–6% annually)

Robbins 10e pp.842–845

<b>🔴 Short note: Haemochromatosis. (Important for VIVA)</b>

<b>Haemochromatosis:</b> Caused by excessive iron absorption, most deposited in the <b>liver and pancreas</b>, followed by heart, joints, and endocrine organs.

<b>🔴 Short note: Wilson disease (hepato-lenticular degeneration). (Important for VIVA)</b>

<b>Wilson disease:</b> An <b>autosomal recessive</b> disorder caused by mutation of the <b>ATP7B gene</b>, resulting in impaired copper excretion into bile and a failure to incorporate copper into <b>ceruloplasmin</b>. Marked by accumulation of toxic levels of copper in many tissues, principally <b>liver, brain, and eye</b>.

Pathogenesis

Normal initial steps of Cu absorption and transport to liver. Deficiency of ATP7B → decreased copper transport into bile, impaired incorporation into ceruloplasmin, inhibited ceruloplasmin secretion → ↓circulating ceruloplasmin + accumulation of copper in hepatocytes.

Excess hepatic copper causes toxic injury by three mechanisms:

Promoting free radical formation by the Fenton reaction
Binding to sulphhydryl groups of cellular proteins
Displacing other metals from hepatic metalloenzymes

Clinical features

Age of onset: 6–40 years (average 11.4 years)
Neurologic: movement disorders (tremor, poor coordination, chorea, dystonia, masklike facies, rigidity, gait disturbances)
Eye: Kayser-Fleischer (K-F) rings — green to brown deposits of copper in Descemet membrane in the limbus of the cornea
Psychiatric symptoms
Haemolytic anaemia (free copper damages red cell membranes)

Diagnostic tests

↓Serum ceruloplasmin
↑Hepatic copper content (most sensitive and accurate test)
↑Urinary copper excretion (most specific test)
Sequencing of ATP7B gene
Demonstration of K-F rings by slit-lamp examination

Treatment

Early recognition + long-term copper chelation (D-penicillamine or trientine) or zinc-based therapy (blocks dietary copper uptake). Liver transplantation can be curative in severe cases.

Robbins 10e pp.850–851

## 11 · Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC)
<b>🔴 Write down the etiology of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Give its morphology and clinical features.</b>

<b>HCC:</b> Most primary liver cancers arise from hepatocytes. Accounts for ~5.4% of all cancers worldwide. <b>Pronounced male predominance (up to 8:1).</b>

Causes / risk factors

Chronic viral hepatitis (B and C)
Hereditary haemochromatosis
α₁-antitrypsin deficiency
Alcoholic liver disease
Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease
Aflatoxin (mycotoxin from Aspergillus species)
Wilson disease
Chronic biliary diseases

Most HCCs occur in the setting of chronic liver disease with cirrhosis; 15–20% arise in noncirrhotic livers.

Clinical features

Abdominal pain, malaise, fatigue, weight loss, hepatomegaly, features of underlying chronic liver disease.

Investigations

Elevated serum α-fetoprotein (AFP)
Ultrasonography, CT scan, MRI with contrast
Triphasic CT scan — early enhancement in arterial phase followed by rapid venous washout (diagnostic of HCC)
Biopsy followed by histopathology

Morphology

<b>Gross:</b> Single mass, multiple discrete masses, or diffuse infiltration. Pale-yellow (fatty change) or green (cholestasis).

<b>Microscopic:</b> Well to highly anaplastic. Tumour cells grow in <b>thick plates/trabeculae, pseudoglandular structures with bile plugs, or sheets</b>. Tumours >2 cm more likely to have vascular invasion and intrahepatic metastases. Haematogenous metastases (especially lung) occur late. Lymph node metastases <5%.

Prognosis

5-year survival: 30% for tumour confined to liver; only 5–10% with extrahepatic spread.

Cause of death

Cachexia, GI/variceal bleeding, liver failure with hepatic coma, rupture with fatal haemorrhage (rare).

Robbins 10e p.868

## 12 · Liver Abscess
<b>🔴 Short note: Liver abscess.</b>

<b>Liver abscess:</b> A form of liver infection common in developing countries.

<b>Cause:</b> Usually echinococcal and amebic infections; less commonly other protozoal/helminthic organisms. Most abscesses are pyogenic (complication of bacterial infection elsewhere).

Portals of entry

Portal vein (previously most common — appendicitis, diverticulitis, colitis)
Arterial supply
Ascending infection in biliary tract (ascending cholangitis)
Direct invasion from a nearby source
Penetrating injury

Now spread occurs primarily through biliary tree or arterial supply in immunocompromised patients.

Morphology

Solitary or multiple lesions; bacteremic spread → multiple small abscesses; direct extension/trauma → solitary large abscesses. Echinococcal infection shows characteristic cystic structure with laminated wall, hooklets, and calcification.

Clinical features

Fever, right upper quadrant pain, tender hepatomegaly, jaundice.

Management

Antibiotic therapy (smaller lesions), surgical drainage (larger lesions). Mortality with large abscesses: 30–90%; with early recognition, up to 80% survive.

## 14 · Gallstones (Cholelithiasis)
<b>🔴 Define gall stones. Write the pathogenesis of gall stone formation (cholesterol + pigment stones).</b>

<b>Gallstones:</b> Concretions produced in the gall bladder due to abnormal composition of bile. Supersaturation of bile with cholesterol or bilirubin salts promotes stone formation.

A) Pathogenesis of cholesterol stones

Cholesterol concentrations exceed the solubilising capacity of bile (supersaturation) — due to hepatocellular hypersecretion of cholesterol + gall bladder hypomotility
Cholesterol can no longer remain dispersed → nucleates into solid cholesterol monohydrate crystals
Hypersecretion of mucus in the gall bladder traps the nucleated crystals
Aggregation of nucleated crystals
Stone formation

B) Pathogenesis of pigment stones

Pigment gallstones are complex mixtures of abnormal insoluble calcium salts of unconjugated bilirubin along with inorganic calcium salts.

i) Infection of the biliary tract (E. coli, Ascaris lumbricoides, C. sinensis) → inhibits bilirubin glucuronide conjugation by release of microbial β-glucuronidase → unconjugated bilirubin → mixed with abnormal insoluble calcium salts → pigment stone formation
ii) Chronic haemolysis (any cause) → ↑excretion of conjugated bilirubin + promotion of unconjugated bilirubin → pigment stone formation

Robbins 10e p.873

## 15 · Acute & Chronic Cholecystitis
<b>🔴 Short note: Acute cholecystitis.</b>

<b>Acute cholecystitis:</b> Acute inflammation of the gall bladder, precipitated in <b>90% of cases by obstruction of the neck of the GB or cystic duct by a gallstone</b>.

Risk factors

Acute calculous cholecystitis (90%): Gallstones
Acute acalculous cholecystitis (10%): Sepsis with hypotension and multisystem organ failure, immunosuppression, major trauma and burns, diabetes mellitus, infections

Clinical features

Progressive right upper quadrant or epigastric pain (colicky / dull and constant) lasting >6 hours
Fever, anorexia, tachycardia, sweating, nausea, and vomiting

Laboratory tests

CBC: Leukocytosis
Serum bilirubin: Raised (suggests CBD obstruction)
Serum ALP: Raised

Definitive diagnosis: USG / CT scan of whole abdomen.

Robbins 10e p.875; BRS Pathology 5e p.255

<b>🔴 What is chronic cholecystitis? Mention morphological changes.</b>

<b>Chronic cholecystitis:</b> Chronic inflammation of the gallbladder due to chemical irritation from long-standing cholelithiasis, with or without superimposed bouts of acute cholecystitis.

Morphology

<b>Gross:</b>

GB may be contracted, normal size, or enlarged
Thickening of the wall (extensive fibrosis)
Serosa usually smooth and glistening
Subserosa and submucosa often thickened from fibrosis (mucosa generally preserved)
Occasionally lumen contains green-yellow mucoid bile and usually stones
On sectioning, wall variably thickened with opaque grey-white appearance

<b>Microscopic:</b>

Degree of inflammation variable
Mucosa and subserosal fibrous tissue infiltrated by chronic inflammatory cells (lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages)
Rokitansky-Aschoff sinuses (pseudodiverticula — outpouchings/herniation of GB mucosa into muscular wall)
Extensive dystrophic calcification within the wall → porcelain gallbladder (shrunken, hard GB due to chronic inflammation & fibrosis)
In xanthogranulomatous cholecystitis: atrophic, chronically obstructed, dilated GB with clear secretions (hydrops) + xanthoma cells (lipid-containing cells with foamy cytoplasm)

Robbins 10e p.876; BRS Pathology 5e p.255

<b>What are the potential complications of chronic cholecystitis?</b>

Bacterial superinfection with cholangitis or sepsis
Gallbladder perforation and local abscess formation
Gallbladder rupture with diffuse peritonitis
Biliary enteric (cholecystenteric) fistula → drainage of bile into adjacent organs, air/bacteria into biliary tree → gallstone-induced intestinal obstruction (ileus)
Aggravation of pre-existing medical illness (cardiac, pulmonary, renal, liver decompensation)

Robbins 10e p.876

## 16 · Carcinoma of Gall Bladder
<b>🔴 What type of carcinoma occurs in gall bladder? Give its morphology.</b>

<b>Type:</b> Most carcinomas of the GB are <b>adenocarcinomas</b> (lined by columnar epithelium). About 5% are squamous cell carcinoma or adenosquamous differentiation. Squamous cell carcinoma may occur in long-standing gallstone disease (gallstones cause squamous metaplasia). A minority may show carcinoid or mesenchymal features (carcinosarcoma).

Morphology

Two patterns of growth:

Infiltrating pattern (more common): Diffuse thickening and induration of GB wall. Deep ulceration → direct penetration or fistula formation to adjacent viscera.
Exophytic pattern: Grows into lumen as irregular cauliflower mass. Luminal portion may be necrotic, haemorrhagic, and ulcerated. Most common sites: fundus and neck; ~20% involves lateral walls.

Robbins 10e pp.876–877

All <b>True</b>. The "big four" risk factors for HCC. <i>MCQ: RU-14Ja</i>

In chronic liver disease: Prothrombin time is prolonged (T), Serum bilirubin is always raised (F), Serum ALP is raised (F), A:G ratio is altered (T), Serum albumin is decreased (T).

PT prolonged = <b>True</b> · Bilirubin always raised = <b>False</b> (may be normal in compensated cirrhosis) · ALP raised = <b>False</b> (usually mildly elevated) · A:G ratio altered = <b>True</b> (↓albumin, ↑globulins) · Albumin decreased = <b>True</b>. <i>MCQ: DU-13Ju</i>

Gallstones are associated with: Viral hepatitis (F), Cirrhosis of liver (F), Hereditary spherocytosis (T), Obesity (T), Sickle cell anaemia (T).

Hepatitis = <b>False</b> · Cirrhosis = <b>False</b> · Hereditary spherocytosis = <b>True</b> (chronic haemolysis → pigment stones) · Obesity = <b>True</b> (↑cholesterol → cholesterol stones) · Sickle cell anaemia = <b>True</b> (chronic haemolysis). <i>MCQs: DU-13Ju, DU-10Ja</i>

Obstruction of common bile duct is associated with: Great increase in ALP (T), Increase in serum unconjugated bilirubin (F), Decrease in faecal stercobilinogen (T), Increase in faecal fat (T), Increase in urinary urobilinogen (F).

ALP ↑ = <b>True</b> (obstructive pattern) · Unconjugated bilirubin ↑ = <b>False</b> (it's <i>conjugated</i> bilirubin that rises in obstructive jaundice) · Faecal stercobilinogen ↓ = <b>True</b> (bile not reaching intestine) · Faecal fat ↑ = <b>True</b> (malabsorption) · Urinary urobilinogen ↑ = <b>False</b> (decreased — no bilirubin reaching gut). <i>MCQ: DU-13Ju</i>

Decreased albumin-globulin (A:G) ratio is found in: Nephrotic syndrome (T), Lymphoma (F), Cirrhosis of liver (T).

Nephrotic syndrome = <b>True</b> (urinary albumin loss) · Lymphoma = <b>False</b> · Cirrhosis = <b>True</b> (↓albumin synthesis + ↑globulins). <i>MCQ: RU-13Ju</i>

Following are raised in chronic liver disease: Bleeding time (F), Clotting time (T), Serum bilirubin (T), Prothrombin time (T), Serum albumin (F — decreased, not raised).

Bleeding time = <b>False</b> · Clotting time = <b>True</b> (↑ due to ↓clotting factors) · Bilirubin = <b>True</b> · PT = <b>True</b> · Serum albumin = <b>False</b> (it's decreased). <i>MCQ: DU-11Ju</i>

🎯 One-liner: Most common cause of cirrhosis worldwide? Most common cause in Bangladesh? Most common cause in Western countries?

Worldwide: <b>Chronic viral hepatitis (HBV/HCV)</b> · Bangladesh: <b>Chronic HBV</b> · Western countries: <b>Alcohol</b>.

🎯 One-liner: What percentage of functional liver capacity must be lost before liver failure appears? What percentage of alcoholics develop cirrhosis?

<b>80–90%</b> of functional capacity must be lost · Only <b>10–15%</b> of alcoholics develop cirrhosis.

🎯 One-liner: What is the "golden" ratio of AST:ALT in alcoholic liver disease? What is the most sensitive test for Wilson disease? Most specific?

AST:ALT ratio <b>≥2:1</b> in alcoholic liver disease · Most sensitive for Wilson = <b>↑hepatic copper content</b> · Most specific = <b>↑urinary copper excretion</b>.

🎯 One-liner: What is Kayser-Fleischer ring? Where is it found? What is Rokitansky-Aschoff sinus? What is porcelain gallbladder?

K-F ring = <b>copper deposits in Descemet membrane of cornea</b> (Wilson disease) · Rokitansky-Aschoff sinus = <b>herniation of GB mucosa into muscular wall</b> (chronic cholecystitis) · Porcelain GB = <b>shrunken, hard gallbladder due to chronic inflammation + dystrophic calcification</b>.

🎯 One-liner: Most common site of gall bladder carcinoma? What type of stone is associated with squamous cell carcinoma of GB?

Most common site = <b>Fundus and neck</b> · Squamous cell carcinoma associated with <b>long-standing gallstones</b> (squamous metaplasia).

🎯 One-liner: What is caput medusae? What is fetor hepaticus? What is asterixis?

Caput medusae = <b>dilated periumbilical veins</b> (portosystemic shunting via paraumbilical veins) · Fetor hepaticus = <b>"musty/sweet and sour" body odour</b> (portosystemic shunting of volatile substances) · Asterixis = <b>flapping tremor</b> (hepatic encephalopathy — nonrhythmic extension-flexion movements of hands).
