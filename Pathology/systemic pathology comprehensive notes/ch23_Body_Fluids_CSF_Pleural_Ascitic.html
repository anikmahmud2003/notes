🧠 Big Picture — Body Fluids (CSF, Pleural, Ascitic)

Core concept: Body fluids occupy defined compartments — intracellular (ICF, ~28 L) and extracellular (ECF, ~14 L). ECF divides into interstitial fluid (11 L), plasma (3 L), and transcellular fluid (CSF, pleural, pericardial, peritoneal, synovial, intra-ocular). Laboratory examination of these fluids is a cornerstone of clinical pathology.

🔴 CSF: Collected by lumbar puncture (LP) between L3–L4. Normal values: pressure 50–250 mm H₂O, glucose 50–70 mg/dL (>50–60% of blood), protein 15–45 mg/dL, chloride 710–750 mg/dL, cells 0–4/mm³ (lymphocytes). Meningitis is the #1 indication — bacterial (pyogenic) vs viral vs tubercular vs fungal have distinct CSF profiles.

📌 Pleural fluid: Distinguishes transudate (low protein, e.g. heart failure, nephrotic, cirrhosis) from exudate (high protein, e.g. TB, pneumonia, malignancy). Tubercular effusion: lymphocytic, high ADA (>40 U/L). Malignant effusion: haemorrhagic, malignant cells on Pap smear.

📌 Ascitic fluid: Gross inspection + differential cell count + albumin assay. SAAG (Serum–Ascites Albumin Gradient) distinguishes portal-hypertensive from non-portal-hypertensive causes. Exudative findings: protein >25 g/L, elevated triglycerides, decreased glucose, increased amylase.

1 · Body Fluid Compartments & Commonly Examined Fluids

What are body fluids? Name the important body fluids commonly sent to the laboratory for examination.

Body fluid: The fluid present in different compartments of the body is called body fluid.

Fluid compartments of the body

CompartmentVolume% Body Weight
Total body fluid42 L~60%
Extracellular fluid (ECF)14 L~20% (⅓)
Intracellular fluid (ICF)28 L~40% (⅔)

ECF breakdown: Interstitial fluid (11 L) + Plasma (3 L). Interstitial fluid further divides into interstitial lymph fluid and transcellular fluid (~1 L).

Transcellular fluids include: cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), intra-ocular fluid, fluid in gastrointestinal (GI) tract, fluid in respiratory tract, and fluid in potential spaces — pleural fluid, pericardial fluid, peritoneal (ascitic) fluid, synovial fluid.

Commonly examined body fluids in clinical practice

  1. Plasma
  2. CSF
  3. Pleural fluid
  4. Peritoneal (ascitic) fluid
  5. Synovial fluid
  6. Pericardial fluid
  7. Hydrocele fluid
  8. Amniotic fluid
  9. Saliva and gastric juice
  10. Semen

2 · CSF Examination & Indications

🔴 What are the indications of CSF examination? Name 5 important indications.

Very Important for VIVA — Indications of CSF examination:

  1. Infections: meningitis, encephalitis
  2. Cerebral abscess
  3. Sub-arachnoid haemorrhage (SAH): if CT scan is negative or unavailable
  4. Inflammatory conditions: Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), neurosyphilis, acute poliomyelitis, multiple sclerosis, sarcoidosis, acute polyneuritis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)
  5. Infiltrative conditions / neurological malignancies: carcinomatous meningitis, lymphoma, leukaemia
  6. To confirm raised intracranial pressure: when CT scan excludes danger of brainstem herniation — benign intracranial hypertension (BIH), cerebral venous thrombosis

Ref: Davidson's 23rd/1077; Lecture SSMC; BSMMU Lab Medicine; AFIP Clinical Pathology

What is lumbar puncture? Give its indications (diagnostic + therapeutic) and contraindications.

Lumbar puncture (LP): A technique used to obtain both a CSF sample and an indirect measure of intracranial pressure.

A. Diagnostic indications

  1. Infections: meningitis, encephalitis
  2. Cerebral abscess
  3. Sub-arachnoid haemorrhage (if CT scan negative/unavailable)
  4. Inflammatory conditions: acute poliomyelitis, multiple sclerosis, sarcoidosis, acute polyneuritis, SLE, neurosyphilis
  5. Primary or secondary metastatic malignancies: carcinomatous meningitis, lymphoma, leukaemia
  6. Raised intracranial pressure: BIH, cerebral venous thrombosis

B. Therapeutic indications

  1. Administration of drugs: antibiotics (meningitis), antimitotics (oncology)
  2. Instillation of contrast media or isotopes: myelography, cisternography
  3. Spinal anaesthesia

Contraindications of lumbar puncture

  • Local tissue suppuration
  • Raised intracranial pressure
  • Suspected intracranial mass lesions
  • Patient on aspirin, heparin, warfarin therapy
  • Depressed level of consciousness
  • Bleeding disorder
  • Lack of cooperation

Complications of lumbar puncture

  • Infection
  • Brainstem herniation
  • Post-lumbar puncture headache
  • Injury to intervertebral disc
  • Bleeding due to injury to vertebral plexus
  • CSF leakage
  • Local pain
  • Shock
  • Transient ischaemic attack (TIA)
  • Vaso-vagal attack

Ref: Davidson's 23rd/1077; Lecture SSMC

How is CSF collected? Describe the steps of CSF collection.

Collection sites

  1. Lumbar puncture (usually between L3 and L4)
  2. Sub-occipital (cisternal) puncture
  3. Lateral cervical puncture
  4. Through ventricular cannulas or shunts

Steps of CSF collection

  1. Explain the procedure to the patient including potential complications
  2. CSF must be collected as aseptically as practicable
  3. The fluid is collected from the subarachnoid space of the spinal cord by a sterile lumbar puncture needle inserted into the L3–L4 intervertebral space under local anaesthesia
  4. CSF is also collected by cisternal puncture (sub-occipital puncture)
  5. Sterile plastic tube should be used
  6. Total 6–10 mL may be collected
  7. 4 samples in 4 sterile tubes:
TubePurpose
Tube 1Biochemical & immunological test
Tube 2Microbiological test
Tube 3Cell count and differentiation
Tube 4Cytology (if malignancy suspected)

NB: CSF examination must be done within 30–60 minutes after collection.

Ref: Prof. Khaleque/285; Lecture SSMC; DMC; BSMMU Lab Medicine

What are the tests done in CSF examination? Describe each briefly.

1. Physical examination

  • Quantity or volume
  • Colour
  • Appearance
  • Pressure
  • Specific gravity
  • Presence of blood
  • Clot formation (CSF is normally clear and colourless)

2. Cell count

Mainly lymphocytes (normally 0–5 lymphocytes/mm³).

3. Chemical examination

Protein, glucose & chloride levels.

4. Bacteriological examination

  • Staining: Gram stain for ordinary bacteria; Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) stain for tubercle bacilli
  • Culture & sensitivity: Chocolate agar media for ordinary bacteria; Lowenstein-Jensen (LJ) media for M. tuberculosis

5. Microbiological examination

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for herpes simplex or tuberculosis (TB).

6. Serological examination

  • Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) for syphilis
  • Complement fixation test (CFT)
  • Latex agglutination test

7. Cytological examination

To detect malignant cells.

4 · Clinical Significance of CSF Findings

State the clinical significance of low CSF sugar. What are the causes of high protein in CSF?

Clinical significance of low CSF sugar

  1. Useful to differentiate pyogenic meningitis from other meningitis — in pyogenic meningitis, CSF sugar is very low or absent
  2. Indicates that pyogenic organisms utilise sugar as food

Causes of high protein in CSF

Any inflammatory condition, such as:

  1. Pyogenic meningitis
  2. Tubercular meningitis
  3. Viral meningitis
  4. Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS)

Ref: Davidson's 23rd; Lecture SSMC

5 · Meningitis — Definition, Causes, Classification

🔴 What is meningitis? Classify meningitis. Give causes of meningitis (bacterial, viral, fungal, protozoal, non-infective).

Meningitis: Inflammation of the meninges.

A. Infective causes

A. Bacterial (mainly)

Age GroupCommon Organisms
First 6 weeks (neonatal)E. coli (mainly), Proteus, Group B streptococci, Listeria monocytogenes
6 weeks – 6 years (pre-school)Haemophilus influenzae, Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Older child & adultN. meningitidis, S. pneumoniae, L. monocytogenes, M. tuberculosis, Staphylococcus aureus, H. influenzae

B. Viral

  • Enteroviruses (echo, Coxsackie, polio)
  • Mumps virus
  • Influenza virus
  • Herpes simplex virus (HSV)
  • Varicella zoster virus (VZV)
  • Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)
  • Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)

C. Protozoa & parasites

  • Cysticerci
  • Amoeba

D. Fungal

  • Cryptococcus neoformans (mainly)
  • Candida
  • Histoplasma
  • Blastomyces
  • Coccidioides
  • Sporothrix

B. Non-infective ("sterile")

  • Malignant disease: breast cancer, bronchial cancer, leukaemia, lymphoma
  • Inflammatory disease: sarcoidosis, SLE, Behçet's disease

Ref: Davidson's 23rd/1118; Lecture SSMC

What is aseptic meningitis?

Aseptic meningitis: Usually refers to viral meningitis — CSF cultures are negative (sterile) but there is meningeal inflammation.

What do you mean by pleocytosis?

Pleocytosis: A term used to describe an increased cell count (Gk. pleion = more), usually referring to an increase in white blood cell (WBC) count in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). It is indicative of an inflammatory, infectious, or malignant condition.

7 · VIVA Corner on CSF Examination & Meningitis

Why does CSF look cloudy or turbid?

Cloudy/turbid CSF: Due to excess amount of protein and/or the presence of cells (neutrophils in pyogenic, lymphocytes in tubercular/viral).

Why is protein increased in all types of meningitis?

Why protein is increased in ALL types of meningitis: Because meningitis is an inflammation and in every inflammation there is exudate formation, which contains much more protein than normal CSF.

Why is CSF sugar markedly reduced in pyogenic meningitis?

Low glucose in pyogenic meningitis: Because pyogenic bacteria consume glucose as their food and energy source.

Why is glucose normal in viral meningitis?

Normal glucose in viral meningitis: Because viruses do not consume glucose.

🎯 Key VIVA point: CSF glucose = 60% of blood glucose. If blood glucose is very high (e.g. uncontrolled diabetes mellitus with blood glucose >200 mg/dL), CSF glucose can still be "high" (>120 mg/dL) even in pyogenic meningitis — because the baseline is elevated.
Mention the hallmark of meningitis caused by Neisseria. Name two important physical signs in meningitis.

Hallmark of meningococcal (N. meningitidis) meningitis: Meningococcal skin rash (purpuric rash).

Important physical signs in meningitis

  1. Neck rigidity
  2. Kernig's sign: extension at the knee with the hip joint flexed causes spasm in the hamstring muscles
  3. Brudzinski's sign: passive flexion of the neck causes flexion of the hips and knees

Ref: Davidson's 23rd/1019

Mention the commonest cause of pyogenic meningitis. Mention the commonest cause of viral meningitis. Name one special test for tubercular meningitis.

Commonest cause of pyogenic meningitis: Neisseria meningitidis.

Commonest cause of viral meningitis: Echovirus and Coxsackie virus.

Special test for tubercular meningitis: Adenosine deaminase (ADA) test — elevated (>40 U/L) in tubercular meningitis.

📋 Clinical case: A 5-year-old boy with high fever, vomiting, neck rigidity. What is your diagnosis?

Clinical diagnosis: Meningitis (probably pyogenic).

Confirmatory investigation: CSF analysis — lumbar puncture followed by physical, cytological, biochemical, and bacteriological examination.

Robbins 10e – CNS: Meningitis

📋 Clinical case: A child with sudden high-grade intermittent fever, neck rigidity, and purpuric rash. What is your diagnosis? Give expected CSF findings.

Diagnosis: Meningococcal meningitis — the purpuric rash is the hallmark of N. meningitidis infection.

Expected CSF findings

  • Physical: Pressure — increased; Colour — cloudy or hazy
  • Cytology: Cell count — 1000–5000/mm³; predominantly neutrophils
  • Biochemical: Protein — normal/elevated; Glucose — decreased
  • Gram stain: Gram-positive diplococci (intracellular)
  • Culture on blood agar: Growth found
📋 Clinical case: A child with recurrent otitis media, headache, fever, drowsiness, neck stiffness. What is the likely diagnosis? Give positive laboratory findings.

Diagnosis: Meningitis — most probably due to H. influenzae or S. pneumoniae (otitis media is a common predisposing factor).

Laboratory findings

  • Physical: Pressure — increased; Colour — cloudy/hazy
  • Cytology: Cell count 1000–5000/mm³; predominantly neutrophils
  • Biochemical: Protein — normal/elevated; Glucose — decreased
  • Gram stain: Gram-positive diplococci (S. pneumoniae) or gram-negative bacilli (H. influenzae)
  • Culture: Blood agar media for S. pneumoniae; Chocolate agar media for H. influenzae
📋 Clinical case: A 6-year-old malnourished child with low-grade fever for 15 days, loss of consciousness, neck rigidity. What is the diagnosis?

Probable diagnosis: Meningitis, probably tubercular — low-grade fever for 15 days + malnourished child favours tuberculosis (TB).

Robbins 10e – TB meningitis

In that TB-meningitis case: what lab tests are needed?

Lumbar puncture & CSF study

  • Colour: clear/cloudy
  • Cell count: 50–5000/mm³, mainly lymphocytes
  • Glucose: low, typically <40 mg/dL
  • Protein: elevated
  • Acid-fast bacilli (AFB) staining: may be positive
  • Culture on Lowenstein-Jensen (LJ) media: growth found
  • ADA (adenosine deaminase): elevated

Other tests

  • Complete Blood Count (CBC): low Hb%, ESR may be high
  • Chest X-ray
  • Tuberculin test (Mantoux)
  • Gastric aspirates for AFB & culture

Robbins 10e – TB meningitis

📋 Clinical case: A patient with pyogenic meningitis has HIGH CSF glucose. How is this possible?

The patient has uncontrolled diabetes mellitus (DM).

Explanation:

  • CSF glucose = 60% of blood glucose level
  • If blood glucose = 100 mg/dL → normal CSF = 60 mg/dL
  • If blood glucose = 200 mg/dL → CSF glucose = 120 mg/dL (still "high" despite pyogenic meningitis)

Therefore, although the patient has pyogenic meningitis, the CSF glucose will be higher than normal because the blood glucose baseline is elevated.

8 · Pleural Fluid Study

What is pleural fluid? Mention the significance of pleural fluid study. Differentiate transudate from exudate.

Pleural fluid: The fluid present in the pleural cavity. Normal amount: 1–10 mL. Abnormal accumulation of fluid beyond this level = pleural effusion.

Significance of pleural fluid study

By studying pleural fluid, we can detect the following diseases:

Exudative pleural fluid (high protein content)

  1. Pulmonary tuberculosis
  2. Pneumonia (para-pneumonic effusion)
  3. Bronchial carcinoma
  4. Pulmonary infarction
  5. Collagen disease (SLE, rheumatoid arthritis)
  6. Lymphoma

Transudative pleural fluid (low protein content)

  1. Congestive cardiac failure (CCF)
  2. Nephrotic syndrome
  3. Cirrhosis of liver
  4. Malnutrition
  5. Hypothyroidism
  6. Meigs' syndrome (ovarian fibroma + ascites + right-sided pleural effusion)
  7. Acute rheumatic fever
🎯 Transudate = systemic causes (low protein): Heart, Liver, Kidney failure. Exudate = local causes (high protein): TB, Pneumonia, Cancer.

Ascitic fluid study: Ascitic fluid should undergo gross inspection as well as laboratory analysis. Routine laboratory tests include: differential cell count, albumin assay, and cultures.

Write down the positive findings of exudative ascitic fluid.

Positive findings of exudative ascitic fluid

  1. Protein: >25 g/L
  2. Triglyceride: Elevated
  3. Glucose: Decreased
  4. Amylase: Increased
  5. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP): Increased
What is SAAG? How does it help differentiate causes of ascites?

SAAG (Serum–Ascites Albumin Gradient): Calculated by subtracting the ascitic fluid albumin from the serum albumin value obtained at the same time.

SAAGIndicatesCauses
≥1.1 g/dL (high)Portal hypertensionCirrhosis, cardiac ascites, Budd-Chiari syndrome, portal vein thrombosis
<1.1 g/dL (low)Non-portal hypertensiveTuberculous peritonitis, malignancy, pancreatitis, nephrotic syndrome
🎯 SAAG ≥ 1.1 = portal hypertension (cirrhosis is #1 cause).
