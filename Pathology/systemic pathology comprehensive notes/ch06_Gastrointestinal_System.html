🧠 Big Picture — The Gastrointestinal System (boss-level overview)

The GI tract runs mouth → anus; each segment has a signature disease set. 🔴 Oesophagus: motility disorders (achalasia), reflux oesophagitis (gastro-oesophageal reflux disease = GERD), Barrett oesophagus (intestinal metaplasia), varices (portal hypertension), squamous cell carcinoma (upper 2/3) & adenocarcinoma (lower 1/3).

🔴 Stomach: gastritis (acute & chronic — Helicobacter pylori vs autoimmune), peptic ulcer disease (PUD), gastric adenocarcinoma (intestinal & diffuse types), gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST — c-kit / CD117).

🔴 Small & large intestine: inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) = Crohn disease (transmural, skip lesions, non-caseating granuloma) vs ulcerative colitis (mucosal, continuous, crypt abscesses); congenital anomalies (Meckel diverticulum, Hirschsprung disease); malabsorption (coeliac disease, tropical sprue, Whipple disease); colorectal carcinoma (adenocarcinoma — 2nd most common cancer cause of death); carcinoid tumours & carcinoid syndrome; polyps (adenomatous → adenoma–carcinoma sequence); acute appendicitis.

📌 Key integrative links: H. pylori → chronic gastritis → PUD → gastric carcinoma / mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma (MALToma). Chronic IBD (especially ulcerative colitis) → increased risk of colorectal carcinoma. Portal hypertension → oesophageal varices. Adenoma–carcinoma sequence → most colorectal cancers.

1 · Oesophagus — Diseases Overview

Name the pathological conditions / diseases of the oesophagus. What are the congenital anomalies?

Classification of oesophageal diseases:

  1. Congenital anomalies (VIVA favourite): agenesis (congenital absence), stenosis (fibrous thickening → narrowed lumen), atresia (segment non-canalised), tracheo-oesophageal fistula, oesophageal mucosal webs, oesophageal rings (Schatzki rings).
  2. Motor dysfunction: achalasia, diverticula, hiatal hernia.
  3. Oesophagitis: Mallory-Weiss tears (lacerations), chemical/infectious oesophagitis (alcohol, corrosive acids/alkalis), reflux oesophagitis / gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD), eosinophilic oesophagitis (atopic).
  4. Oesophageal varices (important cause of oesophageal bleeding — portal hypertension).
  5. Barrett oesophagus (complication of chronic GERD; intestinal metaplasia → increased risk of adenocarcinoma).
  6. Tumours:
    • Benign: squamous cell papilloma, condyloma, leiomyoma, lipoma, fibroma, neurofibroma, rhabdomyoma, haemangioma, lymphangioma.
    • Malignant (VIVA): squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), adenocarcinoma, undifferentiated carcinoma, carcinoid tumour, melanoma, lymphoma, sarcoma.

1a · Achalasia

🔴 Short note: Achalasia. (CMU-23M)

Definition: Disordered oesophageal motility with inability to relax the lower oesophageal sphincter (LES), leading to progressive dysphagia and dilation of the oesophagus (mega-oesophagus).

Triad of achalasia:

  1. Incomplete LES relaxation
  2. Increased LES tone
  3. Aperistalsis of the oesophagus

Types & causes

  • Primary achalasia (rare): degeneration of nitric oxide (NO)-producing neurons that normally induce LES relaxation. Degenerative changes in the extraoesophageal vagus nerve or dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus may also occur.
  • Secondary achalasia: may arise in Chagas disease (Trypanosoma cruzi infection → destruction of myenteric plexus → failure of peristalsis → oesophageal dilation).

Clinical features

  • Dysphagia for solids and liquids
  • Putrid breath
  • High LES pressure on oesophageal manometry
  • "Bird-beak" sign on barium swallow study
  • Increased risk of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma

Treatment

Laparoscopic myotomy; pneumatic balloon dilatation; botulinum neurotoxin (Botox) injection (inhibits cholinergic neurons that promote contraction).

1b · Reflux Oesophagitis / GERD

🔴 Short note: Reflux oesophagitis / GERD (gastro-oesophageal reflux disease). (CU-19M)

Definition: Reflux of gastric acid into the lower oesophagus due to reduced lower oesophageal sphincter (LES) tone. (N.B: It is the opposite of achalasia, where LES tone is increased.)

Causes / risk factors (conditions that decrease LES tone or increase abdominal pressure)

  • Transient LES relaxation (vagal pathways)
  • Abrupt increases in intra-abdominal pressure (coughing, straining, bending)
  • Alcohol & tobacco use, obesity, fat-rich diet, caffeine
  • CNS depressants, pregnancy, hiatal hernia
  • Delayed gastric emptying, increased gastric volume

Clinical features

  • Heartburn (retrosternal burning — may mimic cardiac chest pain)
  • Dysphagia
  • Regurgitation of sour-tasting gastric contents (postprandial)
  • Adult-onset asthma and cough
  • Dental enamel damage

Treatment

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs).

Complications

  • Oesophagitis
  • Oesophageal ulceration with stricture
  • Barrett oesophagus (columnar metaplasia of oesophageal squamous epithelium)

Morphology

  • Mild GERD: simple hyperaemia (redness).
  • More significant reflux: erosions, influx of eosinophils into squamous mucosa, basal zone hyperplasia, elongation of lamina propria papillae. Neutrophil infiltration less frequent (usually in setting of bacterial/fungal infection or chemical damage).

Robbins 10e p.759 · BRS Pathology 5e p.228

Short note: Hiatal hernia.

Definition: Herniation or protrusion of part of the stomach through the oesophageal hiatus of the diaphragm. May cause LES incompetence; symptoms similar to GERD but symptomatic in <10% of adults.

Anatomical types (3 patterns)

  1. Sliding / oesophago-gastric hernia (85%): herniated stomach appears as supra-diaphragmatic bell, sliding up on both sides of the oesophagus.
  2. Rolling / para-oesophageal hernia (10%): true hernia — cardiac end of stomach rolls up para-oesophageally, producing an intrathoracic sac.
  3. Mixed / transitional hernia (5%): combination of sliding and rolling.

Features & complications

  • Heartburn (retrosternal burning), regurgitation of gastric juice — worsened by heavy work, lifting, bending.
  • Complications: ulceration, bleeding, perforation, reflux oesophagitis (more with sliding type).

1c · Oesophageal Varices

🔴 What are oesophageal varices? Mention its mechanism. (CMU-23M)

Definition: Dilated, tortuous veins lying primarily within the mucosa & submucosa of the distal oesophagus and proximal stomach, occurring secondary to portal hypertension. Develops in 90% of cirrhotic patients (most commonly in alcoholic liver disease).

Clinical features

Mostly asymptomatic, but risk of rupture exists. Variceal rupture → haemorrhage into lumen or oesophageal wall → painless haematemesis. Most common cause of death in cirrhosis.

Mechanism

Liver cirrhosis / fibrosis → obstruction of portal blood flow → portal hypertension → porto-systemic shunting → anastomotic channels connecting portal and systemic venous systems open and become distended → oesophageal varices.

Robbins 10e p.760 · BRS Pathology 5e p.228

1d · Barrett Oesophagus

🔴 Short note: Barrett oesophagus. (CU-18Nv, DU-06S/Ja, CMU-21Nv,20M)

Definition: Long-standing complication of chronic GERD characterised by intestinal metaplasia within the oesophageal squamous mucosa — associated with increased risk of cancer. It is the single most important risk factor for oesophageal adenocarcinoma.

Incidence: occurs in up to 10% of individuals with symptomatic GERD; peak age 40–60 years.

Pathogenesis

Distal squamous epithelium replaced by metaplastic columnar epithelium as response to prolonged injury. Sequential evolution: Barrett epithelium (columnar metaplasia with goblet cells) → dysplasia → carcinoma in situ → oesophageal adenocarcinoma.

Morphology

  • Gross: red, velvety mucosa between smooth pale-pink squamous mucosa and light-brown gastric mucosa; may exist as tongues or patches (islands).
  • Microscopic: replacement of squamous epithelium by metaplastic columnar cells with goblet cells (definitive diagnosis). Intestinal metaplasia may be accompanied by dysplastic changes (low → high grade). Inflammatory changes (acute or chronic) commonly accompany.

Diagnostic criteria (2 required)

  1. Endoscopic evidence of metaplastic columnar mucosa above the gastro-oesophageal junction.
  2. Histologic evidence of intestinal-type metaplasia showing goblet cells replacing squamous epithelium.

Treatment

Surgical resection / oesophagectomy; photodynamic therapy, laser ablation, endoscopic mucosectomy; regular follow-up with endoscopy and biopsy.

Complications

Local ulceration with bleeding and stricture; oesophageal adenocarcinoma.

Robbins 10e p.761 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.523

1e · Oesophageal Carcinoma

🔴 Mention the risk factors of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Write the etiopathogenesis.

SCC occurs in adults >45 years; males affected 4× more than females.

Risk factors

  1. Dietary: deficiency of vitamins (A, C, B₂, thiamin, B₆), trace elements (Zn, Mo); deficiency of fruits/vegetables; high nitrites/nitrosamines; betel chewing.
  2. Lifestyle: poverty, frequent consumption of very hot beverages, fungus-contaminated foods, alcohol consumption, tobacco use.
  3. Oesophageal disorders: caustic oesophageal injury, achalasia, tylosis, hiatal hernia, diverticula, Plummer-Vinson syndrome.
  4. Genetic predisposition: long-standing coeliac disease, ectodermal dysplasia, epidermolysis bullosa, racial disposition (more common in African Americans).
  5. Previous radiation to the mediastinum (5–10+ years after exposure).

Robbins 10e pp.763–764

Mention the sites of oesophageal cancer.
  • Squamous cell carcinoma: arises most frequently in the upper and middle thirds of the oesophagus.
  • Adenocarcinoma: arises most frequently in the lower third of the oesophagus.

Robbins 10e pp.762–763 · BRS Pathology 5e p.229

Write the morphologic patterns of oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma.

Three morphologic patterns:

  1. Protruded (60%): polypoid / exophytic lesion protruding into and obstructing the lumen.
  2. Flat (15%): ulcerated or diffusely infiltrative lesion within the wall → thickening, rigidity, luminal narrowing.
  3. Excavated (25%): tumour may invade surrounding structures including the respiratory tree (→ pneumonia), aorta (→ catastrophic exsanguination), mediastinum and pericardium.

2 · Stomach — Diseases Overview

Classify diseases of the stomach. Name the common malignant neoplasms of the stomach.
  1. Congenital anomalies: ectopia, diaphragmatic hernia, congenital hypertrophic pyloric stenosis.
  2. Gastropathy & acute gastritis.
  3. Stress-related mucosal disease: stress ulcers, Curling ulcers (burns/trauma), Cushing ulcers (intracranial disease).
  4. Chronic gastritis & complications: Helicobacter pylori gastritis, autoimmune atrophic gastritis, uncommon forms (eosinophilic, lymphocytic, granulomatous), peptic ulcer disease (PUD), mucosal atrophy & intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, gastritis cystica.
  5. Hypertrophic gastropathies: Ménétrier disease, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome.
  6. Acquired pyloric stenosis.
  7. Polyps & tumours:
    • Benign: inflammatory/hyperplastic polyps, fundic gland polyps, adenoma, leiomyoma, neurofibroma, lipoma, tumours of enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells, granular cell tumours, GIST.
    • Malignant: gastric carcinoma (adenocarcinoma >90%), carcinoid (neuroendocrine) tumours, lymphoma, leiomyosarcoma, schwannoma.
🎯 Who classification of gastric tumours: Epithelial (adenoma → adenocarcinoma), mesenchymal (GIST, leiomyoma/sarcoma), neuroendocrine (carcinoid, ECL-cell), lymphoid (MALToma).

2a · Gastritis (Acute & Chronic)

🔴 Define gastritis. Mention its types and causes. Write the pathogenesis and clinical features.

Definition: Inflammation of the gastric mucosa.

1. Acute gastritis

Acidic damage to the stomach mucosa due to imbalance between mucosal defences and acidic environment. Acid damage → superficial inflammation, mucosal erosion, or ulcer.

Causes/risk factors: heavy NSAID use (especially aspirin), excessive alcohol, ingestion of harsh chemicals (acids/bases), heavy smoking, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, stress-related mucosal diseases (stress ulcers, Curling ulcers, Cushing ulcers), systemic bacterial/viral infections (salmonellosis, CMV), mechanical trauma (nasogastric intubation), distal gastrectomy, decreased oxygen delivery at high altitude.

Clinical features: may be asymptomatic; epigastric pain, nausea, vomiting; mucosal erosion, ulceration, haemorrhage, haematemesis, melaena; rarely massive blood loss.

2. Chronic gastritis

Characterised by chronic mucosal inflammation and atrophy of the mucosal glands. Symptoms less severe but more persistent than acute gastritis.

Causes: H. pylori infection, autoimmune gastritis, radiation injury, chronic bile reflux, mechanical injury (nasogastric tube), systemic diseases (Crohn disease, amyloidosis, graft-versus-host disease [GVHD]).

A. H. pylori gastritis (most common — 90%)

Location: antrum (most commonly infected site). Clinical feature: epigastric abdominal pain.

Sequelae: PUD; gastric adenocarcinoma (intestinal type); MALT lymphoma (MALToma).

Treatment: triple therapy (antibiotics + proton pump inhibitors).

B. Autoimmune atrophic gastritis

Autoimmune destruction of gastric parietal cells (body & fundus). Antibodies to parietal cells / intrinsic factor → vitamin B₁₂ deficiency → defective acid secretion (achlorhydria). Median age at diagnosis 60 (slightly more women).

Clinical features (related to B₁₂ deficiency): pernicious anaemia (megaloblastic anaemia), atrophic glossitis (smooth beefy-red tongue), epithelial megaloblastosis, malabsorptive diarrhoea, CNS features (subacute combined degeneration of spinal cord). Often associated with autoimmune diseases (Hashimoto thyroiditis, type 1 diabetes mellitus, Addison disease).

Sequelae: intestinal metaplasia → increased risk of gastric adenocarcinoma (intestinal type).

2b · Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

🔴 Define peptic ulcer disease (PUD). Enumerate the common sites. (DU-19/17Nv, RU-21/20/19/18Nv)

Definition: Chronic mucosal ulceration affecting the duodenum or stomach, almost always associated with H. pylori infection, NSAIDs, or cigarette smoking. Occur most commonly (98–99%) in duodenum or stomach in ratio 4:1.

Common sites (VIVA)

  • First part of duodenum
  • Stomach (usually antrum)
  • Gastro-oesophageal junction
  • Within margins of gastro-jejunostomy (marginal ulcer)
  • Throughout the GIT in Zollinger-Ellison syndrome
  • Within or adjacent to an ileal Meckel diverticulum containing ectopic gastric mucosa

Robbins 10e p.770 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.535

🔴 What are the causes / risk factors of peptic ulcer disease? (SMU-23/22M, CU-13Ju)
  • H. pylori infection (~90% duodenal ulcer patients, ~70% gastric ulcer patients)
  • Cigarette smoking
  • NSAIDs (potentiated by corticosteroids)
  • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
  • Illicit drugs (e.g. cocaine — reduces mucosal blood flow)
  • Alcoholic cirrhosis (primarily duodenal PUD)
  • Psychological stress (increases gastric acid secretion)
  • Endocrine cell hyperplasia (stimulates parietal cell growth & acid secretion)
  • Zollinger-Ellison syndrome (PUD of stomach, duodenum and jejunum)
  • Viral infection (CMV, herpes simplex virus)

Robbins 10e p.770 · Davidson's 23e p.798

🔴 Write down the defense mechanisms of gastro-duodenal mucosa / mucosal barrier. (RMU-21M)
  1. Surface mucous secretion
  2. Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻) secretion into mucus
  3. Increased mucosal blood flow (washes out H⁺)
  4. Intercellular tight junctions (epithelial barrier to back-diffusion of H⁺)
  5. Apical surface membrane transport
  6. Quick regenerative capacity of lining epithelium
  7. Elaboration of prostaglandin (↑mucus secretion, blood flow and epithelial regeneration)

Robbins 10e p.765

🔴 Describe the pathogenesis of peptic ulcer disease. (DU-22Nv, CMU-23Nv,22M,19M)

PUD results from imbalances between mucosal defence mechanisms (protective factors) and damaging forces. PUD generally develops on a background of chronic gastritis.

1. Impairment of defence mechanism (Very Important for VIVA)

  • Mucus secretion ↓
  • Bicarbonate secretion in mucus ↓
  • Mucosal blood flow ↓
  • Apical surface membrane transport ↓
  • Epithelial regenerative capacity ↓
  • Prostaglandin elaboration ↓

2. Increased damaging forces

  • Gastric hyperacidity
  • Peptic enzymes (pepsin)
  • Helicobacter pylori infection
  • NSAIDs, aspirin
  • Cigarettes, alcohol
  • Duodenogastric reflux

Robbins 10e pp.765, 770

🔴 What is the role of H. pylori in the pathogenesis of peptic ulcer? (CU-12Ja, SU-13Ju)

Although H. pylori does not invade tissues, it causes ulcer through:

  1. Induces intense inflammatory & immune response → liberates proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α).
  2. Secretes urease enzyme → breaks down urea → toxic compounds (ammonium chloride, monochloramine).
  3. Bacterial proteases & phospholipases → breakdown of glycoprotein-lipid complexes in gastric mucus → weakens first-line mucosal defence.
  4. Enhances gastric acid secretion & impairs duodenal bicarbonate production.
  5. Thrombotic occlusion of surface capillaries (via bacterial platelet-activating factor).
  6. Bacterial antigens (including lipopolysaccharide) → recruit inflammatory cells to mucosa.

H. pylori infection most often presents as predominantly antral gastritis with normal or increased acid production.

Robbins 10e p.767

  1. Peptic ulcer disease (PUD)
  2. Chronic gastritis
  3. Gastric carcinoma
  4. Gastric lymphoma / MALToma

🔴 Write the morphology (gross + microscopic) of peptic ulcer. (DU-13/11Ja, SU-13/10Ja)

Gross / macroscopic

  • Location: Gastric ulcer: predominantly along lesser curvature in pyloric antrum, more commonly posterior than anterior wall. Duodenal ulcer: mostly first part of duodenum, more commonly anterior than posterior wall.
  • Shape: round to oval, sharply punched-out defect.
  • Margin: usually level with surrounding mucosa or only slightly elevated; mucosal margin may overhang base slightly.
  • Depth: superficial (confined to mucosa) to deep (penetrating into muscularis propria).
  • Base: smooth and clean (peptic digestion of exudates); blood vessels may be evident.

Microscopic — 4 histological zones (from within outward)

  1. Superficial thin layer of necrotic fibrinoid debris.
  2. Zone of non-specific inflammatory infiltrate (neutrophils predominating).
  3. Zone of active granulation tissue infiltrated with mononuclear leucocytes.
  4. Base of scar tissue.

N.B: Malignant gastric ulcers are larger, bowl-shaped with elevated and indurated mucosa at the margin.

🔴 Mention the clinical features of peptic ulcer disease.

Peptic ulcers are remitting and relapsing lesions.

  1. Periodic epigastric pain — burning or aching: Gastric ulcer: pain immediately or within 1–3 hours after food during the day; never occurs at night. Duodenal ulcer: pain severe, occurs late night (11 pm – 2 am, "hunger pain"); relieved by alkali or food.
  2. Nausea, vomiting, bloating, belching, heartburn (retrosternal pain) and "water brash" (burning fluid into the mouth).
  3. Anorexia and early satiety.
  4. Weight loss in gastric ulcer patients; weight gain in duodenal ulcer patients.
  5. Haematemesis and melaena.
  6. Iron-deficiency anaemia.
  7. Acute perforation.

N.B: With penetrating ulcers, pain may be referred to the back, left upper quadrant, or chest (misinterpreted as cardiac).

Robbins 10e p.771 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.536

🔴 Write down the complications of peptic ulcer disease (PUD). (DU-22/19Nv, RU-21/20/18Nv)
  1. Bleeding: occurs in 15–20% of patients (most frequent complication); may be life-threatening; accounts for 25% of ulcer-related deaths; may be the 1st indication of an ulcer.
  2. Perforation: occurs in up to 5% of patients; accounts for two-thirds of ulcer-related deaths; rarely 1st indication.
  3. Obstruction: mostly in chronic ulcers; secondary to oedema and scarring; ~2% of patients; most often with pyloric channel ulcers; causes incapacitating crampy abdominal pain; rarely causes total obstruction and intractable vomiting.
  4. Malignant transformation: chronic duodenal ulcer never turns malignant; chronic gastric ulcer → carcinoma in <1% of cases.

2c · Gastrointestinal Stromal Tumour (GIST)

🔴 Short note: Gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST). (DU-21Ja,20Nv/M)

Definition: Mesenchymal tumours derived from the interstitial cells of Cajal (pacemaker cells of the gastrointestinal muscularis propria). Most common mesenchymal tumour of the abdomen.

Peak age: 60 years (<10% before age 40).

Site: >50% in the stomach; also small intestine, large intestine, extragastrointestinal sites.

Behaviour: generally benign but may be recurrent, aggressive, or metastatic.

Genetic mutation

  • c-kit oncogene (CD117) — detected by immunohistochemistry from Cajal cells.
  • Platelet-derived growth factor receptor-α (PDGFRA).

Morphology

  • Gross: can reach 30 cm; solitary, well-circumscribed, fleshy mass; overlying mucosa frequently ulcerated; cut surface has whorled appearance.
  • Microscopic: spindle cell type (thin elongated cells) or epithelioid type (plumper epithelial-appearing cells); mixtures common.

Symptoms

Mass effects; mucosal ulceration → blood loss → anaemia.

Treatment

Complete surgical resection (primary treatment for localised GIST); targeted therapy / tyrosine kinase inhibitor (imatinib) — inhibits tyrosine kinase activity of c-kit and PDGFRA.

Robbins 10e p.779 · BRS Pathology 5e p.232

2d · Gastric Adenocarcinoma

🔴 Mention the sites, risk factors, clinical features, morphology (gross & microscopic) and spread of gastric carcinoma.

Adenocarcinoma is the most common gastric malignancy (>90% of all gastric cancers).

Sites

  • Pylorus & antrum (50–60%)
  • Cardia (25%)
  • Body & fundus (15–25%)

Early gastric carcinoma: confined to mucosa & submucosa. Advanced: extends into muscularis propria and beyond.

Risk factors

  1. Environmental: geographic (Japan, Finland, Iceland), smoked foods, nitrates/nitrites, pickled vegetables, excessive salt, low fruit/vegetables, low SES, smoking, alcohol.
  2. Host factors: age >50, male sex, H. pylori infection, achlorhydria, chronic gastritis / pernicious anaemia, gastric adenoma, partial gastrectomy (stump carcinoma), chronic gastric ulcer.
  3. Genetic: blood group A slightly increased risk, family history, hereditary nonpolyposis colon cancer (HNPCC), familial gastric carcinoma (E-cadherin mutation).

Role of H. pylori in gastric carcinoma

H. pylori → disruption of protective mechanisms → chronic gastritis → pangastritis → multifocal atrophic gastritis → reduced acid secretion → intestinal metaplasia/dysplasia → increased risk of gastric adenocarcinoma.

Clinical features

  • Early: dyspepsia, dysphagia, nausea.
  • Advanced: abdominal pain, weight loss (cachexia), anorexia, early satiety, anaemia, altered bowel habits, haematemesis and/or melaena.

Morphology

  • Gross — 3 macroscopic patterns: (1) Exophytic — protrusion into lumen; (2) Flat or depressed; (3) Excavated — shallow/deep ulcer. Large areas of infiltration → diffuse rugal flattening → rigid, thickened wall → linitis plastica ("leather bottle" appearance).
  • Microscopic:
    • Intestinal type: more common; expanding growth; bulky tumours of columnar gland-forming cells; abundant mucin in gland lumens.
    • Diffuse type: infiltrative growth; signet-ring cells diffusely infiltrating gastric mucosa; excessive mucin production.

Spread

  • Local invasion: oesophagus, duodenum, omentum, pancreas, liver, spleen, transverse colon.
  • Metastatic spread:
    • Virchow's node (left supraclavicular lymph node) — Troisier's sign.
    • Sister Mary Joseph nodule (periumbilical lymph node metastasis).
    • Krukenberg tumour — bilateral ovarian metastasis (signet-ring cells); gastric adenocarcinoma is the most common source.
    • Left axillary lymph node, Pouch of Douglas.
    • Haematogenous spread to liver, lungs, brain, bones, kidneys, adrenals.

Investigations

Barium meal X-ray; upper GI endoscopy + biopsy (gold standard); USG/CT (liver metastasis); chest X-ray; tumour markers: CEA, CA 19-9.

3a · Intestinal Obstruction

🔴 Mention the causes of intestinal obstruction. (VIVA)
  1. Hernias: weakness/defect in abdominal wall → protrusion of a serosa-lined pouch of peritoneum (hernia sac).
  2. Adhesions: from surgical procedures, infection, or peritoneal inflammation (e.g. endometriosis).
  3. Volvulus: loop of bowel twists about its mesenteric attachment → luminal and vascular compromise (obstruction + infarction). Most often in sigmoid colon, then caecum, small bowel.
  4. Intussusception: segment of intestine telescopes into the immediately distal segment (propelled by peristalsis). Most common cause of intestinal obstruction in children <2 years. May progress to obstruction, compression of mesenteric vessels, and infarction.

3b · Meckel Diverticulum

Short note: Meckel diverticulum.

Definition: blind outpouching of all three layers of the bowel wall (true diverticulum) due to incomplete obliteration of the vitellointestinal duct. Most common congenital anomaly of the GI tract; occurs in the ileum.

🎯 "Rule of 2s": occurs in ~2% of population; generally within 2 feet (60 cm) of the ileocaecal valve; ~2 inches (5 cm) long; twice as common in males; most often symptomatic by age 2 (only ~4% are ever symptomatic).

Lining & complications

Almost always lined by small-intestinal epithelium; rarely contains ectopic gastric mucosa or pancreatic tissue.

Complications: perforation, haemorrhage, diverticulitis.

Robbins 10e p.755 · Pathoma 2014 p.106

3c · Megacolon & Hirschsprung Disease

Short note: Megacolon. (CMU-22Nv)

Definition: Any form of marked dilatation of the entire colon or its segment.

Types & causes

  1. Congenital:
    • Hirschsprung disease (common): congenital absence of ganglion cells in the bowel wall → aganglionic segment remains contracted.
    • Myopathies, neuropathies (rare).
  2. Acquired:
    • Obstructive (tumour, post-inflammatory strictures)
    • Endocrine (myxoedema, cretinism)
    • CNS disorders (spina bifida, paraplegia, Parkinsonism)
    • Psychogenic (emotional disturbances, psychiatric disorders)
    • Connective tissue disease (scleroderma)
    • Chagas disease (Trypanosoma cruzi) — only example of acquired loss of ganglion cells; in all other acquired causes, bowel innervation is normal.

Harsh Mohan 7e pp.563–564

🔴 Short note: Hirschsprung disease / congenital aganglionic megacolon. (DU-12Ju, CU-15Ja)

Definition: Congenital disease caused by absence of ganglion cells in the nerve plexus (Meissner's & Auerbach's plexus), usually of the distal hind gut, with hypertrophy of nerve fibres → functional obstruction of the colon. Incidence: ~1 in 5000 live births.

Pathogenesis

Migration of neural crest cells from caecum to rectum arrested prematurely (or premature death of ganglion cells) → distal intestinal segment lacking both Meissner submucosal plexus and Auerbach myenteric plexus = aganglionosis → absent coordinated peristalsis → functional obstruction → dilation proximal to the affected segment.

Sites

In ~⅔ of cases: rectum & sigmoid colon. Also: anorectal junction, rectosigmoid junction, whole colon, rarely terminal ileum.

Clinical features

  • Neonate: failure to pass meconium, abdominal distension, bilious vomiting.
  • Chronic (first few weeks of life): chronic intermittent constipation, gradual abdominal distension, failure to thrive.
  • Adult: severe constipation.

Investigations

Plain X-ray abdomen; barium enema X-ray; rectal suction biopsy (lack of ganglion cells); anorectal manometry.

Diagnosis requires histologic confirmation that ganglion cells are absent within the affected segment.

Treatment & complications

Surgical resection of the aganglionic segment + anastomosis of normal proximal colon to the rectum.

Complications: enterocolitis, intestinal obstruction, fluid/electrolyte disturbances, growth retardation, perforation, peritonitis, septicaemia.

Robbins 10e p.756 · Pathoma 2014 p.109

3d · Malabsorption & Diarrhoea

🔴 Short note: Malabsorption syndrome. (For VIVA)

Definition: Defective absorption of fats, fat- and water-soluble vitamins, proteins, carbohydrates, electrolytes/minerals, and water.

Hallmark: steatorrhoea — excessive faecal fat; bulky, frothy, greasy, yellow or clay-coloured stools.

Five important causes

  1. Pancreatic insufficiency
  2. Coeliac disease
  3. Tropical sprue
  4. Crohn disease
  5. Cystic fibrosis

Others: primary bile acid malabsorption, autoimmune enteropathy, disaccharidase deficiency, intestinal GVHD.

Symptoms

  • General: diarrhoea, flatus, abdominal pain, weight loss, muscle wasting, anorexia, distension, borborygmi.
  • Vitamin/mineral deficiency: anaemia (folate, B₁₂, pyridoxine), bleeding (vitamin K), osteopenia/tetany (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, vitamin D), peripheral neuropathy (vitamin A, B₁₂), goitre/vitiligo.

Robbins 10e p.783

Define and classify diarrhoea.

Definition: Increase in stool mass, frequency, or fluidity (>200 g/day). Severe cases: >1 L/day; without fluid resuscitation → death. Painful, bloody, small-volume diarrhoea = dysentery.

Four major categories

  1. Secretory diarrhoea: isotonic stool; persists during fasting.
  2. Osmotic diarrhoea (e.g. lactase deficiency): excessive osmotic forces from unabsorbed luminal solutes; fluid >50 mOsm more concentrated than plasma; abates with fasting.
  3. Malabsorptive diarrhoea: generalised failure of nutrient absorption; associated with steatorrhoea; relieved by fasting.
  4. Exudative diarrhoea: inflammatory disease; purulent, often bloody stools; continues during fasting.

Robbins 10e p.784

3e · Coeliac Disease

What is coeliac disease?

Definition: Immune-mediated disorder triggered by ingestion of gluten-containing foods (wheat, rye, barley) in genetically predisposed individuals. Also known as coeliac sprue or gluten-sensitive enteropathy.

Mechanism of malabsorption: loss of brush-border surface area due to villous atrophy and possibly deficient enterocyte maturation from immune-mediated epithelial damage.

3f · Infectious Enterocolitis

Write about pseudomembranous colitis.

Definition: Generally caused by Clostridioides (Clostridium) difficile; also called antibiotic-associated colitis / antibiotic-associated diarrhoea.

Risk factors: antibiotic treatment, advanced age, hospitalisation, immunosuppression.

Clinical features: fever, leukocytosis, abdominal pain, cramps, watery diarrhoea, dehydration.

Robbins 10e p.793

Write about Whipple disease.

Definition: Rare, multi-visceral chronic disease characterised by macrophages loaded with Tropheryma whippelii organisms.

Clinical presentation (triad): diarrhoea, weight loss, arthralgia. Extraintestinal symptoms (arthritis, fever, lymphadenopathy, neurologic/cardiac/pulmonary disease) may precede malabsorption by months/years.

Morphologic features: distinctive PAS-positive macrophages in intestinal mucosa; T. whippelii bacilli visualised by electron microscopy.

Mechanism of malabsorption: bacteria-laden macrophages accumulate within small-intestinal lamina propria and mesenteric lymph nodes → lymphatic obstruction → impaired lymph drainage.

3g · Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

What do you mean by IBS? (SU-17M)

Definition: Functional gastrointestinal disorder characterised by chronic, relapsing abdominal pain, bloating, and changes in bowel habits. Peak prevalence: 20–40 years; significant female predominance.

Types (Rome criteria)

  1. Diarrhoea-predominant IBS
  2. Constipation-predominant IBS
  3. Mixed subtypes

Diagnosis: endoscopic and microscopic evaluations are normal; diagnosis depends on clinical symptoms (abdominal pain/discomfort ≥3 days/month over 3 months, improving with defecation, with change in stool frequency or form).

3h · Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

🔴 What is inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)? Name the IBDs. Describe the pathogenesis. (DU-24Nv,23M)

Definition: Chronic condition resulting from complex interactions between intestinal microbiota and host immunity in genetically predisposed individuals → inappropriate mucosal immune activation.

Two disorders: (1) ulcerative colitis — limited to colon & rectum, generally mucosa & submucosa; (2) Crohn disease — may involve any area from mouth to anus, typically transmural.

Pathogenesis — three components

  1. Mucosal immunity: immune signalling and regulatory genes (HLA, cytokines) associated with IBD.
  2. Autophagy & cellular stress responses: defects in autophagy contribute to IBD pathogenesis.
  3. Host-microbial interactions: gut microbiome modulates mucosal immunity; interplay contributes to IBD.

Why idiopathic? The basis of the abnormalities is still not established.

Robbins 10e pp.798–800

🔴 Short note: Crohn disease. (DU-24Nv,23M, SU-20M,09Ja)

Definition: Form of IBD; also called regional enteritis; commonly affects terminal ileum, ileocaecal valve & cecum but may occur anywhere from mouth to anus; typically transmural.

Pathogenesis

Prime culprits: T cells (particularly CD4⁺) — delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction induced by IFN-γ & TNF-α produced by TH1 cells.

Clinical features

  • Intestinal: non-bloody diarrhoea, fever, abdominal pain, weight loss, iron-deficiency anaemia, fibrosing strictures, fistulae, perforations, peritoneal abscesses.
  • Extra-intestinal: migratory polyarthritis, uveitis, sacroiliitis, ankylosing spondylitis, erythema nodosum, cutaneous granulomas, clubbing, pericholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis.

Morphology

  • Gross:
    • Site: any portion of GIT (most common: terminal ileum & colon).
    • Distribution: multiple separate, sharply delineated areas (skip lesions).
    • Wall: transmural inflammation down to serosa → strictures, fistulas, adhesions.
    • Lumen: narrowed → "string sign" on X-ray; mucosa shows serpiginous ulcers; intervening surviving mucosa swollen → "cobblestone appearance".
    • Mesenteric fat: extends around serosal surface ("creeping fat").
  • Microscopic:
    • Non-caseating granulomas (hallmark — present in ~35%).
    • Transmural infiltration of chronic inflammatory cells (lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages).
    • Distortion of mucosal architecture with loss of crypt epithelium.
    • Patchy ulceration (deep fissures).
    • Epithelial metaplasia.

Robbins 10e pp.799–801

🔴 Short note: Ulcerative colitis. (DU-17M)

Definition: Severe ulcerating inflammatory bowel disease limited to the colon & rectum; generally limited to the mucosa & submucosa.

Clinical features

  • Intestinal: bloody diarrhoea with stringy mucoid material, urgency, tenesmus, lower abdominal pain and cramps (temporarily relieved by defecation).
  • Extra-intestinal: migratory polyarthritis, uveitis, sacroiliitis, ankylosing spondylitis, skin lesions (pyoderma gangrenosum), pericholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis.

Arthritis is the most common extra-intestinal feature in both Crohn disease and ulcerative colitis.

Morphology

  • Gross:
    • Continuous involvement of rectum and colon (no skip areas).
    • Red, raw colonic mucosa that bleeds easily.
    • Linear and superficial ulcers; adjacent preserved mucosa appears as polyps ("pseudopolyps"); tips may fuse to create mucosal bridges.
    • Chronic disease → mucosal atrophy (smooth surface, no folds).
    • Not transmural → colonic wall not thickened, serosal surface normal, strictures do not occur.
  • Microscopic:
    • Diffuse inflammation limited to mucosa & superficial submucosa.
    • Inflammatory infiltrates in lamina propria.
    • Crypt abscesses.
    • Crypt distortion.
    • Pseudopyloric epithelial metaplasia.
    • Depletion of goblet cells & mucin.
    • Granulomas not present.

Robbins 10e pp.799, 801–803

3i · Colorectal Carcinoma

🔴 Colorectal carcinoma (CRC) — predisposing factors, clinical features, pathogenesis (APC/β-catenin & MSI), types.

Definition: Colorectal adenocarcinoma is the most common malignancy of the GI tract; ~15% of all cancer-related deaths; second only to lung cancer.

Predisposing factors

  1. Age: peaks at 60–70 years; <20% before age 50.
  2. Sex: males slightly more.
  3. Dietary: low unabsorbable vegetable fibre; high refined carbohydrates & fat.
  4. Geographic variations, obesity, physical inactivity.
  5. Pre-existing adenomas.
  6. Hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC / Lynch syndrome).
  7. Inflammatory bowel disease (especially ulcerative colitis).
  8. Diverticular disease (long duration), tobacco smoking.

Clinical features

  • Occult bleeding (melaena)
  • Change in bowel habits (more with left-sided growth)
  • Abdominal pain, weight loss (cachexia), anorexia, anaemia, weakness, malaise

Distribution: ~60% rectum; sigmoid/descending colon 25%; caecum/ileocaecal 10%; ascending colon/flexures 5%; transverse colon uncommon.

Pathogenesis

  1. APC mutation / β-catenin mechanism (80% sporadic cases): loss of APC (adenomatous polyposis coli) tumour suppressor gene → β-catenin translocates to nucleus → activates MYC & cyclin D1 → cell proliferation. Followed by K-RAS point mutation (10–50%), DCC gene deletion (60–70%), p53 loss (70–80%).
  2. Microsatellite instability (MSI) mechanism (10–15%): loss of DNA repair gene → repetitive DNA sequences (microsatellites) unstable during replication = hallmark of this pathway.

Investigations

Barium enema; MRI pelvis; USG/CT abdomen; transrectal ultrasound (TRUS); sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy + biopsy (gold standard); tumour markers: CEA, CA 19-9.

Histopathological types: 95% adenocarcinomas; remainder: undifferentiated, signet-ring cell, adenosquamous carcinomas; also sarcoma (leiomyosarcoma), lymphoma, carcinoid tumours.

Robbins 10e pp.813–814 · Harsh Mohan 7e pp.571–572, 587–588

What information do you want from the histopathology report after hemicolectomy for colon cancer? (CMU-23Nv)
  1. Tumour characteristics: histological type, grade, size, depth of invasion.
  2. Lymph node involvement: total nodes examined, positive nodes, extranodal extension.
  3. Resection margins: proximal & distal (free of tumour?).
  4. Lymphovascular and perineural invasion (LVI, PNI).
  5. Pathologic staging (pTNM): pT (depth), pN (nodal), pM (distant metastasis).
  6. Molecular/immunohistochemical markers: microsatellite instability (MSI) / mismatch repair (MMR); KRAS, NRAS, BRAF mutations; HER2; PD-L1.
  7. Additional findings: precursor lesions (adenomas, dysplasia), signet-ring cells, mucinous features, tumour budding.

3j · Intestinal Polyps

🔴 Define polyp. Classify intestinal polyps. (CU-18M, CMU-24M,23Nv)

Definition: A tumourous mass that protrudes into the lumen of the gut; any elevation of the intestinal surface.

Polyps are most common in the colon but may occur in oesophagus, stomach, or small intestine.

Types

  1. Non-neoplastic polyps:
    • Hyperplastic polyp (commonest)
    • Inflammatory polyp
    • Hamartomatous polyp: (a) juvenile polyps, (b) Peutz-Jeghers polyps
  2. Neoplastic (adenomatous) polyps — true neoplasms:
    • Tubular adenoma (most common — 75%)
    • Tubulovillous adenoma
    • Villous adenoma (highest malignant potential — >30% become malignant; large sessile tumour with velvety surface, fingerlike villi)
    • Sessile serrated adenoma

4 · Appendix

🔴 Give the aetio-pathogenesis of acute appendicitis. (RMU-24M,22Nv)

Aetiology (50–80% associated with luminal obstruction): faecalith (small stone-like stool mass), gallstone, tumour at the appendiceal opening (e.g. benign polyp), mass of worms (e.g. Oxyuris vermicularis).

Pathogenesis

Obstruction of appendiceal lumen → increased mucous secretion & accumulation → increased intraluminal pressure → venous obstruction → ischaemia & venous congestion → microbial proliferation → inflammation → additional oedema & exudates → further embarrassment of blood supply → more injury → acute appendicitis. If not stopped → gangrenous necrosis.

Describe the morphology of acute appendicitis. What are its complications? (DU-03S, RMU-24M,22Nv)

Morphology

  • Gross: appendix swollen & distended; serosal covering reddened; cut section: lumen filled with pus; mucosa shows ulceration & foci of suppurative necrosis.
  • Microscopic: subserosal vessels congested; neutrophil infiltration throughout mucosa, submucosa & muscularis propria; mucosal ulcerations; muscle fibres separated by oedema fluid.

Clinical features

Colicky pain (initially periumbilical, then localised to right iliac fossa); nausea, vomiting; mild pyrexia.

Complications

  1. Appendicular lump
  2. Appendicular abscess
  3. Perforation / burst appendix
  4. Gangrene of the appendix
  5. Peritonitis (generalised)
  6. Pyelophlebitis
  7. Portal venous thrombosis
  8. Liver abscess
  9. Bacteraemia
  10. Pelvic abscess
  11. Subphrenic abscess
