🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of the Heart (boss-level overview)

The heart can fail by six principal mechanisms: (1) Pump failure (systolic/diastolic dysfunction → heart failure), (2) Obstruction to flow (e.g., aortic stenosis, coarctation), (3) Regurgitant flow (valvular insufficiency), (4) Shunted flow (congenital septal defects), (5) Conduction disorders (arrhythmias → sudden cardiac death), (6) Rupture (cardiac free wall, septum, papillary muscle).

The full classification (VIVA favourite): 1. Heart failure (left-sided / right-sided) · 2. Congenital heart disease (CHD) — acyanotic (ASD, VSD, PDA), cyanotic (TOF, TGA, tricuspid atresia), obstructive (coarctation, stenosis) · 3. Ischaemic heart disease (IHD) — angina, MI, chronic IHD, sudden cardiac death · 4. Arrhythmias & sudden cardiac death (SCD) · 5. Hypertensive heart disease · 6. Valvular heart disease — rheumatic fever & RHD, infective endocarditis (IE), non-infected vegetations (NBTE, Libman-Sacks) · 7. Cardiomyopathies — dilated (DCM), hypertrophic, restrictive, arrhythmogenic · 8. Myocarditis · 9. Pericardial disease — effusion, acute pericarditis, constrictive pericarditis · 10. Cardiac tumours — myxoma (most common benign adult), sarcoma (malignant), metastases.

Robbins 10e, Ch 12 (The Heart), pp. 527–580 · Arif 15e (2025) Vol-2, pp.45–75

1 · Classification of Heart Diseases

🔴 Name the major cardiac diseases. Classify congenital heart disease.

Classification of heart diseases (complete):

  1. Heart failure: left-sided & right-sided
  2. Congenital heart disease (CHD):
    • Acyanotic (left-to-right shunts): Atrial septal defect (ASD), patent foramen ovale, ventricular septal defect (VSD), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)
    • Cyanotic (right-to-left shunts): Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF), transposition of the great arteries (TGA), tricuspid atresia
    • Obstructive lesions: Coarctation of the aorta, pulmonary stenosis & atresia, aortic stenosis & atresia
  3. Ischaemic heart disease (IHD): angina pectoris, myocardial infarction (MI), chronic IHD
  4. Arrhythmias: sudden cardiac death (SCD)
  5. Hypertensive heart disease: systemic (left-sided), pulmonary (right-sided = cor pulmonale)
  6. Valvular heart disease: aortic stenosis (AS), aortic regurgitation (AR), mitral stenosis (MS), mitral regurgitation (MR), rheumatic fever & rheumatic heart disease (RHD), infective endocarditis (IE), non-infected vegetations — nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis (NBTE) & Libman-Sacks disease, carcinoid heart disease, prosthetic valve complications
  7. Cardiomyopathies: dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, restrictive cardiomyopathy
  8. Myocarditis & other myocardial disease (cardiotoxic drugs, radiation)
  9. Pericardial disease: pericardial effusion & haemopericardium, acute & chronic pericarditis
  10. Tumours: primary benign (myxoma, fibroma, lipoma, papillary fibroelastoma, rhabdomyoma), primary malignant (sarcoma/angiosarcoma), secondary/metastatic (lung, breast, melanoma, leukaemia, lymphoma)

Robbins 10e pp.527–580; Arif pp.45–46

📌 List the causes of chest pain.

A. Central causes

  1. Anxiety / emotion
  2. Cardiac: myocardial ischaemia (angina), MI, myocarditis, pericarditis, mitral valve prolapse syndrome
  3. Aortic: aortic dissection, aortic aneurysm
  4. Oesophageal: oesophagitis, oesophageal spasm, Mallory-Weiss syndrome
  5. Pulmonary embolus
  6. Mediastinal: malignancy

B. Peripheral causes

  1. Lungs/pleura: pulmonary infarct, pneumonia, pneumothorax, malignancy, tuberculosis, connective tissue disorders
  2. Musculoskeletal: osteoarthritis, rib fracture/injury, intercostal muscle injury, costochondritis (Tietze syndrome)
  3. Neurological: prolapsed intervertebral disc, herpes zoster, thoracic outlet syndrome

Robbins 10e p.529; Davidson's 23e p.177

2 · Congenital Heart Disease (CHD)

🔴 Short note: Congenital heart disease. What are the three major categories?

Congenital heart disease (CHD) = abnormalities of the heart or great vessels present at birth. Most arise from faulty embryogenesis during gestational weeks 3–8, when major cardiovascular structures form. Incidence: ~5% of live births.

A shunt is an abnormal communication between chambers or vessels. Blood flows down pressure gradients from left (systemic) to right (pulmonary) side or vice versa.

Three major categories

CategoryFeaturesExamples
1. Right-to-left shunt (cyanotic CHD)Hypoxaemia, cyanosis (dusky blue skin/mucous membranes), paradoxical embolism, clubbing, polycythaemiaTetralogy of Fallot (TOF), transposition of the great arteries (TGA), persistent truncus arteriosus, tricuspid atresia, total anomalous pulmonary venous connection
2. Left-to-right shunt (acyanotic CHD)Not initially cyanotic; may become cyanotic late (Eisenmenger syndrome); right ventricular hypertrophy (RVH)Atrial septal defect (ASD), ventricular septal defect (VSD), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)
3. Obstructive CHDAbnormal narrowing of chambers, valves, or vessels (complete obstruction = atresia)Coarctation of the aorta, pulmonary stenosis & atresia, aortic stenosis & atresia

Robbins 10e pp.534–536; Arif pp.48–49

3 · Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)

🔴 Short note: Tetralogy of Fallot. Give the four cardinal features, clinical features, and morphology.

Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) — the four cardinal features (mnemonic: V.V.O.V):

  1. Ventricular septal defect (VSD)
  2. Obstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract (subpulmonary stenosis)
  3. An aOrta that overrides the VSD
  4. Ventricular (right) hypertrophy

Embryology: the first three features result from anterosuperior displacement of the infundibular septum; RVH is a secondary consequence of pressure overload.

Clinical features

  • Even untreated, TOF can be tolerated into adulthood
  • Most infants are cyanotic from birth or soon thereafter (classic TOF); some may not show cyanosis ("pink tetralogy")
  • As the child grows, the heart increases in size

Morphology

Heart is typically enlarged and classically "boot-shaped" due to marked right ventricular hypertrophy.

Treatment: complete surgical repair is possible for classic TOF but more complicated for persons with pulmonary atresia and dilated bronchial arteries.

Robbins 10e p.538; Arif p.49

4 · Heart Failure

🔴 Define heart failure. What is pressure-overload vs volume-overload hypertrophy?

Heart failure = the condition in which the heart cannot pump blood to adequately meet the metabolic demands of peripheral tissues, or can do so only at elevated filling pressures.

Patterns of hypertrophy (Robbins)

TypeMechanismSarcomere patternResult
Pressure-overload hypertrophyHypertension, aortic stenosisNew sarcomeres assembled in parallelConcentric increase in wall thickness; ↑ cross-sectional area of myocytes
Volume-overload hypertrophyValvular regurgitationSarcomeres assembled in seriesPrimary ventricular dilation; wall thickness may be normal or increased

In dilated hearts, heart weight (not wall thickness) is the best measure of hypertrophy.

Robbins 10e pp.530–532

🔴 What are the causes and morphologic changes in left-sided heart failure?

Causes of left-sided heart failure:

  • Ischaemic heart disease (IHD)
  • Hypertension
  • Aortic and mitral valvular disease
  • Primary myocardial diseases

Morphologic changes (organ-by-organ):

  1. Heart: hypertrophied & dilated left ventricle, secondary left atrial enlargement, thrombus formation, MI & valvular deformity
  2. Lung: pulmonary congestion & oedema (heavy, wet lungs), hemosiderin-laden macrophages in alveoli (siderophages / "heart failure cells"), pleural effusion (serous)
  3. Kidney: reduction in renal perfusion, prerenal azotaemia
  4. Brain: hypoxic encephalopathy

Cardinal features of left ventricular failure

  • Pulsus alternans
  • Gallop rhythm
  • Bilateral basal crepitations

Robbins 10e p.532; Arif pp.50–52

🔴 What are the causes and morphologic changes in right-sided heart failure?

Causes:

  1. Secondary to left-sided heart failure
  2. Isolated right-sided heart failure (often referred to as cor pulmonale)

Morphologic changes (organ-by-organ):

  1. Heart: hypertrophy & dilation of right atrium and ventricle; rarely structural defects such as valvular abnormalities or endocardial fibrosis
  2. Liver & portal system:
    • Congestive hepatomegaly — prominent passive congestion, most prominent around central veins → "nutmeg liver" appearance (congested red-brown pericentral zones with tan periportal regions)
    • Long-standing → central fibrosis → cardiac cirrhosis
    • Portal venous HTN → congestive splenomegaly (platelet sequestration), chronic congestion & oedema of bowel wall
  3. Pleural, pericardial, peritoneal spaces: effusions (peritoneal effusion = ascites)
  4. Subcutaneous tissues: peripheral/dependent oedema — foot/ankle (pedal) and pretibial oedema; in bedridden patients, presacral oedema; generalized massive oedema (anasarca)

Cardinal features of right ventricular failure

  • Raised JVP (jugular venous pressure)
  • Tender hepatomegaly
  • Dependent oedema

Cardinal features of congestive cardiac failure (bilateral): bilateral basal crepitations + raised JVP + tender hepatomegaly + dependent oedema.

Robbins 10e p.533; Arif pp.51–52

5 · Ischaemic Heart Disease (IHD)

🔴 Define and classify ischaemic heart disease (IHD). Why is it called coronary artery disease?

Ischaemic heart disease (IHD) = a group of related entities resulting from myocardial ischaemia — an imbalance between myocardial supply (perfusion) and cardiac demand for oxygenated blood.

In >90% of cases, myocardial ischaemia results from reduced blood flow due to obstructive atherosclerotic lesions in the epicardial coronary arteries → hence IHD is frequently called coronary artery disease (CAD).

NB: IHD is often the late manifestation of coronary atherosclerosis that began during childhood or adolescence — a long period (up to decades) of silent, slow progression before sudden onset of symptoms.

Types / syndromes of IHD

  1. Angina pectoris (chest pain — ischaemia insufficient to cause necrosis)
    • Stable (typical) angina
    • Unstable or crescendo angina
    • Prinzmetal angina (vasospastic)
  2. Myocardial infarction (MI) — frank cardiac necrosis
  3. Chronic IHD with heart failure
  4. Sudden cardiac death (SCD)

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS): the triad of unstable angina, acute MI, and SCD.

🎯 Atherosclerotic lesions in coronary arteries: highest incidence in LAD (left anterior descending) > RCA (right coronary) > CXA (circumflex). One-third single-vessel, one-third two-vessel, one-third three-vessel disease.

Robbins 10e pp.542–543; BRS Pathology 5e p.137; Harsh Mohan 7e p.407

🔴 What are the risk factors for IHD / MI? (major + additional)

Non-modifiable (constitutional)

  1. Genetic abnormalities
  2. Family history (most important independent risk factor)
  3. Increasing age
  4. Male gender

Modifiable (potentially controllable)

  1. Hyperlipidaemia (more specifically hypercholesterolaemia)
  2. Hypertension
  3. Cigarette smoking
  4. Diabetes mellitus

Additional risk factors

  • Inflammation & elevated C-reactive protein (CRP)
  • Hyperhomocystinaemia
  • Metabolic syndrome
  • Lipoprotein(a) — Lp(a)
  • Prothrombotic factors & elevated fibrinogen

Other factors

  • Obesity
  • Lack of exercise / physical inactivity
  • High carbohydrate intake
  • Competitive, stressful lifestyle (type A personality)

Robbins 10e p.494; BRS Pathology 5e p.124; Harsh Mohan 7e p.373

🔴 Discuss the pathogenesis of IHD. What are the non-atherosclerotic causes?

The dominant cause of IHD = partial or complete interruption of arterial blood flow to the myocardium → insufficient coronary perfusion relative to myocardial demand.

Three broad headings of etiology

  1. Coronary atherosclerosis: chronic, progressive atherosclerotic narrowing of epicardial coronary arteries
  2. Acute plaque change: rupture, superficial erosion, ulceration, fissuring, or deep haemorrhage
  3. Non-atherosclerotic causes:
    • Vasospasm
    • Stenosis of coronary ostia
    • Thrombotic diseases (e.g., polycythaemia vera, sickle cell anaemia)
    • Embolism
    • Trauma to coronary arteries
    • Aneurysms
    • Compression of a coronary from outside

Consequences of myocardial ischaemia (depending on suddenness, duration, degree, location, extent): stable angina, unstable angina, MI, SCD.

Robbins 10e p.542; BRS Pathology 5e p.137; Harsh Mohan 7e p.408

6 · Angina Pectoris

🔴 Define angina pectoris. Describe the three types.

Angina pectoris (literally "chest pain") = paroxysmal, usually recurrent attacks of substernal or precordial chest discomfort caused by transient (15 seconds to 15 minutes) myocardial ischaemia insufficient to induce myocyte necrosis.

Three types

TypeDescriptionMechanism
1. Stable (typical) anginaMost common. Crushing/squeezing substernal sensation radiating down left arm or to jaw. Precipitated by exertion (physical activity, emotional excitement, stress). Relieved by rest or vasodilators (nitroglycerin, calcium channel blockers).Imbalance in coronary perfusion (chronic stenosing coronary atherosclerosis) relative to myocardial demand
2. Unstable / crescendo anginaIncreasingly frequent, prolonged (>20 min), or severe angina at progressively lower levels of activity or even at rest. "Pre-infarction angina" / "acute coronary insufficiency" — warning for MI.Disruption of atherosclerotic plaque with superimposed thrombosis; distal embolization; vasospasm
3. Prinzmetal variant anginaUncommon; episodic myocardial ischaemia at rest.Coronary artery spasm
🎯 In angina, no elevation of enzymes in blood — there is no irreversible myocardial injury.

Robbins 10e p.543; BRS Pathology 5e p.137; Harsh Mohan 7e p.409

7 · Myocardial Infarction (MI)

🔴 Define MI. What are the two patterns? What type of necrosis occurs?

Myocardial infarction (MI) ("heart attack") = death of cardiac muscle due to prolonged ischaemia. The most important and feared consequence of coronary artery disease.

Two patterns of MI

PatternDescription
Transmural (full-thickness) infarctionIschaemic necrosis involves full or nearly full thickness of ventricular wall (endocardium → epicardium) in the distribution of the affected coronary artery
Subendocardial (non-transmural) infarctionIschaemic necrosis involves inner third of ventricular wall; occurs when plaque disruption → coronary thrombus → lysed (therapeutically or spontaneously) before necrosis extends full thickness

Multifocal microinfarction: pathology involving only smaller intramural vessels.

Type of necrosis: Coagulative necrosis.

Robbins 10e pp.543, 546–547; BRS Pathology 5e p.137; Harsh Mohan 7e p.410

🔴 Discuss the pathogenesis of MI. What is the "area at risk"?

A. Coronary arterial occlusion

a) Thrombotic occlusion (most common):

  1. Atheromatous plaque eroded or disrupted (endothelial injury, intraplaque haemorrhage, mechanical forces) → exposes subendothelial collagen & necrotic plaque contents
  2. Platelets adhere, aggregate, activate → release thromboxane A₂, ADP, serotonin → further aggregation & vasospasm
  3. Coagulation activation by tissue factor → growing thrombus
  4. Within minutes → complete coronary artery lumen occlusion

b) Non-thrombotic occlusion:

  • Vasospasm (with or without atherosclerosis; cocaine, ephedrine)
  • Embolism (left atrial fibrillation, mural thrombus, IE vegetations, prosthetic material, paradoxical emboli via patent foramen ovale)
  • Uncommon: small vessel disease (vasculitis), haematologic (sickle cell), amyloid, dissection, marked hypertrophy (aortic stenosis), hypotension (shock), inadequate protection during cardiac surgery

B. Myocardial response

Obstruction → diminished blood flow → ischaemia → rapid dysfunction → with prolonged compromise → myocyte death. The anatomic region supplied by that artery = "area at risk". Outcome depends on severity and duration of flow deprivation.

🎯 Myocardial ischaemia leads to loss of function within 1–2 minutes but causes necrosis only after 20–30 minutes.

Robbins 10e pp.544, 553; Arif pp.56–57

🔴 Mention the clinical features of MI.
  • Prolonged (>30 minutes) central chest pain — crushing, stabbing, or squeezing
  • Anxiety and fear of impending death
  • Nausea and vomiting
  • Hypotension
  • Rapid, weak pulse
  • Profuse sweating (diaphoresis)
  • Breathlessness
  • Collapse / syncope
🎯 Painless / "silent" MI: in as many as 25% of patients MI is entirely asymptomatic (e.g., diabetic neuropathy) — discovered only by ECG or lab tests.

Robbins 10e p.550; Davidson's 23e p.495; Arif p.58

🔴 Describe the biochemical changes and cardiac markers in MI with their timing.

Biochemical changes

  1. Cessation of aerobic glycolysis within seconds (ischaemia)
  2. Inadequate high-energy phosphate production (creatinine phosphate, ATP) → anaerobic glycolysis
  3. Accumulation of noxious metabolites (lactic acid)
  4. Enzyme release from injured cardiac myocytes (membrane leak = hallmark of irreversible cell injury)
  5. Release of myoglobin, troponin-I, and troponin-T

Cardiac biomarkers — timing table

MarkerRisesPeaksReturns to normal
CK-MB (creatine kinase-MB)4–6 hours12–24 hours48–72 hours
Troponin-I & Troponin-T4–6 hours24–48 hours5–14 days
AST (aspartate aminotransferase)12 hours48 hours3–4 days
LDH (lactate dehydrogenase)12 hours2–3 days1–2 weeks

Key facts

  • Troponin-I & CK-MB elevations are highly characteristic of MI
  • Troponin-I = most sensitive & specific cardiac marker (nearly complete tissue specificity)
  • Myoglobin = earliest to appear but NOT cardiospecific
  • CK-MB = useful for detecting reinfarction (returns to normal by 72 hours)
  • Total LDH & total CK = nonspecific, passing into disuse

Other investigations

  • ECG: ST segment elevation (or new bundle branch block), pathological Q-wave formation, T-wave inversion
  • Blood: neutrophilic leukocytosis, raised ESR & CRP
  • Chest X-ray: demonstrate pulmonary oedema
  • Echocardiography: assess ventricular function, detect complications
  • Coronary angiography: for moderate-to-high risk patients (PCI or urgent CABG)
🎯 CRP (C-reactive protein): normal <5 mg/L; >3 mg/L = highest cardiovascular risk; 1–3 mg/L = moderate risk. Strongly predicts MI, stroke, peripheral arterial disease, SCD.

Robbins 10e p.550; Davidson's 23e pp.497–498; BRS Pathology 5e p.138; Arif pp.59–60

📌 Mention the treatment of MI (therapeutic interventions).
  1. Urgent hospitalization (CCU — coronary care unit)
  2. Oxygen supplementation
  3. Morphine (pain relief, improve dyspnoea)
  4. Nitrates (vasodilation, reverse vasospasm)
  5. Antiplatelet agents (aspirin, ADP receptor inhibitors, GPIIb/IIIa inhibitors)
  6. Anticoagulant therapy (unfractionated heparin, LMWH, direct thrombin inhibitors, factor Xa inhibitors) — prevent thrombus propagation
  7. Beta blockers (↓ myocardial O₂ demand, ↓ arrhythmia risk — unless contraindicated in HF)
  8. Prompt reperfusion (fibrinolytics or transcatheter interventions — PCI)
  9. Manage O₂ supply vs demand: anxiety, ischaemic pain, haemodynamics, anaemia, respiratory disorders
  10. Early arrhythmia monitoring & management

Robbins 10e p.550; Davidson's 23e p.498

🔴 What are the complications of acute MI? (enumerate all 10)

Nearly three-fourths of patients experience one or more complications:

  1. Contractile dysfunction: left ventricular failure with hypotension, pulmonary oedema, respiratory impairment, severe "pump failure" (cardiogenic shock)
  2. Papillary muscle dysfunction → postinfarct mitral regurgitation
  3. Right ventricular infarction → right-sided heart failure
  4. Myocardial rupture (1–5% of MIs, frequently fatal; most common 3–7 days post-MI):
    • Left ventricular free wall rupture (most common) → fatal haemopericardium & cardiac tamponade
    • Ventricular septal rupture → VSD with left-to-right shunting
    • Papillary muscle rupture → severe mitral regurgitation
  5. Arrhythmias — most common cause of death in the first hour: heart block, bradycardia, supraventricular tachyarrhythmias, ventricular premature contractions, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation
  6. Pericarditis — typically 2–3 days post-MI; rarely intense pericarditis weeks later (Dressler syndrome)
  7. Chamber dilation — disproportionate stretching, thinning, dilation of infarcted region (especially anteroseptal infarcts)
  8. Mural thrombosis → left-sided thromboembolism
  9. Ventricular aneurysm
  10. Progressive heart failure (chronic IHD)
🎯 One-third of patients with STEMI (ST-elevation MI) at home will die — usually of arrhythmia within 1 hour of symptom onset, before medical attention.

Robbins 10e p.551; BRS Pathology 5e pp.138–139; Arif p.61

📌 Clinical scenario: 50-year-old male with sudden severe chest pain, sweating, loss of consciousness. Diagnosis and expected biochemical findings?

Probable diagnosis: acute myocardial infarction (MI).

Expected biochemical findings:

  • CK-MB — rises within 4–6 hours
  • Troponin-I & Troponin-T — rise within 4–6 hours
  • AST — rises within 12 hours
  • LDH — rises within 12 hours

8 · Sudden Cardiac Death (SCD)

🔴 Short note: Sudden cardiac death (SCD). Give the causes.

Sudden cardiac death (SCD) = unexpected death from cardiac causes, either without symptoms, or within 1–24 hours of symptom onset (Pathoma: <1 hour).

Mechanism: most often a lethal arrhythmia (asystole or ventricular fibrillation).

Causes of SCD

  1. Coronary artery disease / severe atherosclerosis (80–90% of cases)
  2. Non-atherosclerotic causes (younger victims):
    • Cardiac conduction abnormalities (hereditary or acquired)
    • Dilated or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
    • Congenital coronary arterial abnormalities
    • Myocarditis
    • Mitral valve prolapse
    • Pulmonary hypertension
    • Miscellaneous: pericardial tamponade, pulmonary embolism, metabolic & haemodynamic alterations, catecholamines, drugs of abuse (cocaine, methamphetamine)

Robbins 10e pp.551, 555; Arif pp.62–63

9 · Vegetative Heart Disease

🔴 Name the vegetative heart diseases. Compare the four major forms of vegetative endocarditis.

Vegetation = complex of thrombotic debris and organisms, often resulting from colonisation/invasion of heart valves or mural endocardium by a microbe, with destruction of underlying cardiac tissue. (Thrombi on heart valves = vegetations; may be infected or sterile.)

Three categories of vegetative heart disease

  1. Rheumatic fever & rheumatic heart disease (RHD)
  2. Infective endocarditis (IE)
  3. Non-infected (sterile) vegetations — NBTE & Libman-Sacks disease

Comparison of four major forms

TypeVegetation description
1. Rheumatic fever (RHD)Small, warty vegetations along the lines of closure of valve leaflets
2. Infective endocarditis (IE)Large, irregular masses in the valve cusps that can extend into the chordae
3. NBTE (non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis)Small, bland vegetations, usually attached at the line of closure; one or many
4. Libman-Sacks endocarditisSmall or medium-sized vegetations on either or both sides of the valve leaflets

Robbins 10e pp.561–562; Arif pp.63–64

10 · Rheumatic Fever & Rheumatic Heart Disease

🔴 Define rheumatic fever (RF) and rheumatic heart disease (RHD). Which valve is most commonly involved?

Rheumatic fever (RF) = an acute, immunologically mediated, multisystem inflammatory disease occurring a few weeks after group A streptococcal pharyngitis; occasionally follows streptococcal skin infections. Affects children 5–15 years, 1–4 weeks after pharyngitis.

Rheumatic heart disease (RHD) = the chronic stage of RF involving all layers of the heart (pancarditis), characterised principally by deforming fibrotic valvular disease, particularly the mitral valve.

Valves involved in RHD

  • Mitral valve: 65–70% of cases
  • Aortic valve: 25%
🎯 RHD is virtually the only cause of mitral stenosis.

Robbins 10e p.560; Harsh Mohan 7e p.418; BRS Pathology 5e p.139

🔴 Enumerate the Jones criteria for diagnosis of rheumatic fever. (major + minor + supporting evidence)

Diagnosis requires 2 major OR 1 major + 2 minor manifestations PLUS evidence of preceding streptococcal infection.

Major manifestations

  1. Migratory polyarthritis of large joints (most common major manifestation)
  2. Pancarditis (myocarditis, pericarditis, or endocarditis)
  3. Subcutaneous nodules (on extensor surfaces of extremities)
  4. Erythema marginatum (irregular circinate skin rash)
  5. Sydenham chorea (involuntary rapid movements, at least 3 months after acute RF)

Minor manifestations

  • Fever
  • Arthralgia (joint pain without clinical inflammation)
  • Raised ESR or CRP
  • Previous rheumatic fever
  • Leukocytosis
  • First-degree AV block (prolonged P-R interval on ECG)

Supporting evidence of preceding streptococcal infection

  1. Recent scarlet fever
  2. Raised anti-streptolysin O (ASO) or other streptococcal antibody titer
  3. Positive throat culture

Robbins 10e p.562; Davidson's 23e p.515; BRS Pathology 5e p.140; Arif pp.65–66

🔴 Write the pathogenesis of rheumatic fever.

RF is a post-infectious immunologic disease — cross-reaction of antibodies (produced against streptococcal antigens) with host proteins. An autoimmune reaction incited by streptococcal infection.

Molecular mimicry — step by step

  1. Pharyngitis / sore throat by group A β-haemolytic streptococcus (S. pyogenes)
  2. Immune response & antibody formation against streptococcal M proteins (2–3 weeks later)
  3. Cross-reaction of antibodies with host tissues: heart valve glycoprotein, myocardial sarcolemma, cardiac myosin, joints, other tissues
  4. Antigen-antibody complex activates complement → Type II hypersensitivity reaction
  5. Damage to myocardium, heart valves, and other connective tissues

Autoimmunity: in some cases, streptococcal infection can evoke an autoimmune response against cardiac self-antigens.

Robbins 10e p.561; Arif pp.66–67

🔴 Write the morphology of acute and chronic rheumatic heart disease. What is an Aschoff body?

A) Morphology of acute rheumatic heart disease

Focal inflammatory lesions in various tissues, most distinctive within the heart = Aschoff nodules / Aschoff bodies.

Aschoff body consists of:

  • Fragmented collagen & fibrinoid material
  • Foci of lymphocytes (primarily T-cells)
  • Occasional plasma cells
  • Plump activated macrophages = Anitschkow cells (pathognomonic for RF)
  • Occasional multinucleated giant cells = Aschoff cells / Aschoff giant cells

Diffuse inflammation & Aschoff bodies may be found in any of the three heart layers → pancarditis (pericarditis, myocarditis, endocarditis).

Endocardial/valvular lesions: fibrinoid necrosis within cusps or along tendinous cords, with overlying small (1–2 mm) sterile vegetations = verrucae along lines of closure.

B) Morphology of chronic rheumatic heart disease

Gross:

  • Leaflet thickening
  • Commissural fusion & shortening
  • Thickening & fusion of tendinous cords
  • Fibrous bridging across commissures + calcification → "fish mouth" or "buttonhole" stenosis
  • Dilatation of left atrium
  • Mural thrombus in appendage or along LA wall
  • Right ventricular hypertrophy (from long-standing lung changes)

Microscopic: diffuse fibrosis & neovascularisation in valvular leaflets; Aschoff bodies replaced by fibrous scar.

Aschoff body — detailed

The classic lesion of rheumatic fever. Focal inflammatory lesions found in various tissues but most distinctive in the heart.

Anitschkow cells = enlarged macrophages within Aschoff bodies with nuclei showing: longitudinal section → caterpillar-like serrated nuclear chromatin; cross-section → owl-eye appearance of central chromatin mass with perinuclear halo.

Robbins 10e p.561; BRS Pathology 5e p.139; Harsh Mohan 7e pp.420–422; Arif pp.67–68

🔴 Write down the cardiac lesions of rheumatic fever (endocarditis, myocarditis, pericarditis).

Cardiac lesions of RF = pancarditis — focal inflammatory involvement of interstitial tissue of all three heart layers.

1. Rheumatic endocarditis

Involves valvular & mural endocardium → rheumatic valvulitis (chiefly responsible for major cardiac manifestations in chronic RHD) & mural endocarditis.

2. Rheumatic myocarditis

Most characteristic feature = Aschoff bodies scattered throughout interstitial tissue, most frequent in the interventricular septum, left ventricle, and left atrium.

3. Rheumatic pericarditis

Inflammatory involvement of the pericardium → breathlessness or chest pain.

Harsh Mohan 7e pp.420–422; Davidson's 23e p.515; Arif p.69

🔴 Enumerate the complications of rheumatic fever. What is the most common cause of death in acute RF?

Complications

  1. Mitral stenosis
  2. Cardiac hypertrophy (usually right ventricle)
  3. Cardiac dilation
  4. Carditis
  5. Heart failure
  6. Arrhythmias (particularly atrial fibrillation)
  7. Thromboembolic complications
  8. Infective endocarditis

Most common cause of death in acute rheumatic fever: acute rheumatic myocarditis.

Robbins 10e p.562; Pathoma Lecture Notes; Arif pp.69–70

11 · Infective Endocarditis (IE)

🔴 Classify endocarditis. Define infective endocarditis. Compare acute vs subacute IE.

Endocarditis = inflammation of the inner layer (endocardium) of the heart, usually involving the heart valves, characterised by the prototypic lesion — the vegetation.

Classification by etiology

  1. Infective endocarditis (IE): microorganisms are the source
  2. Non-infective endocarditis:
    • Non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis (NBTE) — on previously damaged valves
    • Libman-Sacks endocarditis (LSE) — more often in SLE patients

Infective endocarditis (IE) = microbial infection of heart valves or mural endocardium leading to vegetations composed of thrombotic debris & organisms, often with destruction of underlying cardiac tissue.

Acute IE (ABE) vs Subacute IE (SABE)

FeatureAcute (ABE)Subacute (SABE)
Duration<6 weeks>6 weeks
Most common organismStaphylococcus aureus (50%)Streptococcus viridans (>50%)
VirulenceHighly virulentLess virulent
Previous valve statusUsually previously normalUsually previously damaged
Lesion on valvesInvasive, destructive, suppurativeUsually not invasive or suppurative
Clinical featuresAcute systemic infectionSplenomegaly, clubbing, petechiae

Robbins 10e p.563; BRS Pathology 5e p.141; Harsh Mohan 7e p.425; Arif pp.70–71

🔴 Discuss the etiopathogenesis of infective endocarditis.

A. Etiology

Causative agents:

  • Streptococcus viridans (50–60%)
  • Staphylococcus aureus (20–30%)
  • HACEK group (Haemophilus, Actinobacillus, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, Kingella)
  • Streptococcus bovis
  • Streptococcus pneumoniae
  • Staphylococcus epidermidis
  • Gram-negative enteric bacilli
  • Fungi

Predisposing factors:

  • Bacteraemia / septicaemia / pyaemia sources: dental procedures (tooth brushing, extraction), GI/GU tract infections/surgery, skin infections (boils, carbuncles), respiratory infections, intravenous drug abuse
  • Underlying heart disease: chronic rheumatic valvular disease (~50%), congenital heart disease (VSD, subaortic stenosis, pulmonary stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation, PDA), syphilitic aortic disease, floppy mitral valve, prosthetic valves
  • Impaired host defences: neutropenia, immunodeficiency, malignancy, immunosuppression, diabetes mellitus, alcohol/IV drug abuse

B. Pathogenesis

Organisms entering bloodstream → implanted on cardiac valves or mural endocardium via surface adhesion molecules mediating adherence to injured endocardium.

Robbins 10e p.563; Harsh Mohan 7e p.425; Arif pp.71–72

🔴 What are the complications of infective endocarditis? (cardiac + extracardiac)

A. Cardiac complications

  • Valvular stenosis or insufficiency
  • Perforation, rupture, and aneurysm of valve leaflets
  • Abscesses in the valve ring (ring abscess)
  • Myocardial abscesses
  • Suppurative pericarditis
  • Cardiac failure

B. Extracardiac complications

  • Systemic emboli → infarcts & abscesses in brain, kidneys, spleen, lung
  • Petechiae (skin & conjunctiva)
  • Osler's nodes (SABE) — painful, tender nodules on finger tips of hands & feet
  • Janeway's spots (ABE) — painless, non-tender subcutaneous maculopapular lesions on pulp of fingers
  • Glomerulonephritis (antigen-antibody complex deposition in glomerulus)
🎯 Causes of death in IE: cardiac failure, persistent infection, embolism to vital organs, renal failure, rupture of mycotic aneurysm of cerebral arteries.

Robbins 10e p.564; Harsh Mohan 7e p.428; Arif pp.72–73

12 · Cardiomyopathies

🔴 Define cardiomyopathy. Classify it (primary vs secondary; anatomical patterns).

Cardiomyopathy (literally "heart muscle disease") = any cardiac dysfunction resulting from a myocardial abnormality.

By aetiology

  • Primary cardiomyopathies: involve predominantly the heart; may be genetic or acquired (viral myocarditis, anthracycline cardiotoxicity)
  • Secondary cardiomyopathies: myocardial involvement as component of systemic/multiorgan disorder (haemochromatosis, amyloidosis)

By anatomic abnormalities (three patterns)

  1. Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) (including arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy) — most common (90% of cases)
  2. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
  3. Restrictive cardiomyopathy

Robbins 10e p.567; Arif p.73

13 · Myocarditis

🔴 Define myocarditis. Enumerate its causes (infectious, immune, unknown).

Myocarditis = a diverse group of pathologic entities in which infectious microorganisms and/or immune reactions injure the heart.

Major causes

1. Infections

  • Viruses: Coxsackievirus, Echo virus, influenza, HIV, cytomegalovirus
  • Chlamydiae: Chlamydia psittaci
  • Rickettsiae: R. typhi (typhus fever)
  • Bacteria: C. diphtheriae, N. meningitidis, Borrelia (Lyme disease)
  • Fungi: Candida
  • Protozoa: Trypanosoma (Chagas disease), toxoplasmosis
  • Helminths: Trichinosis

2. Immune / hypersensitivity

  • Post-viral
  • Post-streptococcal (rheumatic fever)
  • Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)
  • Drug hypersensitivity (methyldopa, sulfonamides)
  • Transplant rejection
  • Immune checkpoint inhibitor therapies

3. Unknown

  • Giant cell myocarditis

Robbins 10e p.575; Arif p.74

14 · Pericarditis

🔴 Define and classify pericarditis. Enumerate its causes.

Pericarditis = inflammation of the pericardium. May occur secondary to cardiac, thoracic, or systemic disorders, metastases, or cardiac surgery.

Classification

TypeSubtypes
A. Acute pericarditis1. Fibrinous & serofibrinous (most frequent) · 2. Purulent/suppurative · 3. Haemorrhagic · 4. Caseous
B. Chronic / healed pericarditis1. Adhesive mediastino-pericarditis: pericardial sac obliterated, adherence to surrounding structures → strain on cardiac function · 2. Constrictive pericarditis: heart encased in dense fibrous/fibrocalcific scar → limits diastolic expansion → restricts cardiac output (resembles restrictive cardiomyopathy)

Causes of pericarditis

  1. Infectious agents: viruses, pyogenic bacteria, tuberculosis, fungi, parasites
  2. Presumably immunologically mediated: rheumatic fever, SLE, scleroderma, post-cardiotomy, post-MI (Dressler syndrome), drug hypersensitivity
  3. Miscellaneous: MI, uraemia, cardiac surgery, neoplasia, trauma, radiation

Robbins 10e pp.577–578; Arif pp.74–75

🔴 Give an account of constrictive pericarditis.

Constrictive pericarditis: the heart is encased in a dense, fibrous or fibrocalcific scar that limits diastolic expansion and seriously restricts cardiac output — resembling restrictive cardiomyopathy.

  • History of previous pericarditis may or may not be present
  • Pericardial space obliterated; heart surrounded by dense, adherent layer of scar ± calcification
  • Scar often 0.5–1.0 cm thick
  • Extreme cases: resembles a plaster mold → concretio cordis

Robbins 10e p.578; Arif p.75

15 · Tumours of the Heart

🔴 Name the tumours of the heart (primary benign, primary malignant, metastatic).

1. Primary cardiac tumours

Benign:

  • Myxoma — most common primary tumour of the adult heart
  • Fibroma
  • Lipoma
  • Papillary fibroelastoma
  • Rhabdomyoma

Malignant:

  • Sarcoma (angiosarcoma)

2. Secondary / metastatic neoplasms

Most frequently from lung & breast carcinomas, melanomas, leukaemias, and lymphomas.

Robbins 10e pp.527–580; Arif p.75
