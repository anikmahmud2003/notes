# Ch 22 — Semen Analysis     <i>Full-detail flashcards (Q→A) · All abbreviations expanded · Robbins 4-pillar answers</i>
### 🧠 Big Picture — Semen Analysis (boss-level overview)
<b>Semen analysis</b> evaluates the quality and quantity of seminal fluid (liquid portion) and spermatozoa (microscopic moving cells) secreted during ejaculation. It is the <b>cornerstone investigation for male infertility</b> and also serves medicolegal purposes (paternity, rape cases) and post-vasectomy follow-up.
<b>What is measured:</b> 🔴 <b>Volume</b> (2–5 ml) → <b>Appearance/Colour</b> (opalescent, greyish-white) → <b>pH</b> (≥7.8) → <b>Sperm concentration</b> (20–110 million/ml) → <b>Motility</b> (≥50% forward) → <b>Morphology</b> (≥30–50% normal) → <b>Viability</b> (≥75%) → <b>Liquefaction</b> (within 30 min) → <b>Fructose</b> (1.5–6.5 mg/dl) → <b>Leucocytes</b> (<1 million/ml).
<b>Key terminology map:</b> Normozoospermia (all normal) → Oligospermia (low count) → Asthenozoospermia (low motility) → Teratozoospermia (abnormal morphology) → <b>Azoospermia</b> (no sperm) → <b>Necrozoospermia</b> (all dead) → Polyzoospermia (excessively high count). <b>Causes of oligospermia/azoospermia</b> are classified as pre-testicular, testicular, and post-testicular — always think systematically!

## 1 · Indications of Semen Analysis
<b>🔴 What are the indications of semen analysis? Enumerate the medicolegal importance.</b>

<b>Indications of semen analysis</b> (<b>Very Important for VIVA — asked repeatedly DU, RMU, CMU, BUP, SU</b>):

Evaluation of male infertility — the most common indication; assesses count, motility, morphology.
Follow-up after vasectomy — to confirm successful occlusion (absence of sperm).
Artificial insemination — to evaluate donor or partner semen quality before assisted reproduction.
Paternity test (forensic medicine purpose) — DNA profiling from seminal material.
In suspected rape cases — for identification of the accused (forensic identification).

<b>Medicolegal importance:</b> semen analysis is crucial in (a) paternity disputes — linking biological fatherhood, (b) sexual assault cases — identifying the perpetrator through spermatozoa or seminal markers, and (c) post-vasectomy confirmation — legal proof of sterility.

## 2 · Semen Collection Procedure
<b>How is semen collected for analysis? Describe the collection and transport procedure.</b>

A. Preparation of the Patient

Patient should be instructed to collect semen after 3–5 days of sexual abstinence.
Should be obtained by masturbation (self-stimulation) or collected at home during intercourse by coitus interruptus (withdrawal method).
Condom should be avoided, as condoms contain spermicidal agents that kill sperm.
Patient should evacuate his bladder before ejaculation.
Patient must wash hands before collection.

B. Collection of Specimen & Transport

Should be collected in a dry, clean, plastic container provided by the laboratory.
Condom collection is unacceptable (spermicidal contamination).
Patient's name, age, date, and time of collection should be recorded on the container.
Sample should be taken to the laboratory within 1 hour of collection and kept at body temperature during transport.

<b>WHO cut-offs (note differences):</b>

Most WHO laboratories use a cut-off of 50% motility as the lower limit of normal.
Greater than 30% normal morphology is accepted by WHO (some newer WHO 2010+ criteria use stricter >4% normal forms).

<b>Low semen volume</b> may indicate <b>retrograde ejaculation</b> (semen enters the bladder instead of being expelled externally).

<b>What information can we get from semen analysis? Mention the features examined and important messages in reporting.</b>

<b>Basic semen analysis measures (4 parameters):</b>

Semen volume
Sperm concentration (count)
Sperm motility
Sperm morphology

<b>Additional tests:</b>

pH — high pH may be associated with ejaculatory tract obstruction or seminal vesicle dysfunction.
Fructose levels — absence of fructose may indicate ejaculatory tract obstruction or seminal vesicle dysfunction.
WBC (leucocyte) counts within semen — to detect infection (leucospermia).

<b>Important messages in semen analysis reporting:</b> Always comment on (1) volume, (2) sperm count, (3) motility percentage, (4) morphology, (5) liquefaction status, (6) presence/absence of agglutination, (7) WBC count, (8) fructose (if requested).

Ref: Lecture SSMC, BSMMU Lab. Medicine & AFIP Clinical Pathology

<b>Define normozoospermia.</b>

<b>Normozoospermia:</b> a condition where all semen parameters are within normal reference ranges — normal volume, normal sperm count (>20 million/ml), normal motility (>50% forward motile), and normal morphology (>30% normal forms). The male is considered <b>fertile</b>.

Ref: Lecture SSMC, BSMMU Lab. Medicine & AFIP Clinical Pathology

## 5 · Sperm Analysis Terminology (Classification)

<b>Complete sperm analysis terminology (must-know for VIVA):</b>

## 6 · Male Fertility Status by Examining Semen
<b>How can you know male fertility status by examining semen?</b>

<b>Three parameters determine fertility status:</b>

A. Sperm Count → Fertility Correlation

<table>
<tr><th>Condition</th><th>Sperm Count</th><th>Fertility Status</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Normozoospermia</strong></td><td>Normal (&gt;20 million/ml)</td><td><span class="good">Fertile</span></td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Oligozoospermia</strong></td><td>&lt;20 million/ml<br/>(Mild–moderate: 5–20; Severe: &lt;5)</td><td><span class="warn">Susceptible to infertility</span></td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Azoospermia</strong></td><td>No sperm in semen</td><td><span class="hot">Infertile</span></td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Necrozoospermia</strong></td><td>All sperms non-viable / dead</td><td><span class="hot">Infertile</span></td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Polyzoospermia</strong></td><td>&gt;350 million/ml</td><td><span class="warn">Susceptible to infertility</span></td></tr>
</table>

B. Motility of Sperm

For normal fertility, <b>>50% spermatozoa must be motile</b> (forward progressive motility). Most WHO laboratories use 50% as the lower limit of normal.

C. Morphology of Sperm

For normal fertility, <b>>30% of sperms must be morphologically normal</b> (normal head, body & tail). WHO accepts >30%; newer WHO 2010 criteria use >4% strict normal forms.

## 7 · Oligospermia — Short Note
<b>🔴 What is oligospermia? Give its causes.</b>

<b>Oligospermia:</b> less than <b>20 million sperm/ml of semen</b>. Classified as mild–moderate (5–20 million/ml) or severe (<5 million/ml).

Causes (organised systematically — pre-testicular, testicular, post-testicular)

A. Idiopathic

No identifiable cause found.

B. Pre-testicular Causes

Hypogonadism due to any cause
Drugs, alcohol, smoking
Heat exposure (to the testes)
Strenuous riding of bicycle (repeated perineal pressure & heat)
Tight-fitting undergarments (heat retention)
Diabetes mellitus (DM)
Cancer treatment — radiotherapy / chemotherapy
Severe illness: malignancy, liver failure, renal failure

C. Testicular Causes

Age (advancing age)
Genetic defect
Chromosomal abnormality — e.g., Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)
Cryptorchidism (undescended testis)
Orchitis (testicular infection — e.g., mumps orchitis)
Varicocele, hydrocele
Trauma / surgery to the testes
Neoplasm: seminoma

D. Post-testicular Causes

Infection: prostatitis
Obstruction in vas deferens
Obstruction in ejaculatory duct
Absence of vas deferens (congenital bilateral absence — associated with CFTR mutations / cystic fibrosis carrier state)

Ref: Lecture SSMC, BSMMU Lab. Medicine & AFIP Clinical Pathology

## 8 · Azoospermia — Short Note
<b>🔴 What is azoospermia? What are the causes of azoospermia?</b>

<b>Azoospermia:</b> <b>no sperm in semen — complete lack of spermatozoa in the ejaculate</b>. It occurs in approximately <b>5% of infertile men</b>.

Causes (pre-testicular, testicular, post-testicular + idiopathic)

A. Idiopathic

No identifiable cause found.

B. Pre-testicular Causes

Hypogonadism due to any cause
Drugs, alcohol
Diabetes mellitus (DM)
Cancer treatment — radiotherapy / chemotherapy
Severe illness: malignancy, liver failure, renal failure
Haemochromatosis (iron overload → pituitary/gonadal damage)
Sickle cell anaemia

C. Testicular Causes

Primary / Secondary testicular failure
Genetic defect
Chromosomal abnormality — e.g., Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)
Cryptorchidism (undescended testis)
Orchitis
Varicocele, hydrocele
Trauma / surgery
Sperm autoimmunity — anti-sperm antibodies (Ab)

D. Post-testicular Causes

Infection: epididymitis, prostatitis
Obstruction in vas deferens
Obstruction in ejaculatory duct
Absence of vas deferens
Vasectomy (surgical occlusion)

Ref: Lecture SSMC, BSMMU Lab. Medicine & AFIP Clinical Pathology

<b>A 36-year-old male presented with infertility. Semen analysis showed absent sperm. Comment on the semen analysis and give possible causes.</b>

<b>Comment:</b> <b>Azoospermia</b> — complete absence of spermatozoa in the ejaculate.

<b>Possible causes:</b> (organise as pre-testicular, testicular, post-testicular)

Pre-testicular: hypogonadism, DM, drugs/chemotherapy, severe systemic illness, haemochromatosis, sickle cell anaemia.
Testicular: primary/secondary testicular failure, Klinefelter syndrome, cryptorchidism, orchitis, varicocele, anti-sperm antibodies, prior trauma/surgery.
Post-testicular: epididymitis/prostatitis, vas deferens obstruction (congenital or acquired), ejaculatory duct obstruction, vasectomy.
Idiopathic: no identifiable cause.

Ref: Lecture SSMC, BSMMU Lab. Medicine & AFIP Clinical Pathology

## 9 · Oligoasthenozoospermia
<b>Short note: Oligoasthenozoospermia.</b>

<b>Oligoasthenozoospermia:</b> a sperm disorder involving two abnormalities simultaneously:

Oligospermia: low sperm count (<20 million/ml).
Asthenozoospermia: abnormal sperm motility — more than 60% of sperm being immotile or unable to move in a straight direction.

Causes

In most cases, causes are unknown / idiopathic.
Testicular diseases — e.g., orchitis.
Some genetic disorders — e.g., Klinefelter syndrome, immotile cilia syndrome (Kartagener syndrome).
Immunologic — anti-sperm antibodies causing agglutination and reduced motility.

Treatment

Treatment of <b>reversible causes</b>, if any (e.g., infection → antibiotics; varicocele → surgery; hormonal → correction).

Ref: Lecture SSMC, BSMMU Lab. Medicine & AFIP Clinical Pathology

## 10 · Etiological Factors of Male Infertility
<b>🔴 Mention the etiological factors of male infertility.</b>

<b>Etiological factors of male infertility</b> (<b>Very Important for VIVA</b> — asked DU-10Ja):

A. Failure of Spermatogenesis

Incomplete development of testis / undescended testis (cryptorchidism)
Absence of testis
Orchitis — due to mumps, influenza, Chlamydia
Cryptorchidism (repeated for emphasis)
Chromosomal abnormality: Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)
Damage of testis — due to radiation, trauma, or heat
Diseases of testis — TB (tuberculosis), syphilis, seminoma
Varicocele (↑scrotal temperature → impaired spermatogenesis)
Drugs: nicotine, sulfonamide, antihistamines, cytotoxic drugs, alcohol, pesticides
Anti-sperm antibody (autoimmune destruction)
Endocrine disease:
Hypothalamic–pituitary dysfunction (trauma, radiation, tumour, surgery)
Hyperprolactinaemia
Thyroid disorders (thyrotoxicosis / hyperthyroidism)
Adrenal hyperplasia
DNA damage by reactive oxygen species (ROS), fever, and high testicular temperature
General factors: diabetes mellitus (DM), smoking

B. Failure of Transport of Semen

Bilateral obstruction of <b>epididymis, vas deferens, or ejaculatory duct</b>.

C. Failure to Deposit Sperm in Vagina

Impotency (erectile dysfunction)
Retrograde ejaculation (semen enters bladder)
Premature ejaculation

D. Errors of Seminal Fluid

Low volume, physicochemical abnormality of semen — including azoospermia, oligospermia, teratospermia, hypospermia, etc.

## 11 · Pseudocyesis (False Pregnancy)
<b>Define pseudocyesis.</b>

<b>Pseudocyesis (false pregnancy):</b> a syndrome in which a woman shows all the symptoms and signs of being pregnant except for the presence of a fetus. Her belly grows like a pregnant woman, and she can even go into false labour. It is a psychosomatic condition.

Ref: Lecture SSMC, BSMMU Lab. Medicine & AFIP Clinical Pathology

## 12 · Infertility Test (Male & Female)
<b>🔴 How will you proceed to diagnose a case of male and female infertility? Write a short note on infertility test.</b>

<b>Infertility test:</b> tests meant to establish the reason why a couple is not able to conceive naturally. Overall, <b>20–30% of infertility cases are due to a problem with the male partner</b>.

A. Diagnosis of Male Infertility

Medical, sexual, and social history
General physical examination (including examination of genitals)
Specific fertility tests:
Semen analysis — physical, chemical, and microscopic test
Immunological assay / Anti-sperm antibody test
Sperm function test
Post-ejaculatory urine analysis (to rule out retrograde ejaculation)
Hormone testing (testosterone and other male hormones)
Transrectal and scrotal ultrasound
Genetic testing

B. Diagnosis of Female Infertility

Medical, sexual, and social history
General physical examination (including regular gynaecological examination)
Specific fertility tests:
Hormone assay — LH (luteinising hormone), FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone), oestradiol, progesterone, prolactin, thyroid & pituitary hormones
Hysterosalpingography — special X-ray to locate blockage or problems in the uterus and fallopian tubes
Ovulation testing — blood test to measure hormone levels to determine ovulation
Ovarian reserve test — determines quality and quantity of eggs available for ovulation
Diagnostic laparoscopy — identifies endometriosis, scarring, blockages of fallopian tubes, ovarian & uterine problems
Imaging tests — pelvic ultrasound, hysterosonography, hysteroscopy
Endometrial biopsy — to verify ovulation and inspect uterine lining
Pap's smear
Genetic testing — to determine genetic defects causing infertility

Ref: Lecture SSMC, BSMMU Lab. Medicine & AFIP Clinical Pathology

## 13 · Hormones for Infertility Evaluation
<b>What are the common hormones done in male and female to evaluate infertility?</b>

A. Common Hormones in Male Infertility Evaluation

Testosterone (primary male sex hormone — low levels → impaired spermatogenesis)
Thyroid hormone (T3, T4, TSH — thyroid dysfunction impairs fertility)
Prolactin (hyperprolactinaemia suppresses GnRH → hypogonadism)

B. Common Hormones in Female Infertility Evaluation

Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH) — measured on day 3 of the menstrual cycle; elevated FSH → diminished ovarian reserve
Luteinising Hormone (LH) — LH surge triggers ovulation
Progesterone — to confirm ovulation (elevated in luteal phase)
Thyroid hormone (T3, T4, TSH)
Prolactin — hyperprolactinaemia inhibits ovulation
Testosterone (elevated in PCOS — polycystic ovarian syndrome)
Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH) — marker of ovarian reserve

## 🔁 Rapid-Fire MCQ & Viva Sweep (incl. folded Clinical Pathology MCQs)
<b>🎯 True/False practice — Normal values & liquefaction</b>
<b>Normal semen is characterised by — 2 to 5 ml per ejaculation (T/F)</b>
<b>True</b> — Normal ejaculated volume is 2–5 ml.
<b>Sperm count 20 million per ml is normal (T/F)</b>
<b>True</b> — Normal sperm concentration is 20–110 million/ml.
<b>>70% motile in first hour is normal (T/F)</b>
<b>False</b> — Normal is 60–65% actively motile within 1 hour; most WHO labs use ≥50% as the lower limit.
<b>50% normal in morphology is accepted by WHO (T/F)</b>
<b>True</b> — >50% morphologically normal (normal head, body & tail) is the traditional criterion; WHO also accepts >30%.
<b>Normal liquefaction is completed within half an hour (T/F)</b>
<b>True</b> — Complete liquefaction should occur within 30 minutes.
<b>Normal liquefaction time is 20–30 minutes (T/F) — from Semen analysis MCQ</b>
<b>False</b> — Normal liquefaction time is within 30 minutes, not "20–30 minutes" — the statement is considered False in the MCQ key (likely because the accepted window is simply "≤30 min").
<b>🎯 True/False practice — Sperm terminology</b>
<b>Sperm <20 million/ml is oligospermia (T/F)</b>
<b>True</b> — Oligospermia = sperm count <20 million/ml.
<b>Prolonged liquefaction time indicates sterility (T/F)</b>
<b>True</b> — Significantly prolonged liquefaction may indicate pathology and is associated with reduced fertility.
<b>40 million/ml sperm count is hyperzoospermia (T/F)</b>
<b>False</b> — 40 million/ml is within normal range (20–110 million/ml). Hyperzoospermia (polyzoospermia) = >350 million/ml.
<b>No spermatozoa either living or dead is called asthenozoospermia (T/F)</b>
<b>False</b> — Absence of all spermatozoa = azoospermia. Asthenozoospermia = reduced motility.
<b>🎯 True/False practice — Indications of semen analysis</b>
<b>Indications of semen analysis include: undescended testis (F), following vasectomy (T), hypogonadism (F), testicular neoplasm (F), following chemotherapy (F)?</b>
Undescended testis <b>F</b> · following vasectomy <b>T</b> · hypogonadism <b>F</b> · testicular neoplasm <b>F</b> · following chemotherapy <b>F</b> . Indications are: infertility evaluation, post-vasectomy follow-up, artificial insemination, paternity test, suspected rape case.
<b>Indications of semen analysis are: infertility (T), paternity test (T), after vagotomy (F), rape (T), after vasectomy (T)?</b>
Infertility <b>T</b> · paternity test <b>T</b> · after vagotomy <b>F</b> (vagotomy is a gastric surgery, irrelevant) · rape <b>T</b> · after vasectomy <b>T</b> .
<b>Semen Analysis — One-Liners & Mnemonics (test yourself, then reveal)</b>

🎯 One-liner: Low semen volume indicates?

<b>Retrograde ejaculation</b> (semen enters bladder instead of being expelled externally).

🎯 One-liner: Most WHO laboratories use a cut-off of what percentage motility as the lower limit of normal?

<b>50% motility.</b>

🎯 One-liner: Greater than what percentage normal morphology is accepted by WHO?

<b>>30% normal morphology</b> (traditional WHO criteria).

🎯 One-liner: In azoospermia, what percentage of infertile men are affected?

<b>5% of infertile men.</b>

🎯 One-liner: What is polyzoospermia?

<b>Sperm count >350 million/ml</b> — associated with susceptibility to infertility (paradoxically high count can also impair fertility).

🎯 One-liner: Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) is a cause of which sperm disorder?

<b>Oligospermia / Azoospermia</b> — chromosomal abnormality causing primary testicular failure.

🎯 One-liner: Absence of fructose in semen suggests?

<b>Ejaculatory tract obstruction or seminal vesicle dysfunction</b> (fructose is secreted by seminal vesicles).

<b>Condoms contain spermicidal agents</b> that kill sperm and give false results.
