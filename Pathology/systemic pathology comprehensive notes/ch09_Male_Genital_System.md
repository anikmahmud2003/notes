# Ch 09 — Male Genital System     <i>Full-detail flashcards (Q→A) · All abbreviations expanded · Robbins 4-pillar answers</i>
### 🧠 Big Picture — Male Genital System (boss-level overview)
<b>Three anatomical regions</b> dominate this chapter: <b>Penis</b> (inflammations → balanoposthitis; tumours → squamous cell carcinoma), <b>Testis</b> (the king — germ cell tumours = 95% of all testicular tumours; seminoma is the most common single type at ~50%; non-seminomatous GCTs include embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac tumour, choriocarcinoma, teratoma; sex cord-stromal tumours include Leydig & Sertoli cell tumours), and <b>Prostate</b> (benign prostatic hyperplasia/BPH = most common benign disease >50 yr; adenocarcinoma = 2nd most common cancer death in men).
🔴 <b>Must-know high-yield threads:</b> (1) <b>Cryptorchidism</b> → impaired descent → infertility + ↑risk of testicular cancer · (2) <b>Seminoma</b> = bulky, homogeneous, grey-white mass; radio- & chemosensitive; best prognosis among GCTs · (3) <b>Choriocarcinoma</b> = syncytiotrophoblast + cytotrophoblast; ↑↑hCG; haematogenous spread early · (4) <b>BPH</b> = DHT-driven nodular hyperplasia of periurethral zone; NOT premalignant · (5) <b>Prostatic adenocarcinoma</b> = peripheral zone (70%); osteoblastic bony mets; ↑PSA; Gleason grading · (6) <b>PSA</b> = organ-specific but NOT cancer-specific; elevated in carcinoma, BPH, prostatitis, infarction, instrumentation, ejaculation.
Robbins 10e, Ch 21 (Male Genital System), pp. 963–983 · Arif 15e (2025) Vol-2, pp.250–263
## 1 · Overview — Classification of Diseases
<b>🔴 Classify the diseases of the male genital tract. Enumerate diseases of each part.</b>

1. Penis

Congenital anomalies: Hypospadias, epispadias, phimosis
Inflammations: Syphilis, gonorrhoea, chancroid, granuloma inguinale, lymphogranuloma venereum, genital herpes, balanoposthitis (infection of glans + prepuce caused by Candida albicans, anaerobic bacteria, Gardnerella, pyogenic bacteria)
Tumours:
Benign — Condyloma acuminatum
Malignant — Squamous carcinoma in situ / penile intraepithelial neoplasia (PIN); Invasive squamous cell carcinoma

2. Testis & Epididymis

Congenital: Cryptorchidism (undescended testis, most common), absence of one/both testes, synorchism (fusion of testes)
Regressive: Atrophy, decreased fertility
Inflammatory: Non-specific epididymitis/orchitis, granulomatous (autoimmune) orchitis, infections (gonorrhoea, mumps, TB, syphilis)
Vascular: Torsion
Spermatic cord & paratesticular tumours: benign (lipomas, adenomatoid tumour); malignant (rhabdomyosarcoma in children, liposarcoma in adults)
Testicular tumours: see detailed classification below

3. Prostate

Inflammations: Acute & chronic bacterial prostatitis, chronic abacterial prostatitis, granulomatous prostatitis, autoimmune prostatitis
Benign enlargement: Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) / nodular hyperplasia
Tumours: Primary — adenocarcinoma (most common), ductal adenocarcinoma, small cell (neuroendocrine) carcinoma; Secondary — urothelial cancer, testicular metastases

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.250–251; Robbins 10e pp.963–983

## 2 · Penis
<b>Describe the important inflammations and tumours of the penis.</b>

Infections of the penis (STIs)

Syphilis — Treponema pallidum; chancre (painless ulcer)
Gonorrhoea — Neisseria gonorrhoeae; urethral discharge
Chancroid — Haemophilus ducreyi; painful genital ulcer + inguinal lymphadenopathy
Granuloma inguinale — Klebsiella granulomatis; painless, progressive genital ulceration
Lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV) — Chlamydia trachomatis L1–L3 serovars; inguinal lymph node enlargement with suppuration
Genital herpes — Herpes simplex virus (HSV); vesicular ulcers, recurrent

Balanoposthitis

Infection of the glans penis and prepuce; causative agents: <i>Candida albicans</i>, anaerobic bacteria, <i>Gardnerella vaginalis</i>, pyogenic bacteria.

Tumours of the penis

Condyloma acuminatum — benign; caused by HPV (human papillomavirus) types 6, 11; wart-like growths
Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) — malignant; arises from squamous epithelium of the glans or prepuce; precursor: squamous carcinoma in situ / penile intraepithelial neoplasia (PIN)

## 4 · Cryptorchidism
<b>🔴 What is cryptorchidism? Describe its morphology, complications, and treatment.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Complete or partial failure of the intra-abdominal testes to descend into the scrotal sac. Present in about <b>1% of 1-year-old male infants</b>. Associated with testicular dysfunction and <b>increased risk of testicular cancer</b>.

Location of undescended testis

70% — inguinal ring
25% — abdomen
5% — other sites along descent path

Morphology

<b>Gross:</b> Usually unilateral (bilateral in 25%). Cryptorchid testes are small and firm.

<b>Microscopic:</b> Changes begin as early as <b>2 years of age</b>:

Thickening of the basement membrane of the seminiferous tubules
Progressive loss of spermatogonia → tubules contain only Sertoli cells
Increase in interstitial stroma
Leydig cells are relatively prominent
Progressive tubular atrophy

Complications

Sterility (infertility)
Inguinal hernia (in about 10–20% of cases)
Development of testicular cancer (↑ risk 10–40×)

<b>Treatment:</b> Surgical correction (<b>orchiopexy</b>).

🎯 <b>Testicular atrophy causes = "A-TIP":</b> Atherosclerotic narrowing of blood supply (old age) · Inflammatory orchitis · Failure of genetic origin (Klinefelter syndrome) · Disuse atrophy (high FSH stimulation).

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.252–253; Robbins 10e pp.966–967

## 5 · Seminoma
<b>🔴 Give the morphology (gross + microscopic) and prognosis of seminoma.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> A malignant germ cell tumour of the testis — the <b>most common testicular GCT (50%)</b>.

Characteristics

Analogous to ovarian dysgerminoma and CNS germinoma
Peak incidence: fourth decade; almost never in infants
Presentation: painless enlargement of the testis
Sometimes associated with ↑serum human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)
Very radiosensitive and chemosensitive — often curable

Subtypes of seminoma

Classic (typical) seminoma — most common
Spermatocytic seminoma
Anaplastic seminoma

Morphology — Macroscopic

Bulky masses, sometimes 10× the size of the normal testis
Homogeneous, grey-white, lobulated cut surface
Usually devoid of haemorrhage or necrosis
Tunica albuginea usually not penetrated; occasional extension to epididymis, spermatic cord, or scrotal sac

Morphology — Microscopic

Sheets of uniform cells divided into poorly demarcated lobules by delicate fibrous septa containing lymphocytic infiltrate
Ill-defined granulomas may be present
Classic seminoma cell: round to polyhedral, distinct cell membrane, clear or watery-appearing cytoplasm, large central nucleus with one or two prominent nucleoli
Cytoplasm contains varying amounts of glycogen

Prognosis

Better than other germ cell tumours. Highly radiosensitive and chemosensitive. Most patients are cured by orchiectomy with or without chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

🎯 <b>Seminoma cell features = "CLEAR-L":</b> Clear cytoplasm (glycogen) · Lobulated growth · Equal/uniform nuclei · Avascular (avascular fibrous septa) · Raised hCG (sometimes) · Lymphocytic septa.

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.253–255; Robbins 10e pp.970, 973; BRS Pathology 5e p.289

## 6 · Choriocarcinoma
<b>🔴 Short note: Choriocarcinoma of the testis.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> A highly malignant type of germ cell tumour (GCT) that can occur as an element of other GCTs.

Characteristics

Analogous to choriocarcinoma of the ovary
Incidence peaks in the second to third decade
Causes ↑↑serum hCG (β-hCG)
Highly chemosensitive

Morphology — Macroscopic

Often no testicular enlargement; detected only as a small palpable nodule
Small — rarely larger than 5 cm in diameter
Haemorrhage and necrosis extremely common

Morphology — Microscopic

Two intimately juxtaposed cell types:
Syncytiotrophoblasts — large multinucleated cells, abundant eosinophilic vacuolated cytoplasm containing hCG
Cytotrophoblasts — more regular, polygonal, distinct borders, clear cytoplasm, single fairly uniform nucleus; grow in cords or sheets

🎯 <b>Choriocarcinoma key points = "BLEED":</b> Both cell types present · Large hCG elevation · Early haematogenous spread · Erodes blood vessels · Distant metastases (lung, brain).

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.254–255; Robbins 10e p.972; BRS Pathology 5e p.291

## 7 · Teratoma
<b>🔴 Define teratoma. Give its classification, morphology, and malignant potential.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> GCTs having various cellular or organoid components reminiscent of normal derivatives of <b>more than one germ layer</b>.

May occur at any age from infancy to adulthood. <b>Most frequently malignant in the testis</b> (unlike ovary where mature teratoma is almost always benign).

Classification (three subtypes)

Mature teratoma: Almost always malignant in the testis (contrast: benign in ovary)
Immature teratoma: Primitive neural elements present; behaviour is malignant
Teratoma with malignant transformation: Contains malignant tissue such as squamous cell carcinoma, mucin-secreting adenocarcinoma, sarcoma

Morphology — Gross

Usually large (5–10 cm in diameter)
Heterogeneous appearance with solid (sometimes cartilaginous) and cystic areas
Haemorrhage and necrosis usually indicate admixture with embryonal carcinoma, choriocarcinoma, or both

Morphology — Microscopic

Collections of differentiated cells or organoid structures: neural tissue, muscle bundles, cartilage, squamous epithelium with/without skin adnexa, thyroid gland-like structures, bronchial epithelium, bits of intestinal wall or brain substance
Embedded in fibrous or myxoid stroma
Elements may be mature (adult-tissue appearance) or immature (fetal/embryonic-tissue features)
Somatic-type malignant transformation: focus of SCC, adenocarcinoma, sarcoma

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.255–256; Robbins 10e p.973; BRS Pathology 5e p.290

## 9 · Spread & Laboratory Diagnosis of Testicular Tumours
<b>🔴 Mention the mode of spread of testicular tumours and the laboratory workup.</b>

Mode of spread

Testicular tumours spread by <b>both lymphatic and haematogenous routes:</b>

Lymphatic spread: Common to all forms. Retroperitoneal para-aortic nodes are the first to be involved. Subsequent spread to mediastinal and supraclavicular nodes.
Haematogenous spread: Primarily to the lungs; liver, brain, and bones may also be involved.

<b>Seminoma:</b> metastases typically involve lymph nodes first; haematogenous spread occurs later.

<b>NSGCT:</b> metastasise earlier and more frequently via the haematogenous route.

Laboratory workup (for a testicular mass)

Provisional diagnosis: Testicular tumour (most probably GCT)
Confirmatory: Radical inguinal orchiectomy followed by histopathology
New techniques: Immunohistochemistry (IHC), molecular diagnosis, flow cytometry
Tumour markers:
α-Fetoprotein (AFP)
Human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG)
Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

Testicular tumour markers — importance

Widely used in: (1) Initial evaluation of testicular masses, (2) Staging of GCTs, (3) Assessing tumour burden, (4) Monitoring response to therapy (follow-up).

Nice to Know — Which tumours raise which markers?

Scenario 1 — Sudden severe pain

<b>Probable diagnosis: Testicular torsion.</b> Torsion of the spermatic cord → acute ischaemia → sudden, severe testicular pain. Requires emergency surgical detorsion.

Scenario 2 — Painless testicular swelling (25 yr)

<b>Provisional diagnosis:</b> Testicular tumour (most likely GCT).

<b>Tumour markers to advise:</b>

α-Fetoprotein (AFP)
Human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG)
Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

<b>Confirmatory:</b> Radical inguinal orchiectomy → histopathology. Additional: IHC, molecular diagnosis, flow cytometry.

## 11 · Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)
<b>🔴 Give the pathology/morphology, pathogenesis, effects, and clinical features of benign prostatic hyperplasia.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> BPH (also called nodular hyperplasia) is the <b>most common benign prostatic disease in men older than age 50 years</b>. Results from nodular hyperplasia of prostatic stromal and epithelial cells; often leads to urinary obstruction.

It is NOT a premalignant lesion.

<b>Age of incidence:</b> Over 50 years, most often between 60 & 70.

Pathogenesis

The main androgen in the prostate is dihydrotestosterone (DHT), the ultimate mediator of prostatic growth
DHT is synthesised from testosterone via type 2 5α-reductase
DHT binds to and activates androgen receptors (ARs) in both stromal and epithelial cells
ARs translocate from cytoplasm to nucleus → activate transcription of androgen-dependent genes → encode growth factors and their receptors
DHT-induced growth factors increase stromal cell proliferation and decrease epithelial cell death

Morphology — Gross

Prostate enlarged; weight becomes 3–5× normal (60–100 g)
Nodules in lateral lobe compress urethra to a slit-like orifice
Cross-section: hyperplastic nodules of various size, colour, consistency
Gland-rich nodules: yellow-pink, soft, exude milky-white prostatic fluid
Fibromuscular stroma-rich nodules: pale grey, firm
No true capsule

Morphology — Microscopic

Nodules contain small-to-large-to-cystically dilated glands separated by bland spindle-shaped stromal cells
Glands lined by two cell layers: inner columnar secretory cell + outer cuboidal/flattened basal epithelium
Gland infolding may produce papillary architecture
Markedly enlarged glands may have compromised vascular supply → prostatic infarcts; adjacent squamous metaplasia may occur

Effects of BPH

Prostatic enlargement + stromal smooth muscle contraction → urinary obstruction
↑Resistance to urinary outflow → bladder hypertrophy & distension + urine retention
Incomplete bladder emptying → reservoir of residual urine → common source of infection

Clinical Features

Increased urinary frequency & nocturia
Difficulty starting and stopping the urinary stream
Overflow dribbling
Dysuria (painful micturition)
↑Risk of bacterial infections of the bladder and kidney
Sudden acute urinary retention (requires emergency catheterisation)

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.258–260; Robbins 10e pp.976–977

## 12 · Prostatic Carcinoma
<b>🔴 Describe the morphology, spread, clinical features, and treatment of prostatic carcinoma.</b>

<b>Prostate cancer is the second most common cause of cancer-related death in men</b> (surpassed only by lung cancer).

<b>Incidence:</b> Men over age 50.

Etiology

Age, race, family history
Hormone levels (androgen dependence)
Environmental influences
Precursor lesion: prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PIN)

Morphology — Gross

In ~70% of cases, arises in the peripheral zone
Cross-section: neoplastic tissue is gritty and firm

Morphology — Microscopic

Glands arranged in well-defined, easily recognised patterns
Glands typically smaller than benign glands; lined by a single uniform layer of cuboidal or low columnar epithelium
Malignant glands: tightly packed cells, lacking branching and papillary infoldings
Outer basal cell layer is absent (key diagnostic feature)
Cytoplasm ranges from pale-clear to distinctive amphophilic appearance
Nuclei enlarged, one or more large nucleoli; some nuclear size/shape variation but pleomorphism not marked
Mitotic figures uncommon

Local Extension

Most commonly involves periprostatic tissue, seminal vesicles, and base of urinary bladder → advanced disease may cause ureteral obstruction.

Metastases

Lymphatic: Obturator nodes → para-aortic nodes
Haematogenous: Chiefly to bones (axial skeleton); bony metastases are typically osteoblastic. Bones involved (descending order): lumbar spine, proximal femur, pelvis, thoracic spine, ribs

Clinical Features

Localised cancer: asymptomatic; discovered by suspicious nodule on DRE or ↑serum PSA
Advanced: urinary obstruction symptoms (difficulty starting/stopping stream, dysuria, frequency, haematuria)
Back pain from vertebral metastases (diagnosed by radionuclide bone scan)

Diagnosis

Digital rectal examination (DRE)
Transrectal ultrasound (TRUS)
Transrectal ultrasound-guided needle biopsy
Measurement of serum PSA levels

Treatment

Surgery (radical prostatectomy)
Radiation therapy
Androgen deprivation therapy: orchiectomy, LHRH agonists

<b>Grade (Gleason system) and stage are the most important prognostic factors.</b>

## 13 · Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA)
<b>🔴 What is PSA? Give its normal value, causes of raised PSA, and clinical importance.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is a product of prostatic epithelium, normally secreted in semen. An androgen-regulated <b>serine protease</b> whose function is to cleave and liquefy the seminal coagulum formed after ejaculation.

In normal men, only minute amounts circulate in the serum.

<b>Normal value: <4 ng/mL.</b>

Causes of raised PSA

PSA is organ-specific but NOT cancer-specific. Serum levels elevated in:

Carcinoma of prostate
Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)
Prostatitis
Prostate gland infarction
Instrumentation of the prostate
Ejaculation

Clinical importance of PSA

Most important test for diagnosis and management of prostate cancer
20–40% of patients with serum PSA >4.0 ng/mL will have prostate cancer on biopsy
High levels (>100 ng/mL) almost always indicate distant bone metastases
Most useful for monitoring treatment response and disease progression
Also a tumour marker of some breast cancers

🎯 <b>PSA = "Prostate Specific but NOT cancer Specific":</b> Elevated in Ca prostate, BPH, prostatitis, infarction, instrumentation, ejaculation. Useful for staging and follow-up.

<b>A 70-year-old male presents with difficulty in micturition, urgency, frequency. PR exam reveals palpable prostate. Likely diagnosis? Give the diagnostic workup.</b>

<b>Likely diagnosis: Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) / nodular hyperplasia of prostate.</b>

Diagnostic workup

Urine examination:
Routine — RBC, glucose, protein, pus cells
Culture & sensitivity (C/S) — infection
Cytology — if suspicious, to exclude malignancy
Blood examination:
Complete blood count (CBC)
Urea, creatinine, electrolytes
Blood sugar
PSA — to exclude carcinoma prostate
Ultrasound (USG) of whole abdomen
Uroflowmetry
Urodynamic study
Intravenous urography (IVU) — usually not required unless haematuria
Cystourethroscopy
Transrectal ultrasound scanning (TRUS)
FNAC — in suspected cases of carcinoma prostate

🎯 One-liners: Most common testicular tumour? Most common testicular tumour in elderly? Most common tumour of the prostate? GCT analogous to ovarian dysgerminoma?

Most common testicular tumour = <b>Seminoma</b> · most common in elderly (>60) = <b>Testicular lymphoma</b> · most common prostate tumour = <b>Adenocarcinoma</b> · analogous to dysgerminoma = <b>Seminoma</b> (identical tumour arises in ovary as dysgerminoma, in CNS as germinoma).

🎯 What are testicular tumour markers? Which tumour raises AFP? Which raises hCG?

Markers: <b>AFP (α-fetoprotein), hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin), LDH (lactate dehydrogenase)</b>. AFP ↑ in: <b>yolk sac tumour, embryonal carcinoma</b>. hCG ↑ in: <b>seminoma (low), embryonal carcinoma, choriocarcinoma (high)</b>.

🎯 Define PSA. Normal value? In which conditions is it raised?

PSA = Prostate-Specific Antigen, an androgen-regulated serine protease from prostatic epithelium. Normal: <b><4 ng/mL</b>. Raised in: <b>Ca prostate, BPH, prostatitis, prostatic infarction, instrumentation, ejaculation</b>. Organ-specific but NOT cancer-specific.

🎯 What is cryptorchidism? What are its complications? Treatment?

Cryptorchidism = failure of testicular descent into scrotum. Complications: <b>sterility, inguinal hernia (10–20%), testicular cancer</b>. Treatment: <b>surgical correction (orchiopexy)</b>.

🎯 What are the types of seminoma? Which is most common? What is anaplastic seminoma?

Types: <b>(1) Classic/typical (most common), (2) Spermatocytic, (3) Anaplastic</b>. Classic is the most common (~50% of all testicular GCTs).
