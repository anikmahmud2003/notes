🧠 Big Picture — Endocrine System Disorders (boss-level overview)

🔴 Thyroid Gland — Histology (follicles + parafollicular C-cells) → Classification of diseases → Thyroid function tests (TSH, FT3, FT4) → Hyperthyroidism (Graves, toxic adenoma, toxic MNG) vs Hypothyroidism (Hashimoto, iodine deficiency, cretinism/myxoedema) → Thyroiditis (Hashimoto, de Quervain, painless lymphocytic) → Goiter (simple/colloid, multinodular) → Thyroid carcinoma (papillary 80-85%, follicular 10-15%, medullary 5%, anaplastic <5%).

🔴 Endocrine Pancreas — Islets of Langerhans cell types (β → insulin; α → glucagon; δ → somatostatin; PP → pancreatic polypeptide; D1 → VIP; enterochromaffin → serotonin) → Diabetes mellitus (Type 1 = autoimmune β-cell destruction vs Type 2 = insulin resistance + β-cell dysfunction) → Complications: microvascular (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy) + macrovascular (MI, stroke, gangrene) → Pancreatitis (acute, chronic).

1 · Thyroid Histology & Function

Describe the histology of the thyroid gland. What are the cell types and their hormones?

The thyroid gland is divided by thin fibrous septa into lobules composed of follicles (about 20–40 per lobule), which are the main functional units. The follicles are lined by cuboidal to low columnar epithelium with numerous fine microvilli extending into the follicular colloid that contains the glycoprotein thyroglobulin (which contains the thyroid hormones within its molecule).

The thyroid gland follicles also contain a population of parafollicular cells (C cells), which synthesize and secrete the hormone calcitonin.

Cell TypeHormoneFunction
Follicular cellsT3 (triiodothyronine) & T4 (thyroxine)Regulate basal metabolic rate, growth, development
Parafollicular C cellsCalcitoninLowers blood calcium (inhibits osteoclasts)

Robbins 10e p.1075; Harsh Mohan 7e p.793

2 · Classification of Thyroid Diseases

🔴 Classify the diseases of the thyroid gland.

1. Congenital anomalies:

  • Thyroglossal duct / thyroglossal cyst
  • Lingual thyroid
  • Median ectopic thyroid
  • Lateral aberrant thyroid

2. Acquired diseases:

a. Functional disorders:

  • Hyperthyroidism (thyrotoxicosis)
  • Hypothyroidism — Cretinism (child with severe hypothyroidism), Myxoedema (adult-onset severe hypothyroidism)

b. Thyroiditis:

  • Hashimoto thyroiditis
  • Subacute lymphocytic (painless) thyroiditis
  • Granulomatous (de Quervain) thyroiditis

c. Graves' disease

d. Goiter — Diffuse nontoxic (simple) goiter, Multinodular goiter

e. Tumours:

  • Benign: Follicular adenoma
  • Malignant (Primary):
    • Papillary thyroid carcinoma (80–85%)
    • Follicular carcinoma (10–15%)
    • Anaplastic (undifferentiated) carcinoma (<5%)
    • Medullary carcinoma (5%)
    • Sarcoma, lymphoma (rarely)
  • Secondary: Metastatic, Local infiltration

Robbins 10e pp.1075–1092; Harsh Mohan 7e p.793

3 · Thyroid Function Tests

Enumerate the thyroid function tests. What are the normal values of FT3, FT4 & TSH?

Thyroid function tests (TFTs):

  1. Measurement of serum thyroid hormones:
    • TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone)
    • Total T3, T4
    • Free T3, T4
    • TRH (Thyrotropin-Releasing Hormone)
    • Thyroid binding globulin
  2. Iodine uptake scan (radioactive iodine uptake, RAIU)
  3. Thyroid scan — Cold nodule (non-functioning) vs Hot nodule (hyperactive)
  4. Ultrasonography (USG) of thyroid
  5. Thyroid antibodies
  6. FNAC (Fine Needle Aspiration Cytology)
  7. Biopsy and histopathology

Normal values

TestNormal Range
FT3 (Free Triiodothyronine)0.9–2.4 nmol/L (59–156 ng/dL)
FT4 (Free Thyroxine)9–21 pmol/L (700–1632 pg/dL)
TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone)0.2–4.5 mU/L
🎯 V.V.I. for VIVA: The measurement of serum TSH concentration is the most useful / most sensitive single (number one) screening test for hyper/hypo-thyroidism.

Robbins 10e p.1078; Davidson's 21e p.1295

Describe the hormonal changes in primary and secondary hypothyroidism.

Primary hypothyroidism (problem is in the thyroid gland)

  • FT3: Low
  • FT4: Low
  • TSH: Elevated (>20 mU/L) — the pituitary is trying to stimulate the failing thyroid

Secondary hypothyroidism (problem is in the pituitary/hypothalamus)

  • FT3: Low
  • FT4: Low
  • TSH: Low — the pituitary itself is failing, cannot produce TSH

Robbins 10e p.1078

4 · Hyperthyroidism (Thyrotoxicosis)

🔴 Define thyrotoxicosis and hyperthyroidism. Enumerate the causes of hyperthyroidism.

Thyrotoxicosis & hyperthyroidism: Hyperthyroidism, also called thyrotoxicosis, is a hypermetabolic clinical and biochemical state caused by excess production of thyroid hormones (T3 and T4). Because it is caused most commonly by hyperfunction of the thyroid gland, it is often referred to as hyperthyroidism.

📌 N.B.: Strictly speaking, hyperthyroidism is only one cause of thyrotoxicosis. In certain conditions the oversupply is from excessive release of preformed hormone (e.g. in thyroiditis) or an extrathyroidal source (e.g. Struma ovarii, exogenous thyroxine).

Causes of hyperthyroidism

Primary:

  1. Diffuse hyperplasia of the thyroid — Graves' disease (~85% of cases)
  2. Hyperfunctioning ("toxic") multinodular goiter
  3. Hyperfunctioning ("toxic") adenoma
  4. Thyroiditis (transient)
  5. Iodine-induced hyperthyroidism (Jod-Basedow phenomenon)
  6. Neonatal thyrotoxicosis associated with maternal Graves' disease

Secondary:

  1. TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone)-secreting pituitary adenoma (rare)

Robbins 10e pp.1075–1076

Define hypothyroidism. Enumerate its causes.

Hypothyroidism: A hypometabolic clinical state resulting from inadequate production of thyroid hormones for prolonged periods, or rarely, from resistance of the peripheral tissues to the effects of thyroid hormones.

Causes of hypothyroidism

Primary:

  1. Genetic defects in thyroid development (rare)
  2. Thyroid hormone resistance syndrome (rare)
  3. Postablative: Surgery, radioiodine therapy, or external radiation
  4. Autoimmune hypothyroidism: Hashimoto thyroiditis
  5. Iodine deficiency
  6. Drugs: Lithium, iodides, para-aminosalicylic acid
  7. Congenital biosynthetic defect (dyshormonogenetic goiter)

Secondary (central):

  1. Pituitary failure (rare)
  2. Hypothalamic failure (rare)

Robbins 10e p.1077

What are the clinical manifestations of hypothyroidism? Distinguish cretinism from myxoedema.

Clinical manifestations depend upon the age at onset and are divided into 2 forms:

  • Cretinism (congenital hypothyroidism): Severe hypothyroidism during infancy and childhood → growth retardation, mental retardation, delayed skeletal maturation, coarse facies, protuberant abdomen, umbilical hernia, hypotonia, constipation, dry skin, hypothermia.
  • Myxoedema (adult hypothyroidism): Adult-onset hypothyroidism → fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, dry skin, hair loss, bradycardia, periorbital oedema, menstrual irregularities, depression.
📌 The term myxoedema connotes non-pitting oedema due to accumulation of hydrophilic mucopolysaccharides in the ground substance of dermis and other tissues.

Harsh Mohan 7e p.794; Robbins 10e p.1077

5 · Hypothyroidism — Clinical Case

A middle-aged female has high TSH and low T3 & T4. What is your diagnosis? What may be the causes? (RMU-22Nv)

Diagnosis: Primary hypothyroidism — the elevated TSH with low FT3/FT4 confirms the thyroid gland itself is failing; the pituitary is producing maximal TSH in an attempt to stimulate it.

Possible causes: Hashimoto thyroiditis (most common in iodine-sufficient areas), iodine deficiency, post-thyroidectomy, post-radioiodine therapy, drugs (lithium, amiodarone), congenital biosynthetic defects.

🎯 True/False practice — Hypothyroidism
Primary causes of hypothyroidism: thyroid dysgenesis, pituitary failure, iodine deficiency, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, hypothalamic failure. (DU-08Ju)
Item Thyroid dysgenesis = True · Pituitary failure = False (secondary) · Iodine deficiency = True · Hashimoto's thyroiditis = True · Hypothalamic failure = False (tertiary) (ch18 MCQ)

6 · Hashimoto Thyroiditis

🔴 What are the inflammatory conditions of the thyroid gland? Describe Hashimoto thyroiditis (pathogenesis, morphology, clinical features).

Inflammatory conditions of the thyroid gland (thyroiditis):

  • Hashimoto thyroiditis — autoimmune, most common cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient areas
  • Granulomatous (de Quervain's) thyroiditis — painful, probably secondary to viral infection
  • Subacute lymphocytic (painless) thyroiditis — postpartum thyroiditis

Hashimoto Thyroiditis — Pathogenesis

It is an autoimmune disease that results in destruction of the thyroid gland and gradual progressive thyroid failure. Breakdown in self-tolerance to thyroid auto-antigens results in progressive autoimmune destruction by:

  1. CD8+ cytotoxic T cell-mediated cell death — CD8+ cytotoxic T cells cause thyrocyte destruction
  2. Cytokine-mediated cell death — Excessive T-cell activation → production of Th1 inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IFN-γ) → recruitment/activation of macrophages → damage to follicles
  3. Antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) — Binding of anti-thyroid antibodies (anti-thyroglobulin and anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies) followed by ADCC

Morphology

Grossly:

  • Thyroid gland is usually diffusely enlarged
  • Capsule is intact, gland well demarcated from adjacent structures
  • Cut surface is pale, yellow-tan, firm, and somewhat nodular

Microscopically:

  • Extensive infiltration by a mononuclear inflammatory infiltrate containing small lymphocytes, plasma cells, and well-developed germinal centres
  • Thyroid follicles are atrophic and lined in many areas by epithelial cells with abundant eosinophilic, granular cytoplasm — Hürthle cells
  • Presence of Hürthle cells + heterogeneous population of lymphocytes = characteristic of Hashimoto thyroiditis

Epidemiology: Most common cause of hypothyroidism where iodine levels are sufficient. Most prevalent between 45 and 65 years; F:M = 10–20:1.

Robbins 10e p.1078

7 · Graves' Disease

🔴 Short note: Graves' disease (pathogenesis, morphology, clinical features, lab findings, treatment).

Graves' disease: An autoimmune thyroid disorder and the most common cause of endogenous hyperthyroidism. Characterised by a clinical triad:

  1. Hyperthyroidism with diffuse enlargement of the thyroid gland
  2. Infiltrative ophthalmopathy with resultant exophthalmos
  3. Localised infiltrative dermopathy (pretibial myxoedema)

Age/Sex: Peak incidence 20–40 years; women affected ~10× more frequently than men.

Pathogenesis

Autoimmune disease caused by production of multiple autoantibodies against the TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone) receptor:

  • TSI (Thyroid-Stimulating Immunoglobulin) — stimulates increased release of thyroid hormone
  • TGI (Thyroid Growth Stimulating Immunoglobulins) — stimulates proliferation of follicular epithelium
  • TBII (TSH-Binding Inhibitor Immunoglobulins) — inhibits binding of TSH to its own receptor; depending on whether it is inhibitory or stimulatory, may result in alternate episodes of hypo- and hyperthyroidism

Morphology

Grossly:

  • Symmetrical enlargement of thyroid gland due to diffuse hypertrophy and hyperplasia
  • Weight increases to over 80 gm
  • Cut section: soft, meaty appearance resembling normal muscle

Microscopically:

  • Follicles are small and lined by tall columnar epithelium, more crowded than usual
  • Considerable epithelial hyperplasia and hypertrophy with formation of papillary infoldings into follicular lumina
  • Colloid is markedly diminished
  • Stroma shows increased vascularity and accumulation of lymphoid cells; germinal centres are common

Clinical Features

  1. Diffuse enlargement of the thyroid (diffuse goiter) with features of thyrotoxicosis
  2. Ophthalmopathy (exophthalmos)
  3. Dermopathy (pretibial myxoedema — scaly thickening over shins)

Laboratory Findings

TestResult
Serum TSH level↓ Decreased
Serum free T3 and T4↑ Increased
Radioactive iodine uptake (RAIU)Very high
Thyroid scanningDiffuse uptake of iodine throughout the gland
Thyroid auto-antibodyTSH receptor antibody / thyroid receptor antibody (TRAb) positive

Treatment

  • Anti-thyroid drugs (carbimazole or propylthiouracil)
  • β-blocker (propranolol) — reduces sympathetic symptoms (tremor, tachycardia, palpitation, sweating, anxiety)
  • Radioiodine therapy
  • Surgery (subtotal thyroidectomy)

Nice to Know — Mechanism of Graves' Ophthalmopathy

Protrusion of the eyeball (exophthalmos) is caused by increase in volume of retro-orbital connective tissues and extraocular muscles due to:

  1. Marked infiltration by mononuclear cells (predominantly T cells)
  2. Inflammation, oedema, and swelling of extraocular muscles
  3. Accumulation of extracellular matrix components, specifically hydrophilic glycosaminoglycans (hyaluronic acid and chondroitin sulphate)
  4. Increased numbers of adipocytes (fatty infiltration)

Robbins 10e p.1081; Harsh Mohan 7e p.797

A 30-year-old lady presents with diffuse thyroid enlargement + exophthalmos. What is your diagnosis? What is the status of thyroid hormones? (DU-13Ju, DU-12Ja)

Clinical diagnosis: Graves' disease

Thyroid hormone levels:

  • Plasma TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone): ↓ Low (suppressed)
  • Plasma FT3 (Free Triiodothyronine): ↑ High
  • Plasma FT4 (Free Thyroxine): ↑ High

Immunological mechanism: Circulating antibodies against the TSH receptor activate the receptor, making the thyroid gland hyperactive.

8 · Goiter (Simple & Multinodular)

🔴 What is goiter? Classify goiter. What are its causes?

Goiter: Any enlargement of the thyroid gland, caused by compensatory hyperplasia and hypertrophy of the follicular epithelium in response to thyroid hormone deficiency.

Pathogenesis: Impairment of thyroid hormone synthesis → compensatory rise in serum TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone) → hypertrophy and hyperplasia of thyroid follicular cells → gross enlargement of the thyroid gland.

Types of goiter

  1. Diffuse non-toxic (simple) goiter
    • Endemic goiter (iodine-deficient geographic areas)
    • Non-endemic / Sporadic goiter
  2. Multinodular goiter
    • Toxic multinodular goiter
    • Non-toxic multinodular goiter

Causes of goiter

  1. Physiological: Puberty, Pregnancy
  2. Autoimmune disorder: Graves' disease (overactive goiter), Hashimoto thyroiditis (underactive goiter)
  3. Iodine deficiency (endemic) — geographic areas where diet is deficient in iodine
  4. Goitrogens — foods or drugs that suppress synthesis of thyroid hormones (cruciferous vegetables, lithium, amiodarone)
  5. Tumours (rare) — benign or malignant
  6. Congenital — maternal anti-thyroid drugs, maternal hyperthyroidism, dyshormonogenesis

Robbins 10e p.1082; BRS Pathology 5e p.322

What are the causes of hyperfunctional and hypofunctional goiter? (CMU-24M)

Hyperfunctional goiter (overactive thyroid — hyperthyroidism)

  1. Graves' disease — antibodies (TSI - thyroid-stimulating immunoglobulins) overstimulate the thyroid
  2. Toxic multinodular goiter (Plummer's disease) — multiple nodules become hyperactive
  3. Toxic adenoma — single benign tumour autonomously produces excess hormone
  4. Thyroiditis (subacute or silent) — transient hyperthyroidism
  5. Excess iodine intake (Jod-Basedow phenomenon)

Hypofunctional goiter (underactive thyroid — hypothyroidism)

  1. Iodine deficiency — most common cause worldwide
  2. Hashimoto's thyroiditis — autoimmune destruction
  3. Congenital hypothyroidism — thyroid dysgenesis or enzyme defects
  4. Goitrogenic foods or drugs — cruciferous vegetables, soy, lithium, amiodarone
  5. Post-treatment hypothyroidism — after radioiodine, surgery, or anti-thyroid drugs
Short note: Diffuse non-toxic (simple/colloid) goiter. Describe its pathogenesis.

Simple goiter (colloid goiter): A form of goiter that causes enlargement of the entire gland without producing nodularity. Because the enlarged follicles are filled with colloid, the term colloid goiter has been applied.

Pathogenesis

Lack of iodine (dietary iodine deficiency) → ↓ Synthesis of thyroid hormones → compensatory rise in serum TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone) → hypertrophy and hyperplasia of thyroid follicular cells → gross enlargement of the thyroid gland.

Types and causes

a. Endemic goiter: Goiter occurring with high frequency in iodine-deficient geographic areas (mountainous regions where soil, water, food supply contain only low levels of iodine).

b. Sporadic goiter: Occurring in non-iodine-deficient areas:

  • Striking female preponderance; peak incidence at puberty or young adult life
  • Ingestion of goitrogenic substances (excessive calcium, Brassicaceae family — cabbage, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, turnips, cassava)
  • Hereditary enzymatic defects interfering with thyroid hormone synthesis
  • In most cases, the cause is not apparent

Robbins 10e p.1083; BRS Pathology 5e p.323

🔴 Short note: Multinodular goiter (pathogenesis, morphology).

Multinodular goiter: Irregular enlargement of the thyroid resulting in nodule formation. Virtually all long-standing simple goiters convert into multinodular goiters. Occurs in both endemic and sporadic forms; more common in females; affects older individuals (late complication).

Pathogenesis

Dietary iodine (I) deficiency → ↓ T4 & T3 production → ↓ blood T4 & T3 → ↑ TSH secretion from anterior pituitary → persistent TSH stimulation of thyroid gland → uneven follicular hyperplasia → generation of new follicles and uneven accumulation of colloid → distension and stress causing rupture of follicles and vessels → haemorrhage, cystic change, scarring, sometimes calcification → scarring adds tension → in this cyclic manner nodularity appears.

Morphology

Grossly:

  • Multilobulated, asymmetrically enlarged glands — can weigh over 2000 gm
  • Enlargement may involve one lobe far more than the other, producing lateral pressure on trachea and oesophagus
  • May grow behind sternum and clavicles → intrathoracic or plunging goiter
  • Cut section: irregular nodules containing variable amounts of brown, gelatinous colloid; older lesions have areas of haemorrhage, fibrosis, calcification, and cystic change

Histologically:

  • Colloid-rich follicles lined by flattened, inactive epithelium
  • Areas of follicular hyperplasia, accompanied by degenerative changes
📌 Non-toxic goiter: Goiter without thyroid hormone dysfunction. Toxic goiter: Goiter associated with hyperthyroidism. If patient is euthyroid or hypothyroid → "non-toxic" is appropriate.

Robbins 10e p.1083

9 · Thyroid Carcinoma

🔴 Enumerate the pathogenesis and premalignant tumours of thyroid cancer. What is the role of genetic factors?

Pathogenesis of thyroid cancer

1. Genetic factors — distinct genetic events in each histologic variant:

  • Papillary carcinomas: Activation of the MAP kinase pathway by rearrangement of tyrosine kinase receptors RET or NTRK (neurotrophic tyrosine kinase receptor), and point mutations in BRAF
  • Follicular carcinomas: Mutations in the RAS family of oncogenes
  • Anaplastic (undifferentiated) carcinomas: Inactivating point mutations in the p53 tumour suppressor genes
  • Medullary thyroid carcinomas: MEN-2 (Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia type 2) and RET proto-oncogene mutations

2. Environmental factors: Exposure to ionizing radiation, particularly during the first 2 decades of life.

Premalignant tumours/lesions of the thyroid gland

  • Solitary thyroid nodule (cold nodule)
  • Thyroid cyst
  • Follicular adenoma
  • Hashimoto's thyroiditis
  • Carcinoid tumour of thyroid
  • C-cell hyperplasia

Robbins 10e pp.1084–1085

🔴 Short note: Papillary carcinoma of thyroid (morphology, clinical features, prognosis).

Papillary carcinoma of thyroid: Most common form of thyroid cancer (80–85%). Occur throughout life but peak between 25 and 50 years. Found in ~3× more frequently in females than males. Account for majority of thyroid carcinomas associated with previous exposure to ionizing radiation.

Morphology

Grossly:

  • Solitary or multifocal lesions within the thyroid
  • Some well circumscribed/encapsulated; others infiltrate adjacent parenchyma with ill-defined margins
  • May contain fibrosis, calcification; often cystic
  • Cut surface sometimes reveals papillary foci

Microscopically (PATHOGNOMONIC features):

  • Branching papillae having a fibrovascular stalk covered by single to multiple layers of cuboidal epithelial cells
  • Nuclei with optically clear or empty appearance — "Ground-glass" or "Orphan Annie eye" nuclei
  • Psammoma bodies — concentrically calcified structures, usually within the cores of the papillae
📌 N.B.: The diagnosis of papillary carcinoma can be made based on these nuclear features alone, even without papillary architecture!

Clinical Features

  • Asymptomatic thyroid nodule
  • Cervical lymphadenopathy (regional lymph node metastasis — common)
  • Nodule moves freely during swallowing
  • Hoarseness, dysphagia, cough, or dyspnoea → advanced disease
  • Distant metastasis is rare (lung if haematogenous)

Investigations: Radionuclide scanning; neck ultrasound with FNAC; scintiscans.

Prognosis: Excellent — 10-year survival rate in excess of 95%.

Robbins 10e p.1088; Harsh Mohan 7e p.802

Short note: Follicular carcinoma of thyroid (morphology, metastasis, prognosis).

Follicular carcinoma: Second most common thyroid cancer (10–15%). Common in women (F:M = 3:1); older age than papillary, peak incidence 40–60 years.

Morphology

Grossly:

  • Single nodules — may be well-circumscribed or widely infiltrative
  • Larger lesions may penetrate the capsule and infiltrate adjacent neck structures
  • Gray to tan to pink on cut section; central fibrosis and calcification sometimes present

Microscopically:

  • Fairly uniform cells forming follicles of various sizes containing colloid
  • Occasional tumours dominated by Hürthle cells (cells with abundant granular, eosinophilic cytoplasm)
  • Vascular invasion and capsular invasion are significant features; lymphatic invasion is rare
📌 The tumour differs from papillary carcinoma in lacking: papillae, ground-glass nuclei, and psammoma bodies.

Metastasis: Regional lymph node metastases are rare but distant metastases by haematogenous route are common, especially to bones, lungs, and liver.

Prognosis: Largely dependent on extent of invasion and stage at presentation. 10-year survival: 50–70%.

Treatment: Total thyroidectomy → radioactive iodine. Thyroid hormone given post-surgery to suppress endogenous TSH.

Robbins 10e p.1089; Harsh Mohan 7e p.803

What is the prognosis of thyroid cancer? (CU-08Ja)

Prognosis largely depends on age, extent of invasion, and stage at presentation (presence/absence of distant metastasis).

  • Papillary carcinoma: Excellent — 10-year survival >95%. 5–20% have local/regional recurrence; 10–15% have distant metastases.
  • Follicular carcinoma: 10-year survival 50–70%.
  • Medullary carcinoma: Excellent if no metastases. Late metastases via lymphatics and blood. 10-year survival 60–70%.
  • Anaplastic carcinoma: Almost uniformly fatal. No effective therapy. Death in <1 year in most cases due to aggressive growth and compromise of vital neck structures. 10-year survival 5–10%; median survival ~2 months.

Robbins 10e pp.1086–1092; Harsh Mohan 7e p.802

A 25-year-old lady has a solitary nodule in the right lobe of thyroid with right-sided cervical lymphadenopathy for 3 months. (BUP-21Nv/M) (a) Probable diagnosis? (b) Investigations?

(a) Probable diagnosis: Carcinoma thyroid (likely papillary — most common, common cervical lymph node metastasis, young female).

(b) Investigations:

  1. Serum TSH, FT3 & FT4
  2. Thyroid scan
  3. USG of thyroid including the neck
  4. FNAC or biopsy from the thyroid nodule
  5. Lobectomy followed by histopathology

10 · Endocrine Pancreas

Describe the cell types of the endocrine pancreas and their hormones. What are the diseases of the endocrine pancreas?

The endocrine pancreas consists of about 1 million clusters of cells — the Islets of Langerhans, which contain four major and two minor cell types:

Cell TypeHormoneFunction
β (Beta) cellsInsulinRegulates glucose utilisation; reduces blood glucose levels
α (Alpha) cellsGlucagonStimulates glycogenolysis in liver; increases blood sugar
δ (Delta) cellsSomatostatinSuppresses both insulin and glucagon release
PP cellsPancreatic PolypeptideStimulates gastric/intestinal enzyme secretion; inhibits intestinal motility
D1 cells (rare)VIP (Vasoactive Intestinal Polypeptide)Induces glycogenolysis, hyperglycaemia; stimulates GI fluid secretion; causes secretory diarrhoea
Enterochromaffin cells (rare)SerotoninSource of pancreatic tumours causing carcinoid syndrome

Diseases of the endocrine pancreas

A. Diabetes mellitus (DM)

B. Islet cell tumours / Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (PanNETs):

  • β-cell tumours (insulinomas)
  • Zollinger-Ellison syndrome (gastrinoma)
  • MEN (Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia)

C. Other rare pancreatic endocrine neoplasms:

  • α-cell tumours (glucagonomas)
  • δ-cell tumours (somatostatinomas)
  • VIPoma
  • Pancreatic carcinoid tumours
  • Pancreatic polypeptide-secreting endocrine tumours
  • Multi-hormonal tumours — produce insulin, glucagon, gastrin, ACTH (Adrenocorticotropic Hormone), MSH (Melanocyte Stimulating Hormone), ADH (Antidiuretic Hormone), serotonin, norepinephrine, etc.

Robbins 10e pp.1097–1115

11 · Pancreatitis

Define acute pancreatitis. What are its causes and clinical features? What are its sequelae?

Acute pancreatitis: Reversible pancreatic parenchymal injury associated with inflammation.

Causes

Common:

  • Biliary tract disease (gallstones)
  • Alcoholism

Less common:

  1. Obstruction of the pancreatic duct system: Periampullary neoplasms, pancreas divisum, choledochoceles, biliary sludge, parasites (Ascaris lumbricoides, Clonorchis sinensis)
  2. Medications: Furosemide, azathioprine, oestrogens
  3. Infections: Mumps
  4. Metabolic disorders: Hypertriglyceridaemia, hyperparathyroidism, hypercalcaemic states
  5. Ischaemic injury: Shock, vascular thrombosis, embolism, vasculitis
  6. Trauma: Blunt abdominal trauma, iatrogenic (surgery or ERCP — Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangio-Pancreatography)
  7. Inherited alterations in genes encoding pancreatic enzymes and their inhibitors

Clinical Features

  • Constant, intense abdominal pain — referred to upper back and left shoulder
  • Anorexia, nausea, vomiting
  • Full-blown acute pancreatitis: Leukocytosis, haemolysis, DIC (Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation), fluid sequestration, ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome), peripheral vascular collapse and shock, acute tubular necrosis

Sequelae of acute pancreatitis

  1. Systemic organ failure — shock, ARDS, acute renal failure, DIC
  2. Pancreatic abscess
  3. Pancreatic pseudocyst
  4. Duodenal obstruction

Robbins 8e pp.893, 895–896

What is chronic pancreatitis? Mention its causes and sequelae.

Chronic pancreatitis: Inflammation of the pancreas with irreversible destruction of exocrine parenchyma, fibrosis, and in late stages, destruction of the endocrine parenchyma.

Causes

  1. Long-term alcohol abuse (most common cause)
  2. Long-standing obstruction of the pancreatic duct by: pseudocysts, calculi, trauma, neoplasms, pancreas divisum
  3. Tropical pancreatitis
  4. Hereditary pancreatitis

Sequelae

  1. Pseudocyst
  2. Duct obstruction
  3. Malabsorption, steatorrhoea
  4. Secondary diabetes

Robbins 8e pp.896, 898

12 · Diabetes Mellitus

🔴 Define and classify diabetes mellitus. What are the different types? (DU-17Nv, RU-20/18Nv, CU-05Ja)

Diabetes mellitus (DM): A clinical syndrome characterised by an increase in plasma blood glucose (hyperglycaemia) due to absolute or relative deficiency of insulin. It is a group of metabolic disorders sharing the common feature of hyperglycaemia caused by defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or most commonly both.

Classification of DM

A. Type 1 diabetes (T1D) — β-cell destruction, usually leading to absolute insulin deficiency

  • Immune-mediated
  • Idiopathic (autoantibody-negative)

B. Type 2 diabetes (T2D) — combination of insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction

C. Other types:

  1. Genetic defects of β-cell function: MODY (Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young), neonatal diabetes, MIDD (Maternally Inherited Diabetes and Deafness), defects in proinsulin conversion, insulin gene mutations
  2. Genetic defects in insulin action: Type A insulin resistance, lipoatrophic diabetes
  3. Exocrine pancreatic defects ("pancreatogenic" / Type 3C diabetes): Pancreatitis, pancreatectomy/trauma, pancreatic cancer, cystic fibrosis, haemochromatosis, fibrocalculous pancreatopathy
  4. Endocrinopathies: Acromegaly, Cushing syndrome, hyperthyroidism, pheochromocytoma, glucagonoma
  5. Infections: Cytomegalovirus, Coxsackie B virus, congenital rubella
  6. Drugs: Glucocorticoids, thyroid hormone, interferon-α, protease inhibitors, β-adrenergic agonists, thiazides, nicotinic acid, phenytoin, Vacor
  7. Genetic syndromes: Down syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome, Turner syndrome, Prader-Willi syndrome
  8. Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM)

Robbins 10e p.1099; Davidson's 23e p.733

🔴 Short note: Type 1 diabetes mellitus (pathogenesis, genetics, environmental factors).

Type 1 diabetes / IDDM (Insulin-Dependent DM) / Juvenile-onset DM: An autoimmune disease characterised by pancreatic β-cell destruction and absolute deficiency of insulin. Accounts for ~5–10% of diabetes; most common subtype in patients <20 years.

Pathogenesis

Generally considered a T-cell-mediated autoimmune disease involving destruction of insulin-secreting β cells in the pancreatic islets. Both genetic susceptibility and environmental factors play important roles.

Autoimmunity — mechanisms of β-cell destruction:

  1. Insulitis: Infiltration of islets with mononuclear cells containing activated macrophages, CD4+ T helper cells, CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, NK cells, and B lymphocytes
  2. Cytokine-mediated damage: IFN-γ (from T cells), TNF and IL-1 (from macrophages) → direct β-cell damage
  3. Autoantibodies: Against islet cells and insulin produced in 70–80% of patients (antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity)

Genetic susceptibility: HLA-DR3 and HLA-DR4 (MHC Class II genes) are associated with increased susceptibility.

Environmental factors: Viruses — Coxsackieviruses group B, mumps, measles, cytomegalovirus, rubella, infectious mononucleosis.

Robbins 10e pp.1099, 1101; Davidson's 23e p.728

🔴 Short note: Type 2 diabetes mellitus (etiopathogenesis, pathogenesis).

Type 2 diabetes / NIDDM (Non-Insulin-Dependent DM) / Adult-onset DM: Caused by a combination of peripheral resistance to insulin action and an inadequate secretory response by the pancreatic β cells ("relative insulin deficiency"). Accounts for ~90–95% of diabetic patients; vast majority are overweight.

Etiopathogenesis

  1. Genetic factors
  2. Obesity
  3. Sedentary lifestyle
  4. Dietary habits (overeating, especially with obesity and under-activity)
  5. Age — middle-aged and elderly (usually >40 years)

Pathogenesis — two key abnormalities

  1. Insulin resistance: Decreased response of peripheral tissues (especially skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, liver) to insulin
  2. β-cell dysfunction: Inadequate insulin secretion in the face of insulin resistance and hyperglycaemia
📌 The combination of obesity + hyperglycaemia + increased serum cholesterol/triglycerides + hypertension = Metabolic Syndrome.

Robbins 10e p.1103; Davidson's 23e p.730

🔴 Mention the clinical features and complications of diabetes mellitus. (DU-21Ja, 20Nv/M, RMU-21Nv)

Classic triad of diabetes

  1. Polyuria
  2. Polydipsia
  3. Polyphagia

Other clinical features: Ketoacidosis, dehydration, muscle wasting, weakness, weight loss.

📌 Diabetic ketoacidosis manifestations: Fatigue, nausea/vomiting, severe abdominal pain, characteristic fruity odour, deep laboured breathing (Kussmaul breathing).

Complications of diabetes mellitus

Acute complications:

  1. Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)
  2. Hypoglycaemic coma
  3. Hyperglycaemic hyperosmolar state (HHS)
  4. Lactic acidosis

Chronic complications — Microvascular / neuropathic:

  1. Diabetic retinopathy → impaired vision
  2. Diabetic nephropathy → renal failure
  3. Diabetic neuropathy → sensory loss, motor weakness, pain
  4. Autonomic neuropathy → gastroparesis, altered bowel habit, postural hypotension
  5. Diabetic foot disease → ulceration, arthropathy

Chronic complications — Macrovascular:

  1. Coronary circulation: Myocardial ischaemia / infarction
  2. Cerebral circulation: Transient ischaemic attack, stroke
  3. Peripheral circulation: Claudication, ischaemia (e.g. gangrene of lower extremities)

Davidson's 23e pp.734, 756; Robbins 10e pp.1106–1107

Mention the causes of death in diabetes mellitus. What are the effects of DM on the kidney?

Causes of death in diabetes mellitus

  1. Myocardial infarction (most common cause of death in diabetes)
  2. Renal failure
  3. Cerebrovascular accidents (stroke)

Effects of diabetes mellitus on the kidney

The kidneys are prime targets of diabetes. Renal failure is second only to MI as a cause of death from this disease. Three lesions are encountered:

  1. Glomerular lesions:
    • Capillary basement membrane thickening
    • Diffuse mesangial sclerosis
    • Nodular glomerulosclerosis (Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules)
  2. Renal vascular lesions — principally atherosclerosis
  3. Pyelonephritis, including necrotising papillitis

Robbins 10e pp.1109–1110

Thyroid dysgenesis = True · pituitary failure = False (that's secondary/central) · iodine deficiency = True · Hashimoto's = True · hypothalamic failure = False (that's secondary).

Decreased insulin = True · 100% twin concordance = False (~30–50%) · MHC/HLA linkage = True · late insulitis = False (early insulitis) · no anti-islet antibodies = False (present in 70–80%).
🎯 Most sensitive single screening test for thyroid disorders?
Serum TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone) — elevated in primary hypothyroidism, suppressed in hyperthyroidism.
🎯 Pathognomonic microscopic features of papillary thyroid carcinoma?
Ground-glass / Orphan Annie eye nuclei + Psammoma bodies + Branching papillae. Diagnosis can be made on nuclear features alone even without papillary architecture.
🎯 Pathognomonic feature of follicular carcinoma of thyroid vs papillary?
Vascular and capsular invasion (follicular) — lacking papillae, ground-glass nuclei, and psammoma bodies (which are papillary features).
🎯 Hürthle cells are seen in which two thyroid conditions?
Hashimoto thyroiditis (characteristic) and Follicular carcinoma (occasional tumours dominated by Hürthle cells with abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm).
🎯 Genetic alteration in each type of thyroid carcinoma?
Papillary = RET/NTRK/BRAF · Follicular = RAS · Medullary = RET (MEN-2) · Anaplastic = p53 loss.
🎯 Most common cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient areas? Most common cause of hyperthyroidism?
Hypothyroidism = Hashimoto thyroiditis · Hyperthyroidism = Graves' disease (~85%).
🎯 Graves' disease triad?
Hyperthyroidism + Diffuse goiter → Exophthalmos → Pretibial myxoedema (dermopathy).
🎯 Autoantibodies in Graves' disease?
TSI (Thyroid-Stimulating Immunoglobulin), TGI (Thyroid Growth Immunoglobulin), TBII (TSH-Binding Inhibitor Immunoglobulin) — all directed against the TSH receptor.
🎯 HLA association in Type 1 DM? Most common cause of death in DM?
Type 1 DM = HLA-DR3 / HLA-DR4. Most common cause of death = Myocardial infarction.
🎯 Insulitis = ? What type of DM?
Infiltration of pancreatic islets with mononuclear cells (activated macrophages, CD4+, CD8+, NK cells, B lymphocytes) — hallmark of Type 1 DM pathogenesis.
🎯 Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules = ?
Nodular glomerulosclerosis — pathognomonic renal lesion of diabetes mellitus.
🎯 Kussmaul breathing = ?
Deep, laboured breathing — seen in diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).
🎯 Metabolic syndrome = combination of?
Obesity + Hyperglycaemia + Increased serum cholesterol/triglycerides + Hypertension (associated with Type 2 DM).
🎯 10-year survival rates of thyroid carcinomas?
Papillary = 80–95% · Follicular = 50–70% · Medullary = 60–70% · Anaplastic = 5–10% (median survival ~2 months).
🎯 Difference between thyrotoxicosis and hyperthyroidism?
Hyperthyroidism = excessive hormone production due to gland hyperfunction. Thyrotoxicosis = broader term; includes hyperthyroidism plus excess from non-gland sources (thyroiditis, Struma ovarii, exogenous thyroxine).
🎯 What is goitrogenic substance? Give examples.
Substances that suppress thyroid hormone synthesis → cruciferous vegetables (cabbage, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, turnips, cassava), lithium, amiodarone.
🎯 Jod-Basedow phenomenon = ?
Iodine-induced hyperthyroidism — occurs in individuals with underlying thyroid disorders when exposed to excess iodine.
🎯 Plummer's disease = ?
Toxic multinodular goiter — multiple thyroid nodules become hyperactive, producing excessive thyroid hormones.
