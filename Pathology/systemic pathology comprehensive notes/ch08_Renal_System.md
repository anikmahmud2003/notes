# Ch 08 — Renal System     <i>Full-detail flashcards (Q→A) · All abbreviations expanded · Robbins 4-pillar answers</i>
### 🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of the Kidney & Urinary Tract
The kidney's core job is <b>filtration → reabsorption → secretion → excretion</b>. Every renal disease ultimately disrupts this pipeline. Organize it by location:
🔴 <b>Glomerular diseases</b> — the big two syndromes: <b>Nephritic syndrome</b> (haematuria, hypertension, oliguria, mild proteinuria) vs <b>Nephrotic syndrome</b> (massive proteinuria ≥3.5 g/day, hypoalbuminaemia, oedema, hyperlipidaemia, lipiduria). Primary glomerulopathies: Minimal Change Disease (MCD), Membranous Nephropathy, Membranoproliferative GN (MPGN), Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), IgA Nephropathy, Rapidly Progressive (Crescentic) GN (RPGN). Secondary: SLE nephritis, Diabetic Nephropathy, Goodpasture, vasculitis-associated. Hereditary: Alport syndrome, Fabry disease.
📌 <b>Tubulo-interstitial diseases</b> — Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN), Pyelonephritis (acute + chronic), Drug-induced nephropathy.
📌 <b>Vascular diseases</b> — Nephrosclerosis, Renal Artery Stenosis, Thrombotic Microangiopathies (TTP/HUS).
🎯 <b>Cystic diseases</b> — ADPKD (Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease), ARPKD (Autosomal Recessive PKD), Simple cysts.
🎯 <b>Tumours</b> — Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC, clear-cell most common), Wilms Tumour (nephroblastoma, most common paediatric renal tumour), Urothelial carcinoma, Angiomyolipoma, Oncocytoma.
📌 <b>Obstructive uropathy & stones</b> — Calcium oxalate (70%), Struvite/staghorn (infection), Uric acid, Cystine. Bladder: Transitional cell carcinoma (risk: smoking, aryl amines, <i>Schistosoma haematobium</i>).
Robbins 10e, Ch 18 (Kidney), pp. 895–962 · Arif 15e (2025) Vol-2, pp.225–249

## 1 · Renal Histology & Filtration Barrier
<b>Describe the structure of a nephron and the three components of the glomerular filtration barrier.</b>

The parenchyma of each kidney contains approximately <b>1 million nephrons</b>. A nephron has 5 major parts: (1) Glomerular capsule (glomerulus + Bowman's capsule), (2) Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT), (3) Loop of Henle, (4) Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT), (5) Collecting ducts.

Glomerular Filtration Barrier — 3 components (inside → out):

Fenestrated endothelial cells lining the capillary loops
Glomerular Basement Membrane (GBM) — endothelial cells rest on this
Filtration slit pores between the foot processes (pedicels) of visceral epithelial cells (podocytes) external to the GBM

The glomerulus performs complex filtration from capillaries into the urinary space.

Robbins 10e pp.895–896 · Arif pp.225–226

## 2 · Classification of Kidney Diseases
<b>🔴 Name and classify the diseases of the kidney.</b>

<b>Comprehensive classification:</b>

Congenital & developmental anomalies — Agenesis, Hypoplasia, Ectopic kidney, Horseshoe kidney, Polycystic kidney
Glomerular diseases — Nephritic syndrome, Nephrotic syndrome, Hereditary (Fabry disease, Alport syndrome), Systemic (SLE nephritis, Henoch-Schönlein Purpura, Diabetic nephropathy, Amyloidosis)
Tubular & interstitial diseases — Acute Tubular Injury/Necrosis (ATI/ATN), Tubulointerstitial nephritis, Pyelonephritis & UTI, Drug-induced nephropathy, Others (urate nephropathy, hypercalcaemia & nephrocalcinosis, light-chain cast nephropathy/"myeloma kidney")
Vascular diseases — Nephrosclerosis, Renal artery stenosis, Thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP, HUS), Atherosclerotic ischaemic renal disease, Atheroembolic renal disease, Sickle cell nephropathy, Renal infarcts
Cystic diseases — Simple cyst, Acquired cystic disease, Polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD, ARPKD), Cystic diseases of renal medulla (Medullary sponge kidney, Nephronophthisis), Multicystic renal dysplasia
Urinary tract obstruction (obstructive uropathy)
Urolithiasis (renal calculi/stones)
Tumours — Benign (papillary adenoma, angiomyolipoma, oncocytoma, mesoblastic nephroma) · Malignant — Primary: RCC (adenocarcinoma), Urothelial carcinoma of renal pelvis, Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma) · Secondary: Blood-borne metastases (lung, breast, stomach)

## 4 · Clinical Manifestations of Renal Diseases
<b>🔴 What are the clinical manifestations of renal diseases?</b>

<b>11 major clinical presentations:</b>

Azotaemia — elevation of Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine levels (biochemical abnormality)
Uraemia — clinical signs & symptoms of azotaemia with biochemical abnormalities
Nephritic syndrome
Nephrotic syndrome
Asymptomatic haematuria or proteinuria
Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) (formerly acute renal failure)
Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) (formerly chronic renal failure)
End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) — GFR <5% of normal
Renal tubular defects — polyuria, nocturia, electrolyte disorders (e.g. metabolic acidosis)
Urinary tract obstruction/infection — bacteriuria + pyuria; pyelonephritis or cystitis
Nephrolithiasis (renal stones) — renal colic, haematuria

🎯 <b>Azotaemia</b> = biochemical (↑BUN + creatinine) alone; <b>Uraemia</b> = biochemical + clinical symptoms.

Robbins 10e p.896 · Arif pp.227–228

## 5 · Classification of Glomerular Diseases
<b>🔴 Classify glomerular diseases into primary, secondary, and hereditary. List the key terminologies.</b>

A) Primary Glomerular Diseases

Minimal-Change Disease (lipoid nephrosis) · Membranous Glomerulonephritis · Membranoproliferative GN (MPGN) · Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) · IgA Nephropathy (Berger disease) · Chronic GN · Acute GN · Rapidly Progressive (Crescentic) GN (RPGN)

🎯 Primary = "3 M-F-I-C-A" → <b>M</b>CD, <b>M</b>embranous, <b>M</b>PGN, <b>F</b>SGS, <b>I</b>gA nephropathy, <b>C</b>hronic GN, <b>A</b>cute GN/RPGN. Or mnemonic: "3M Formula-1 CAR" → Minimal change, Membranous, MPGN, FSGS, IgA, RPGN/Crescentic.

B) Secondary Glomerular Diseases

SLE (Lupus Nephritis) · Diabetic Nephropathy · Amyloidosis · Goodpasture Syndrome · Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN) · Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA, formerly Wegener) · Microscopic Polyangiitis · Henoch-Schönlein Purpura (HSP) · Cryoglobulinaemia · Systemic Infectious Diseases (Bacterial: endocarditis, syphilis, leprosy; Viral: HBV, HCV, HIV; Parasitic: <i>P. falciparum</i> malaria, filariasis)

C) Hereditary Disorders

Alport Syndrome · Thin Basement Membrane Disease · Fabry Disease

## 6 · Nephritic Syndrome (Acute Glomerulonephritis)
<b>🔴 Define nephritic syndrome / Acute Glomerulonephritis (AGN). What are its causes?</b>

<b>Definition:</b> A glomerular disease characterised by <b>inflammation in the glomeruli</b>, manifested as <b>haematuria</b> (red blood cells and RBC casts in urine), <b>proteinuria</b> (usually subnephrotic range), with or without <b>oedema, azotaemia, and hypertension</b>.

Causes of Nephritic Syndrome (AGN):

<b>A. Primary Glomerular Diseases:</b>

Acute proliferative GN: (a) Post-streptococcal GN (Group A β-haemolytic streptococcus) · (b) Non-streptococcal acute GN (post-infectious): Bacterial (staphylococcal endocarditis, pneumococcal pneumonia, meningococcaemia); Viral (HBV, HCV, mumps, HIV, varicella, infectious mononucleosis); Parasitic (malaria, toxoplasmosis)
RPGN (Rapidly Progressive Crescentic GN)
Membranoproliferative GN
IgA Nephropathy

<b>B. Systemic Diseases:</b> Goodpasture syndrome (pulmonary haemorrhage + renal failure), SLE (Lupus nephritis), PAN, GPA (Wegener), Microscopic Polyangiitis, HSP, Cryoglobulinaemia.

Robbins 10e p.906 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.648 · Arif pp.229–230

<b>🔴 Describe the pathogenesis of Post-Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis (PSGN).</b>

<b>Causative agent:</b> Group A β-haemolytic streptococcus (<i>Streptococcus pyogenes</i>).

<b>Lag period:</b> 1–4 weeks after streptococcal pharyngitis (sore throat) or skin infection (infected scabies/impetigo).

<b>PSGN is an immunologically mediated disease — three mechanisms:</b>

Circulating immune complex: Antibody against M-protein of streptococcal cell wall → Ag-Ab complex in circulation → deposition in glomeruli
In-situ immune complex: Streptococcal antigen settles in glomeruli → antibody formed → reacts with settled Ag in glomeruli
Anti-GBM mechanism: Bacterial enzyme (proteinase) alters GBM → Ab against altered GBM → Ag-Ab reaction

<b>Final common pathway:</b> Ag-Ab complexes implanted in GBM → activation of <b>classical complement pathway</b> → formation of Chemotactic factors + Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) → aggregation of neutrophils & macrophages → release of enzymes → injury to epithelial, endothelial & mesangial cells → <b>AGN</b>.

Robbins 10e p.906 · Arif pp.230–231

<b>🔴 Describe the morphology (gross + microscopic) of Acute Diffuse Proliferative GN.</b>

Macroscopic (Gross):

Kidney is enlarged
Surface is smooth, pale with red haemorrhagic spots scattered all over ("flea-bitten kidney")

Microscopic:

Enlarged, hypercellular glomeruli
Combination of proliferation, swelling, and leukocyte infiltration obliterates the capillary lumens
May be interstitial oedema and inflammation
Tubules often contain RBC casts

Robbins 10e p.908 · Arif pp.231

<b>🔴 Mention the clinical features and complications of AGN.</b>

Clinical Features:

Malaise · Fever (low-grade) · Nausea
Oliguria (reduced urine output)
Haematuria — smoky or cola-coloured urine
Mild proteinuria (usually <1 g/day)
Periorbital oedema
Mild to moderate hypertension

Complications (Important for VIVA):

Acute renal failure
Chronic glomerulonephritis
Chronic renal failure
Electrolyte & acid-base disturbance (hyperkalaemia, metabolic acidosis, hyponatraemia)
Hypertensive encephalopathy
Left ventricular failure
Papillary necrosis

Robbins 10e p.909 · Arif pp.231–232

<b>Write about the prognosis of AGN.</b>

<b>Children:</b> >95% eventually recover completely with conservative therapy. A small minority (<1%) become severely oliguric, develop RPGN, and a few may progress to chronic GN (chronic renal failure).

<b>Adults:</b> About 60% recover promptly; some develop RPGN; others develop chronic GN.

Robbins 10e p.909 · Arif p.232

## 7 · Nephrotic Syndrome
<b>🔴 Define nephrotic syndrome. Enumerate its causes. Differentiate primary causes in children vs adults.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> A glomerular syndrome characterised by <b>massive proteinuria (≥3.5 g/day)</b>, <b>hypoalbuminaemia</b> (plasma albumin <3 g/dl), <b>generalised oedema</b>, <b>hyperlipidaemia</b>, and <b>lipiduria</b>.

Causes of Nephrotic Syndrome:

<b>1. Primary Glomerular Diseases:</b>

Minimal-change disease (most common in children)
Membranous glomerulonephritis (most common in adults)
Membranoproliferative GN
Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)
IgA nephropathy

<b>2. Systemic Diseases:</b> Diabetes mellitus, Amyloidosis, SLE, Drugs (NSAIDs, penicillamine, heroin), Infections (malaria, syphilis, HBV, HCV, HIV), Malignancy (carcinoma, lymphoma, myeloma), Miscellaneous (bee sting allergy, hereditary nephritis).

🎯 <b>Children</b> → primary glomerulonephritis most common; most frequent = MCD (65%). <b>Adults</b> → systemic diseases (DM, amyloidosis, SLE) more frequent; most common primary = Membranous GN (40%).

Robbins 10e pp.896, 912 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.648 · Arif pp.232–233

<b>What are the laboratory findings in nephrotic syndrome?</b>

Urinalysis (R/M/E): Massive proteinuria (albumin +++/++++); casts: hyaline & granular casts may be present
24-hour urinary total protein: >3.5 g
Albumin/globulin ratio: Reduced
Serum cholesterol & triglycerides: Increased (hyperlipidaemia)
Serum C₃: Normal
Total serum calcium: Decreased
Renal biopsy: In suitable cases

Arif p.236

## 9 · Minimal Change Disease (Lipoid Nephrosis)
<b>🔴 Write about Minimal Change Disease (lipoid nephrosis). Why is it called lipoid nephrosis?</b>

<b>Definition:</b> The prototype of nephrotic syndrome. Characterised by <b>diffuse effacement of foot processes of podocytes</b> on electron microscopy, with glomeruli appearing virtually normal by light microscopy.

<b>Peak incidence:</b> 2–6 years of age. <b>Dramatic response to corticosteroid therapy</b> (highly steroid-sensitive).

Etiology & Pathogenesis:

Involves some immune dysfunction → elaboration of a <b>cytokine</b> that damages visceral epithelial cells (podocytes) → proteinuria. Ultrastructural changes point to primary visceral epithelial cell injury + loss of glomerular polyanions. <b>Defects in charge barrier</b> contribute to proteinuria.

Morphology:

Gross: Kidney pale/yellowish, slightly larger & heavier than normal
Microscopy (LM): No visible basement membrane changes; lipid accumulation in renal tubular cells
EM: Fused/effaced foot processes of podocytes (pathognomonic finding)

<b>Why "lipoid nephrosis"?</b> Proximal tubular cells are laden with lipid and protein, reflecting tubular reabsorption of lipoproteins passing through diseased glomeruli → historical term.

<b>Prognosis:</b> Excellent — despite massive proteinuria, renal function remains good; excellent long-term prognosis for children and adults.

Robbins 10e p.914 · BRS Pathology 5e p.265 · Arif pp.237

## 10 · Membranous Nephropathy
<b>What is membranous nephropathy? Describe its morphology.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> A common cause of the <b>nephrotic syndrome in adults</b>. Characterised by <b>diffuse thickening of the glomerular capillary wall</b> due to accumulation of deposits containing immunoglobulin along the subepithelial side of the basement membrane.

Morphology:

LM: Uniform diffuse thickening of the glomerular capillary wall
EM: GBM markedly thickened by intramembranous and epimembranous (subepithelial) immune complex deposits appearing as irregular spikes protruding from the GBM → "spike and dome" appearance
IF: Granular deposits of immunoglobulins and various amounts of complement along the GBM

Robbins 10e p.913 · BRS Pathology 5e p.265 · Arif p.237

## 11 · IgA Nephropathy (Berger Disease)
<b>🔴 Write a short note on IgA nephropathy (Berger disease).</b>

<b>Definition:</b> One of the most common causes of <b>recurrent microscopic or gross haematuria</b> and the <b>most common glomerular disease worldwide</b>. Characterised by <b>deposition of IgA in the mesangium</b>.

Pathogenesis:

Genetic or acquired abnormality of immune system
Increased IgA synthesis in response to respiratory or GI infection (virus, bacteria, food protein)
IgA₁ and IgA₁-containing immune complexes trapped in the mesangium
Activation of alternate complement pathway
Glomerular injury

Robbins 10e p.920 · Arif p.238

## 12 · Chronic Glomerulonephritis
<b>What are the causes of chronic glomerulonephritis?</b>

<b>Chronic GN</b> refers to end-stage glomerular disease that may result from specific types of GN or develop without antecedent history of any well-recognised acute GN form.

<b>Primary glomerular diseases that lead to chronic GN:</b>

Post-streptococcal GN
RPGN (Rapidly Progressive GN)
Membranous nephropathy
MPGN (Membranoproliferative GN)
IgA nephropathy
FSGS (Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis)

Robbins 9e p.925 · Arif p.238

## 13 · Diabetic Nephropathy
<b>🔴 Write a short note on diabetic nephropathy. What are the lesions and morphology?</b>

<b>Definition:</b> The conglomerate of lesions that often occur concurrently in the diabetic kidney. It is one of the leading causes of chronic kidney failure. Advanced/end-stage kidney disease occurs in as many as <b>40%</b> of both Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes.

Glomerular Syndromes in Diabetic Nephropathy:

Non-nephrotic proteinuria
Nephrotic syndrome
Chronic renal failure

Diabetes also causes <b>hyalinising arteriolar sclerosis</b>, increased susceptibility to pyelonephritis, papillary necrosis, and various tubular lesions.

Three Categories of Lesions:

Glomerular lesions
Renal vascular lesions (principally arteriosclerosis)
Pyelonephritis including necrotising papillitis

Morphology:

Basement membrane markedly thickened
Diffuse or nodular mesangial accumulations of basement membrane-like material (Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules = nodular glomerulosclerosis — pathognomonic)

Robbins 10e pp.923, 1118 · BRS Pathology 5e p.265 · Arif pp.238–239

## 14 · Acute Tubular Injury / Necrosis (ATI / ATN)
<b>🔴 Define acute tubular injury (ATI) / acute tubular necrosis (ATN). Give its types, pathogenesis, and morphology.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> A clinicopathologic entity characterised by damage to tubular epithelial cells and an <b>acute decline in renal function</b>, often associated with shedding of granular casts and tubular cells into the urine. It is the <b>most common cause of acute kidney injury (AKI)</b> and may produce oliguria (<400 mL/day).

Types & Causes:

<b>1. Ischaemic ATI</b> (decreased/interrupted blood flow): Hypotension & shock (hypovolaemic), severe trauma, acute pancreatitis, HUS (Haemolytic Uraemic Syndrome), TTP (Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura), DIC (Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation).

<b>2. Nephrotoxic ATI</b> (direct toxic injury): Drugs (gentamycin, other antibiotics), radiographic contrast agents, heavy metals (mercury), organic solvents (carbon tetrachloride).

<b>3. Combined ischaemic & nephrotoxic ATI:</b> Haemoglobinuria (mismatched blood transfusion, haemolytic crises), Myoglobinuria (severe skeletal muscle injuries).

Pathogenesis (common pathway):

Two critical events: (1) <b>Tubular injury</b> & (2) <b>Persistent severe disturbances in blood flow</b>. → Tubular backleak of filtrate + sloughed cells + interstitial inflammation → decreased GFR → decreased urine output.

Morphology:

Injury or necrosis of segments of tubules (typically <b>proximal tubules</b>), proteinaceous casts in distal tubules, and <b>interstitial oedema</b>.

Clinical Features:

Electrolyte abnormalities · Acidosis · Uraemia · Fluid overload with oliguria.

Robbins Basic Pathology 10e pp.562, 567–569 · Robbins 10e p.924 · Arif pp.239–240

## 15 · Pyelonephritis
<b>🔴 Define pyelonephritis. What are its types, etiology, and routes of infection?</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Inflammation affecting the <b>tubules, interstitium, and renal pelvis</b>. One of the most common diseases of the kidney.

Types:

<b>1. Acute pyelonephritis</b> — suppurative inflammation caused by bacterial infection; associated with UTI.

<b>2. Chronic pyelonephritis</b> — more complex; bacterial infection + other factors (vesicoureteral reflux, obstruction) predispose to repeat episodes.

Etiology:

<b><i>E. coli</i> (most common)</b> · <i>Proteus</i> · <i>Klebsiella</i> · <i>Enterobacter</i> · <i>Streptococcus faecalis</i> · Staphylococci · Polyoma virus · Cytomegalovirus · Adenovirus.

Routes of Infection:

Ascending infection from lower UTI (most common)
Haematogenous infection (through bloodstream)

Pathogenesis of Ascending Infection:

Colonisation of organisms in distal urethra & introitus (in females)
Entrance from urethra to bladder during catheterisation / instrumentation
Urinary obstruction + stasis favours bacterial multiplication
Vesico-ureteral reflux → bacteria ascend into renal pelvis
Intra-renal reflux → passage deep into renal parenchyma through open ducts at papillae tips

Robbins 10e pp.927–928 · Arif pp.240–241

<b>What are the predisposing factors and complications of acute pyelonephritis?</b>

Predisposing Factors:

Urinary tract obstruction (congenital or acquired)
Instrumentation (most commonly catheterisation)
Vesicoureteral reflux
Pregnancy
Gender & age — females more affected; males increase with prostatic hypertrophy
Pre-existing renal lesions
Diabetes mellitus
Immunosuppression & immunodeficiency

Complications:

Papillary necrosis (mainly in diabetics, sickle cell disease, UT obstruction)
Pyonephrosis (total/near-complete obstruction, especially high in urinary tract)
Perinephric abscess

<b>What are the urinary findings?</b>

Urinary Findings:

<b>Acute:</b> Protein = trace amount; Microscopy = many leucocytes (pyuria), leucocyte/neutrophil casts (pus casts), some RBCs, epithelial cells, bacteria (bacteriuria); Urine culture = positive.

<b>Chronic:</b> Protein in small amount; pus cells.

## 16 · Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)

Types:

<b>Upper tract:</b> Renal parenchyma + renal pelvis · <b>Lower tract:</b> Bladder

Common Pathogens:

## 17 · Acute Renal Failure (ARF) / Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)
<b>🔴 Define acute renal failure / AKI. Classify its causes. Describe the stages.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> A sudden, usually reversible loss of renal function developing over days to weeks, usually accompanied by reduced urine volume. Characterised by rapid decline in GFR (within hours to days) with dysregulation of fluid/electrolyte balance and retention of metabolic waste products (urea, creatinine).

Causes (Prerenal / Renal / Postrenal):

<b>A. Prerenal:</b> (1) Systemic: Heart failure, blood/fluid loss · (2) Local: Renal artery occlusion/stenosis, diseases affecting arterioles

<b>B. Renal (intrinsic):</b> (1) ATN / toxic / septic RF (85%) · (2) Glomerular disease (primary or component of systemic disease) · (3) Interstitial disease

<b>C. Postrenal:</b> Obstruction — stones, tumour, prostatic enlargement

Stages:

Initiating stage
Maintenance stage — Oliguria, uraemia, progressive rise of blood creatinine, hyperkalaemia, metabolic acidosis, salt & water overload
Recovery stage — Gradual increase in urine volume (may reach 3 L/day); blood urea & creatinine return toward normal

Davidson 21e pp.482–483 · Robbins 10e p.898 · Arif pp.242–243

<b>A girl of 6 years has watery diarrhoea and then cessation of micturition. Explain the mechanisms involved.</b>

<b>Pathogenesis (stepwise):</b>

Acute watery diarrhoea → loss of plenty of fluid
Acute hypovolaemia
Several autoregulatory mechanisms come into play
When persistent hypovolaemia is present → these mechanisms fail
Severe prolonged underperfusion of kidneys → acute decline of GFR → low volume, highly concentrated urine
More severe and persistent hypovolaemia → Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN)
ATN → Acute Renal Failure
Cessation of micturition (anuria)

Arif p.243

## 20 · Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC)
<b>🔴 Write about RCC: definition, risk factors, morphology, and clinical features.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> Derived from renal tubular epithelium (adenocarcinoma). Represents <b>80–85% of all primary malignant renal neoplasms</b> and 2–3% of all adult cancers.

Risk Factors / Causes:

Tobacco smoking
Male gender
Obesity (particularly in women)
Hypertension
Unopposed oestrogen therapy
Exposure to asbestos, petroleum products, heavy metals
ESRD, chronic kidney disease, acquired cystic disease, tuberous sclerosis

Morphology — Gross:

Arises in any portion of kidney; more commonly affects the poles
Clear cell carcinoma: Solitary, spherical; yellow to orange to grey-white tissue; areas of cystic softening, haemorrhage (fresh or old)
Papillary RCC: Necrosis, haemorrhage, cystic degeneration; tend to be bilateral and multiple
Chromophobe RCC: Grossly tan-brown

Morphology — Microscopic:

<table>
<tr><th>Type</th><th>%</th><th>Key Features</th></tr>
<tr><td>Clear cell carcinoma</td><td>65%</td><td>Rounded/polygonal cells; abundant clear or granular cytoplasm</td></tr>
<tr><td>Papillary RCC</td><td>10–15%</td><td>Cuboidal/low columnar cells in papillae with fibrovascular cores; clear or pink cytoplasm</td></tr>
<tr><td>Chromophobe RCC</td><td>5%</td><td>Clear, flocculent cytoplasm; very prominent cell membranes; nuclei surrounded by halos of clear cytoplasm</td></tr>
</table>

Clinical Features (Very Important for VIVA):

Haematuria (>50% of cases) — painless haematuria is characteristic
Costovertebral (flank) pain + palpable mass
Constitutional symptoms (fever, malaise, weakness, weight loss)
Polycythaemia (EPO production)
Paraneoplastic syndromes (hypercalcaemia, hypertension, Cushing syndrome, feminisation/masculinisation)
Wide metastases: lungs & bones (most common), then lymph nodes, liver, adrenals, brain

🎯 Classic triad: <b>Painless haematuria + palpable abdominal mass + dull flank pain</b>

Robbins Basic Pathology 10e pp.578–580 · Robbins 10e pp.948–950 · Arif pp.244–246

<b>A male 55 yr with haematuria, loin pain, palpable lumbar mass. Diagnosis?</b>

<b>Likely diagnosis:</b> Renal Cell Carcinoma.

<b>Investigations:</b>

<b>Investigations?</b>

Urine R/E — RBC; Cytology — malignant cells may be present
Blood urea, serum creatinine — renal functional status
USG of whole abdomen
Intravenous urography (IVU)
Plain X-ray KUB — may show calcification
Contrast CT scan of abdomen
Chest X-ray / CT chest — lung metastasis ("cannon ball")
Isotope renogram — functional status of kidneys
Isotope bone scan — bone metastasis

Arif pp.245–246

## 21 · Wilms Tumour (Nephroblastoma)
<b>🔴 Write a short note on Wilms tumour: pathogenesis, morphology, and clinical features.</b>

<b>Definition:</b> The <b>most common primary renal tumour of childhood</b>. Usually occurs between 2–5 years of age. ~5–10% involve both kidneys.

Pathogenesis:

Associated gene at chromosome 11p13
Risk increased with three congenital malformations:
WAGR syndrome — Wilms tumour, Aniridia, Genitourinary anomalies, Mental Retardation
Denys-Drash syndrome — Wilms tumour, ambiguous genitalia, nephropathy
Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome — macrosomia, omphalocele, visceromegaly, macroglossia, hypoglycaemia

Morphology — Gross:

Large, solitary, well-circumscribed mass
10% bilateral or multicentric at diagnosis
Cut section: soft, homogeneous, tan to grey; foci of haemorrhage, cyst formation, necrosis

Morphology — Microscopic:

Different stages of nephrogenesis
Classic triphasic combination: blastemal + stromal + epithelial cell types (in vast majority)
Epithelial differentiation: abortive tubules or glomeruli
Stromal cells: fibrotic or myxoid; may show skeletal muscle differentiation
May show heterologous elements: squamous/mucinous epithelium, smooth muscle, adipose tissue, cartilage, osteoid, neurogenic tissue
~5% show anaplasia

Robbins 10e p.480 · Arif pp.246–247

## 22 · Renal Stones (Urolithiasis)
