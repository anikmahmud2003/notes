# Bones, Joints and Soft Tissues     <i>Full-detail flashcards (Q→A) · All abbreviations expanded · Robbins 4-pillar answers</i>
### 🧠 Big Picture — Bones, Joints and Soft Tissues (boss-level overview)
This chapter covers <b>three interlinked domains</b>:
<b>1. Bone infections — Osteomyelitis:</b> 🔴 Pyogenic (Staphylococcus aureus 80–90%) and tubercular forms; key morphological landmarks = <b>sequestrum</b> (dead bone), <b>involucrum</b> (reactive new bone), <b>Brodie's abscess</b> (intraosseous abscess); <b>Pott's disease</b> = TB spine → kyphosis. Chronicity from delayed diagnosis, extensive necrosis, inadequate antibiotics.
<b>2. Bone tumours — Benign vs Malignant:</b> 🔴 Primary tumours classified by matrix: <b>osteogenic</b> (osteosarcoma = most common primary malignant; osteoid osteoma, osteoblastoma = benign), <b>chondrogenic</b> (chondrosarcoma = 2nd most common malignant; osteochondroma = most frequent benign), <b>unknown origin</b> (Ewing sarcoma = childhood round-cell tumour with onion-skin periosteal reaction; giant cell tumour/osteoclastoma = locally aggressive epiphyseal lesion), <b>notochordal</b> (chordoma). Secondary metastases: prostate, breast, kidney, lung (adults); neuroblastoma, Wilms (children).
<b>3. Joint diseases:</b> 📌 <b>Osteoarthritis (OA)</b> = mechanical/degenerative (weight-bearing joints, cartilage degeneration, osteophytes); <b>Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)</b> = autoimmune (CD4⁺ T cells, pannus, symmetric small joints, ACPA/rheumatoid factor); <b>gout</b> = crystal arthropathy; infectious arthritis; reactive arthritis (Reiter syndrome = arthritis + urethritis + conjunctivitis).
<b>4. Soft tissue tumours:</b> 🎯 Lipoma (most common soft tissue tumour), liposarcoma, rhabdomyoma/myosarcoma, leiomyoma/myosarcoma, haemangioma, angiosarcoma, schwannoma, neurofibroma, synovial sarcoma, fibrosarcoma, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma.
<b>5. Pathological fractures:</b> fracture through diseased bone — causes: osteomyelitis, benign tumours (osteoclastoma), malignant tumours (osteosarcoma, Ewing, metastatic thyroid/kidney/breast), metabolic (osteoporosis, osteomalacia), cystic (aneurysmal bone cyst).

## 1 · Osteomyelitis — Definition, Classification & Routes
<b>🔴 Define osteomyelitis and classify it according to clinical course and aetiology.</b>

<b>Osteomyelitis:</b> inflammation of <b>bone and marrow</b>, virtually always secondary to infection. It frequently manifests as a primary solitary focus of disease but can also be a complication of any systemic infection. All types of organisms — including viruses, parasites, fungi, and bacteria — can produce osteomyelitis, but certain pyogenic bacteria and mycobacteria are the most common culprits.

A) Classification by clinical course

Acute suppurative osteomyelitis
Chronic osteomyelitis:
Chronic suppurative osteomyelitis
Tubercular osteomyelitis
Syphilitic lesions of bone

B) Classification by aetiology

<table>
<tr><th>Group</th><th>Types</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>1. Bacterial</strong> (most common)</td><td>Pyogenic osteomyelitis · Tubercular osteomyelitis · Syphilitic osteomyelitis</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>2. Viral</strong></td><td>Viral osteomyelitis</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>3. Fungal</strong></td><td>Fungal osteomyelitis</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>4. Parasitic</strong></td><td>Parasitic osteomyelitis</td></tr>
</table>

Robbins 10e p.1185; Arif 2025 Vol-2 p.348

<b>Enlist the routes by which bacteria enter the bone.</b>

Bacteria may reach the bone by:

Haematogenous spread (most common route, especially in children)
Extension from a contiguous site (e.g. nearby soft tissue infection, open fracture)
Direct implantation (e.g. in traumatic injury, surgical procedures)

In adults, osteomyelitis more often occurs as a complication of <b>open fractures, surgical procedures, and infections of the feet in the setting of diabetes</b>.

Robbins 10e p.1185

## 2 · Chronic Osteomyelitis — Pathogenesis, Predisposing Factors & Etiologic Agents
<b>🔴 Describe the aetiopathogenesis and predisposing factors of chronic osteomyelitis. What are the etiologic agents?</b>

<b>Definition:</b> "A severe, persistent and incapacitating infection of bone and bone marrow."

A) Predisposing factors

History of trauma
Debilitation
Immunodeficiency state
Sickle cell anaemia (especially Salmonella osteomyelitis)

B) Etiologic agents

<table>
<tr><th>Group</th><th>Organisms</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>1. Pyogenic bacteria</strong></td><td><span class="hot">Staphylococcus aureus</span> (80–90%) · Group B streptococci · <em>E. coli</em> · Pseudomonas · Klebsiella · Salmonella · <em>Haemophilus influenzae</em></td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>2. Mycobacteria</strong></td><td>Tubercular osteomyelitis (<em>Mycobacterium tuberculosis</em>)</td></tr>
</table>

C) Pathogenesis & morphology — sequential steps

Localised infection at metaphyseal endosteum
Bacteria proliferate → induce acute inflammatory reaction → cell death
Entrapped bone undergoes necrosis within the first 48 hours
Bacteria and inflammation spread within the shaft → percolate through the Haversian system to reach the periosteum
Subperiosteal abscess formation → lifts the periosteum
Impairs blood supply → ischaemia and suppurative necrosis of bone
Segmental bone necrosis → formation of a piece of dead bone = sequestrum
Formation of soft tissue abscess → rupture through periosteum → draining sinus
After ~1 week, chronic inflammatory cells cause fibrous growth and formation of reactive bone around the devitalised bone = involucrum
The interosseous stenotic abscess = Brodie's abscess

Robbins 10e pp.1185–1186; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.349–350

<b>When does acute osteomyelitis turn into the chronic form?</b>

In <b>5–25% of cases</b>, acute osteomyelitis fails to resolve and persists as chronic infection. Chronicity may develop in the following situations:

Delay in diagnosis
Extensive bone necrosis
Inadequate antibiotic therapy
Inadequate surgical debridement
Weakened host defence

Robbins Basic Pathology 10e p.807; Arif 2025 Vol-2 p.351

## 3 · Sequestrum, Brodie's Abscess & Involucrum
<b>🔴 What is a sequestrum? Describe its cause, mechanism, and fate.</b>

<b>Sequestrum:</b> a <b>dead piece of bone within a living bone</b>, produced as a result of avascular necrosis from underlying infection, ultimately separated by osteoclasts.

<b>Cause:</b> Chronic pyogenic osteomyelitis.

<b>Mechanism of formation:</b> bacteria at the metaphyseal endosteum → acute inflammation → bone necrosis within 48 h → spread through Haversian canals → subperiosteal abscess → periosteal lifting → ischaemia → segmental necrosis of bone → dead bone fragment = sequestrum.

<b>Fate of sequestrum</b> (depends on wound size):

If small → can be absorbed by osteoclastic activity and replaced by granulation tissue (resolution)
If large → persists for long with chronic infection inside and discharging sinus

Robbins 10e p.1186; Arif 2025 Vol-2 p.351

<b>🔴 What is Brodie's abscess?</b>

<b>Brodie's abscess:</b> a <b>small intraosseous abscess</b> that frequently involves the cortex, walled off by reactive bone. It is a <b>sequelae of chronic osteomyelitis</b>.

Alternatively: a <b>circumscribed, round/oval cavity containing pus and pieces of dead bone (sequestra) surrounded by sclerosis</b>.

Robbins Basic Pathology 10e p.807; Arif 2025 Vol-2 p.351

<b>What is an involucrum? (VIVA favourite)</b>

<b>Involucrum:</b> the <b>reactive lamellar/woven bone</b> that forms around devitalised (necrotic) bone in chronic osteomyelitis. It is produced by chronic inflammatory cells about one week after the initial infection, and represents the body's attempt to wall off and stabilise the necrotic segment.

Together with <b>sequestrum</b> (dead bone inside) and a <b>cloaca</b> (opening in the involucrum through which sequestra and pus may extrude), the involucrum is a hallmark of chronic osteomyelitis morphology.

Robbins 10e p.1186; SMU-23M exam question

## 4 · Clinical Features — Pyogenic vs Tuberculous Osteomyelitis
<b>🔴 Compare the clinical features of pyogenic and tuberculous osteomyelitis.</b>

Pyogenic osteomyelitis

Malaise
Fever
Chills
Leukocytosis
Intense throbbing pain over the infected bone
Characteristic X-ray findings: lytic focus of bone destruction surrounded by a zone of sclerosis
Blood cultures can be positive (in untreated cases)

Tuberculous osteomyelitis

Localised pain
Low-grade fever
Chills
Weight loss

Robbins 10e pp.1186–1187; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.351–352

<b>🔴 What is Pott's disease? Describe its clinical features and complications.</b>

<b>Pott's disease:</b> <b>tuberculous osteomyelitis of the spine</b> (especially thoracic and lumbar vertebrae), which is the <b>most common site of skeletal TB</b>.

In the spine, infection breaks through <b>intervertebral discs to involve multiple vertebrae</b> and extends into the surrounding soft tissues, forming abscesses.

Clinical features

Localised pain
Low-grade fever
Chills
Weight loss

Complications

Permanent compression fracture of discs & vertebrae → severe scoliosis, kyphosis, and neurologic deficits
Tuberculous arthritis
Sinus tract formation
Psoas abscess
Amyloidosis (secondary AA amyloid from chronic inflammation)

Robbins 10e pp.1186–1187; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.352–353

## 5 · Diseases Associated with Abnormal Mineral Homeostasis
<b>What are the diseases associated with abnormal mineral homeostasis of bone?</b>

Three main disease groups:

Rickets (children) and osteomalacia (adults) — defective mineralisation of osteoid
Hyperparathyroidism — excess parathyroid hormone (PTH) → increased osteoclastic bone resorption
Renal osteodystrophy — bone disease secondary to chronic kidney disease (includes osteitis fibrosa cystica, osteomalacia, osteosclerosis, osteoporosis)

## 8 · Bone-Forming Tumours (Osteogenic)
<b>What are the bone-forming tumours? Describe their key features.</b>

<b>Bone-forming tumours</b> are characterised by production of bone by neoplastic cells. The tumour bone is usually deposited as <b>woven trabeculae</b> (except in osteomas) and is variably mineralised.

<b>The bone-forming tumours are:</b>

Osteoid osteoma (benign)
Osteoblastoma (benign)
Osteosarcoma (malignant — most common primary malignant bone tumour)

## 10 · Osteosarcoma
<b>🔴 Describe osteosarcoma — definition, age, sites, morphology (gross + microscopic), clinical features, and radiographic findings.</b>

<b>Osteosarcoma:</b> the <b>most common primary malignant tumour of bone</b>, accounting for approximately <b>20% of bone cancers</b>.

Age & demographics

Bimodal age distribution: 75% occur before 20 years of age; smaller peak in older adults (>60 yr) — frequently associated with predisposing conditions: Paget disease of bone, infarcts, and prior radiation (sometimes called secondary osteosarcomas)
Males > females (ratio 1.6 : 1)

Sites of origin

Tumours usually arise in the <b>metaphyseal region of long bones</b>; almost 50% are near the <b>knee — distal femur or proximal tibia</b>. Although any bone can be involved.

Morphology — Gross

Bulky, gritty, grey-white tumours often containing haemorrhage and cystic degeneration
Frequently destroy surrounding cortices and produce soft tissue masses
Spread extensively in the medullary canal, replacing haematopoietic marrow
Infrequently, they penetrate the epiphyseal plate, or enter the joint

Morphology — Microscopic

Tumour cells vary in size and shape (pleomorphism) with large hyperchromatic nuclei
Bizarre tumour giant cells and abundant mitoses including abnormal (e.g. tripolar) forms are common
Extensive necrosis and intravascular invasion are common
If neoplastic cells produce abundant cartilage → classified as chondroblastic osteosarcoma

Clinical features

Painful progressively enlarged mass
Pathologic fractures

<b>Metastases:</b> lungs, bones, brain, and elsewhere.

Radiographic findings

Destructive, mixed lytic and blastic mass with infiltrative margins
Frequently breaks through the cortex and lifts the periosteum, inducing reactive periosteal bone formation
Codman triangle — triangular shadow between the cortex and raised periosteal ends (indicates aggressive tumour)

Robbins 10e pp.1188; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.354–356

<b>A 17-year-old male presents with pain and progressive swelling around the right knee. X-ray shows mixed osteoblastic and lytic mass at the lower part of the femur. What is your diagnosis?</b>

<b>Diagnosis:</b> <b>Osteosarcoma</b>

The age group (adolescent/young adult), metaphyseal location around the knee (distal femur), and the mixed lytic/blastic X-ray appearance are all classic features of osteosarcoma.

Arif 2025 Vol-2 p.356

## 11 · Chondrosarcoma
<b>🔴 Describe chondrosarcoma — definition, subtypes, age, sites, and morphology.</b>

<b>Chondrosarcoma:</b> malignant cartilage-producing tumours. After osteosarcoma, it is the <b>second most common malignant matrix-producing tumour of bone</b>.

Subtypes

Conventional · Clear cell · Dedifferentiated · Mesenchymal.

Age & demographics

Usually in their <b>40s or older</b>; men affected twice as frequently as women.

Sites of origin

Commonly arise in the <b>axial skeleton</b> — pelvis, shoulder, and ribs. Unlike benign enchondroma, <b>distal extremities are rarely involved</b>.

Morphology — Gross

Large bulky tumours made up of nodules of glistening grey-white, translucent cartilage
Matrix is often gelatinous or myxoid and can ooze from the cut surface
Spotty calcifications typically present; central necrosis may create cystic spaces
Tumour spreads through the cortex into surrounding muscle and fat

Morphology — Microscopic

Malignant cartilage infiltrates the marrow space & surrounds pre-existing bony trabeculae
Tumours vary in degree of cellularity, cytologic atypia & mitotic activity

Robbins 10e p.1191; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.356–357

## 12 · Ewing Sarcoma
<b>🔴 Describe Ewing sarcoma — definition, age, sites, morphology (gross + histology), clinical features, and radiographic findings.</b>

<b>Ewing sarcoma:</b> a malignant bone tumour characterised by <b>primitive round cells without obvious differentiation</b>. Accounts for approximately <b>6–10% of primary malignant bone tumours</b> and is the <b>second most common group of bone sarcomas in children</b>.

Age & demographics

Most affected individuals are <b>10 to 15 years old</b>; approximately 80% are younger than 20 years. Boys affected slightly more frequently than girls.

Sites of origin

Usually arises in the <b>diaphysis of long tubular bones</b>, especially the <b>femur</b> and the <b>flat bones of the pelvis</b>. Presents as a painful, enlarging mass.

Morphology — Gross

Arises in the medullary cavity and invades the cortex, periosteum, and soft tissue
Soft, tan-white tumour with areas of haemorrhage and necrosis

Morphology — Histology

Sheets of uniform small, round cells with scant cytoplasm that may be clear due to abundant glycogen
Homer-Wright rosettes (rounded cell clusters with a central fibrillary core) → indicate neuroectodermal differentiation
Fibrous septae may be present; little stroma
Geographic necrosis may be prominent

Clinical features

Painful enlarging masses; affected site is tender, warm and swollen
Some may have fever, high ESR, anaemia, leukocytosis

Radiographic findings

Destructive lytic tumour with permeative or moth-eaten margins extending into surrounding soft tissues
Characteristic periosteal reaction producing layers of reactive bone in an "onion-skin" fashion

Robbins 10e p.1192; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.357–358

## 13 · Giant Cell Tumour (Osteoclastoma)
<b>🔴 Describe the giant cell tumour of bone — definition, age, sites, behaviour, and morphology (gross + microscopic).</b>

<b>Giant cell tumour:</b> so named because it is characterised by the presence of numerous <b>multinucleated osteoclast-type giant cells</b>. Also called <b>osteoclastoma</b>. Although classified as benign, it can be <b>locally aggressive</b>.

Age & demographics

Uncommon tumour; usually arises in the <b>third to fifth decades of life</b> (20–40 years).

Sites of origin

Develop within the <b>epiphysis</b> and may extend into the metaphysis. The majority are near the <b>knee</b> (distal femur or proximal tibia), but virtually any bone can be involved.

Morphology — Gross (macroscopic)

Large, red-brown mass
Frequently undergo cystic degeneration

Morphology — Microscopic

Consist of uniform oval mononuclear tumour cells and abundant osteoclast-type giant cells with 100 or more nuclei
Necrosis and mitotic activity may be prominent
Although reactive bone may be present (especially at periphery), tumour cells do NOT synthesise bone or cartilage

Robbins 10e p.1193; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.358–359

## 14 · Soft Tissue Tumours
<b>🔴 Define soft tissue tumour and classify it. Give examples of common benign and malignant soft tissue tumours.</b>

<b>Soft tissue tumour:</b> tumours that originate in <b>fibrous connective tissue, adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, joint tissue, and the peripheral nervous system</b>.

<i>N.B.: In terms of clinical and pathologic entities, soft tissue refers to non-epithelial tissue excluding the skeleton, joints, central nervous system, haematopoietic and lymphoid tissues.</i>

Examples — Benign

<table>

<b>What is osteoarthritis (OA)?</b>

<b>Osteoarthritis (OA)</b>, also called <b>degenerative joint disease</b>, is characterised by <b>cartilage degeneration that results in structural and functional failure of synovial joints</b>. It is the <b>most common type of joint disease</b>.

Primary pathogenic abnormality = <b>mechanical injury to articular cartilage</b>. Primarily involves <b>weight-bearing joints</b> (knees, hips). Pathology includes cartilage degeneration and fragmentation, bone spurs (osteophytes), subchondral cysts, with <b>minimal inflammation</b>. No serum antibodies. <b>No involvement of other organs.</b>

Robbins 10e pp.1197–1198; Arif 2025 Vol-2 p.361

## 16 · Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) — Pathogenesis & Morphology
<b>🔴 Describe rheumatoid arthritis — definition, occurrence, pathogenesis, cytokines, and morphology (joints + extra-articular).</b>

<b>Rheumatoid arthritis (RA):</b> a <b>chronic autoimmune disorder</b> that principally attacks the joints, producing a <b>non-suppurative proliferative and inflammatory synovitis</b>. RA often progresses to articular cartilage destruction and, in some cases, joint fusion (<b>ankylosis</b>). Extra-articular lesions may involve skin, heart, blood vessels, and lungs.

Occurrence

Three times more common in <b>women than men</b>. Most common in those aged <b>40 to 70</b>.

Pathogenesis

RA is an autoimmune disease mediated by <b>antibodies against self-antigens</b> and inflammation caused by cytokines, predominantly secreted by <b>CD4⁺ T cells</b>. CD4⁺ T helper (T<sub>H</sub>) cells initiate the autoimmune response by reacting with an <b>arthritogen</b> — perhaps microbial or a chemically modified self-antigen.

Continuing autoimmune reaction → Activation of CD4⁺ T helper cells and other lymphocytes → Local release of inflammatory mediators and cytokines → Destruction of bone and cartilage in joints.

Mediators (cytokines) and their role in joint injury

<tr><th>Cytokine / Molecule</th><th>Source</th><th>Role</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>IFN-γ</strong></td><td>T<sub>H</sub> cells</td><td>Activates macrophages and synovial cells</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>IL-17</strong></td><td>T<sub>H</sub>17 cells</td><td>Recruits neutrophils and monocytes</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>RANKL</strong></td><td>Activated T cells</td><td>Stimulates osteoclasts → bone resorption</td></tr>

Morphology — Joints

RA typically affects small joints of the hands and feet
Grossly: synovium is oedematous, thickened, and hyperplastic
Histologic features:
Synovial cell hyperplasia and proliferation
Dense inflammatory infiltrates (often forming lymphoid follicles) of CD4⁺ helper T cells, B cells, plasma cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages
Increased vascularity due to angiogenesis
Fibrinopurulent exudate on the synovial and joint surfaces
Osteoclastic activity in subchondral bone → inflamed synovium penetrates bone → periarticular erosions and subchondral cysts
Pannus formation (exuberant synovium invading and destroying cartilage)

## 17 · Reiter Syndrome
<b>What is Reiter syndrome? (VIVA)</b>

<b>Reiter syndrome:</b> a form of <b>reactive arthritis</b>, defined by the <b>triad</b>:

Arthritis
Non-gonococcal urethritis or cervicitis
Conjunctivitis

🎯 <b>"Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree"</b> = conjunctivitis + urethritis + arthritis.

## 🔁 Rapid-Fire MCQ & Viva Sweep (incl. folded Systemic Pathology MCQs)
<b>Bones, Joints & Soft Tissues — True/False & one-liners (test yourself, then reveal)</b>

Characteristics of pyogenic osteomyelitis: (a) Always occurs by blood-borne infection? (b) May be induced by viruses? (c) Commonest organism is <i>E. coli</i>? (d) Sequestrum formation? (e) Chronicity is a common consequence?

<b>(a) False</b> — can also occur by direct implantation or contiguous spread. <b>(b) True</b> — viruses can rarely cause it. <b>(c) False</b> — <i>S. aureus</i> is the commonest (80–90%). <b>(d) False</b> — sequestrum is a feature of <i>chronic</i>, not acute, osteomyelitis (MCQ answer is F). <b>(e) True</b> — in 5–25% of cases chronicity develops.

Ewing sarcoma is characterized by: (a) Involvement of long bone only? (b) Metaphysis commonly affected? (c) Onion skin appearance radiologically? (d) Painful presentation common? (e) Prognosis always favourable?

<b>(a) False</b> — also flat bones (pelvis, ribs). <b>(b) False</b> — diaphysis is commonly affected. <b>(c) True</b> — classic onion-skin periosteal reaction. <b>(d) True</b> — painful enlarging mass. <b>(e) False</b> — aggressive tumour, prognosis variable.

Malignant tumours of bone are: (a) Paget's disease? (b) Osteoblastoma? (c) Multiple myeloma? (d) Ewing's tumour? (e) Chordoma?

<b>(a) False</b> — Paget's is a metabolic bone disease. <b>(b) False</b> — osteoblastoma is benign. <b>(c) True</b>. <b>(d) True</b>. <b>(e) True</b> — chordoma is malignant (notochordal).

Osteosarcoma: (a) Is a secondary malignant tumour of bone? (b) Is a malignant tumour that produces cartilage? (c) Majority occurs in younger men? (d) Presents as painful gradually enlarging mass? (e) Majority occurs around the wrist joint?

<b>(a) False</b> — it is the most common <i>primary</i> malignant tumour. <b>(b) False</b> — it produces <i>bone</i> (osteoid). <b>(c) True</b> — M:F = 1.6:1, 75% before age 20. <b>(d) True</b>. <b>(e) False</b> — majority around the <i>knee</i> (distal femur/proximal tibia).

Osteomyelitis is related to: (a) Pathological fracture? (b) Sequestrum formation? (c) No new bone formation? (d) <i>Salmonella typhi</i> may cause it? (e) Only bone is inflamed?

<b>(a) True</b>. <b>(b) True</b>. <b>(c) False</b> — new bone (involucrum) <i>does</i> form. <b>(d) True</b> — especially in sickle cell patients. <b>(e) False</b> — marrow and soft tissues are also affected.

Pyogenic osteomyelitis: (a) Occurs by blood-borne infection? (b) Is caused by bacteria? (c) Is caused frequently by <i>Streptococcus</i>? (d) Frequently shows granuloma? (e) Frequently reveals new bone formation?

<b>(a) True</b> — most common route. <b>(b) True</b>. <b>(c) False</b> — <i>S. aureus</i> is the most frequent. <b>(d) False</b> — pyogenic = neutrophilic, not granulomatous. <b>(e) True</b> — involucrum formation.

Bone-forming tumours include: (a) Giant cell tumour? (b) Osteoid osteoma? (c) Enchondroma? (d) Chondroblastoma? (e) Osteosarcoma?

<b>(a) False</b> — giant cell tumour is of unknown origin. <b>(b) True</b>. <b>(c) False</b> — enchondroma is cartilage-forming. <b>(d) False</b> — chondroblastoma is cartilage-forming. <b>(e) True</b>.

Ewing's sarcoma: (a) Is a childhood tumour? (b) Occurs in epiphysis of long bone? (c) Associated with p53 gene mutation? (d) Has prominent necrosis? (e) Has characteristic lytic lesion in X-ray?

<b>(a) True</b> — 80% <20 yr. <b>(b) False</b> — occurs in the <i>diaphysis</i>. <b>(c) False</b> — associated with <i>EWS-FLI1</i> translocation (t(11;22)). <b>(d) True</b> — geographic necrosis. <b>(e) True</b> — destructive lytic tumour with permeative margins.

Osteomalacia: (a) May occur in coeliac disease? (b) Associated with normal serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP)? (c) Characterised by excessive osteoid formation which is poorly mineralised? (d) May be complicated by treatment with phenobarbitone? (e) Associated with atrophy of parathyroid gland?

<b>(a) True</b> — vitamin D malabsorption. <b>(b) False</b> — serum ALP is <i>elevated</i>. <b>(c) True</b> — defective mineralisation. <b>(d) True</b> — phenobarbitone induces vitamin D metabolism. <b>(e) False</b> — parathyroid hormone (PTH) is usually elevated secondary (secondary hyperparathyroidism).

🎯 One-liner: Most common organism in osteomyelitis? Most common primary malignant bone tumour? Most frequent benign bone tumour? Classic X-ray of Ewing? Codman triangle seen in?

<i>S. aureus</i> (80–90%) · Osteosarcoma · Osteochondroma · Onion-skin periosteal reaction · Osteosarcoma.

🎯 Mnemonic: "Pott's disease" = ? · "Onion skin" periosteal reaction = ? · "Codman triangle" = ? · "Pannus" = ? · "Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree" = ?

Pott's disease = <b>TB of spine</b> · Onion skin = <b>Ewing sarcoma</b> · Codman triangle = <b>Osteosarcoma</b> · Pannus = <b>Rheumatoid arthritis</b> · Can't see/pee/climb = <b>Reiter syndrome</b>.

🎯 Most common site of skeletal TB? Most common primary bone tumour in children (after excluding haematopoietic)? Giant cell tumour location? Most common soft tissue tumour? Most common site of osteosarcoma?

Thoracic/lumbar spine (Pott's) · Ewing sarcoma · Epiphysis of long bones (near knee) · Lipoma · Metaphysis of long bones (distal femur/proximal tibia).

🎯 Rheumatoid arthritis is associated with which serum antibodies? OA has which serum antibodies? RA mainly affects which joints first? OA mainly affects which joints?

RA: ACPA (anti-citrullinated peptide antibody) + rheumatoid factor · OA: none · RA: small joints of hands and feet · OA: weight-bearing joints (knees, hips).

🎯 Name the cytokines in RA joint destruction: which cytokine activates macrophages? which recruits neutrophils? which stimulates osteoclasts? which stimulates protease secretion?

IFN-γ (from T<sub>H</sub> cells) activates macrophages · IL-17 (from T<sub>H</sub>17) recruits neutrophils · RANKL (from T cells) stimulates osteoclasts · TNF and IL-1β (from macrophages) stimulate protease secretion.

🎯 Homer-Wright rosettes are seen in which tumour? Onion-skin periosteal reaction? Codman triangle? Multinucleated giant cells with 100+ nuclei?

Homer-Wright rosettes = <b>Ewing sarcoma</b> · Onion-skin = <b>Ewing sarcoma</b> · Codman triangle = <b>Osteosarcoma</b> · Giant cells with 100+ nuclei = <b>Giant cell tumour (osteoclastoma)</b>.

🎯 Secondary bone tumours in adults: most common primary sites? In children? Which tumour causes pathological fracture in sickle cell patients?

Adults: prostate, breast, kidney, lung · Children: neuroblastoma, Wilms tumour, osteosarcoma, Ewing, rhabdomyosarcoma · Salmonella osteomyelitis in sickle cell patients → pathological fracture.

🎯 What is Reiter syndrome? What are its three components? Name the mnemonic.

Reiter syndrome = reactive arthritis triad: <b>arthritis + non-gonococcal urethritis/cervicitis + conjunctivitis</b>. Mnemonic: "Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree."

🎯 In osteomyelitis pathogenesis: what is a sequestrum? What is an involucrum? What is a Brodie's abscess? What is a cloaca?

<b>Sequestrum</b> = dead piece of bone separated by osteoclasts · <b>Involucrum</b> = reactive new bone formed around sequestrum · <b>Brodie's abscess</b> = small intraosseous abscess walled off by reactive bone · <b>Cloaca</b> = opening in involucrum through which pus and sequestra extrude.

🎯 Carcinoma of prostate is associated with: raised acid phosphatase? frequent bony metastasis? raised PSA? osteosclerotic or osteolytic bone metastases?

Raised acid phosphatase = <b>True</b> · Frequent bony metastasis = <b>True</b> · Raised PSA = <b>True</b> · <b>Osteosclerotic</b> (blastic) bone metastases.
