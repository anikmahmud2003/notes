🧠 Big Picture — Renal Function Test (RFT) — the boss-level overview

All kidney function tests fall into three big buckets: (1) Urine examination (routine physical, chemical, microscopic + special tests), (2) Blood analysis (blood urea, serum creatinine, serum uric acid, serum electrolytes), and (3) Renal clearance tests (creatinine clearance / GFR measurement) plus imaging & biopsy.

The clearance concept is the single most important idea — it measures the virtual volume of plasma cleared of a substance per minute, and it directly quantifies GFR (glomerular filtration rate), RPF (renal plasma flow) and RBF (renal blood flow).

Two classic syndromes tie it all together: Nephrotic (protein-wasting → massive proteinuria, hypoalbuminaemia, oedema, hyperlipidaemia) vs Nephritic/AGN (inflammatory → haematuria, RBC casts, azotemia, hypertension, oliguria).

🎯 One-liner sweep: Creatinine clearance = No.1 test for GFR · Serum creatinine = No.1 single test in kidney function · Inulin = gold standard clearance (125 ml/min) · RBC cast = hallmark of AGN · Hyaline cast = normal · E. coli = 70% of UTI.

6 · Microscopic Findings of Urine in Pathological Conditions

🔴 Write the microscopic findings of urine in pathological conditions (SU-14Ju); Enumerate diseases that can be diagnosed by urine examination — explain with clinical and laboratory findings (RU-15Ju); Mention the indications of renal biopsy (for VIVA).

Microscopic findings of urine in pathological conditions

1. RBC — different causes of haematuria, such as:

  • Acute glomerulonephritis (AGN)
  • Acute pyelonephritis
  • Renal calculi
  • Renal tuberculosis
  • Renal cell carcinoma
  • Neoplasm of ureter, urinary bladder and urethra
  • Bleeding disorders e.g. haemophilia, thrombocytopenia, DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation)
  • Overdose of anticoagulant therapy
  • Inflammation: e.g. cystitis, urethritis, etc.
  • Trauma to any part of urinary tract

2. Pus cells: Urinary tract infection.

3. Epithelial cells: Increased epithelial cells signify contaminated urine, not appropriate for culture.

4. Different casts: Epithelial casts → renal tubular necrosis (RTN) · RBC casts → AGN · WBC casts → pyelonephritis · Granular casts → glomerulonephritis · Hyaline casts → normal.

5. Different crystals (e.g. calcium oxalate, struvite/triple phosphate): different types of renal, ureteric or urinary bladder stones.

Indications of renal biopsy

  1. Unexplained acute renal failure
  2. CKD (chronic kidney disease / chronic renal failure) with normal-sized kidney
  3. Nephrotic syndrome in adults
  4. Steroid non-responsive nephrotic syndrome in children
  5. Isolated haematuria or haematuria

State the procedure for collection of sample of urine for routine examination (CU-10Ju).
  1. Urine is collected in a clean, dry, sterile pot / sterilized test tube
  2. For male: mid-stream urine is normally collected
  3. For female: before collection, vulva and vestibule must be washed with soap and rinsed with dry cloth; she must urinate keeping vulva apart; mid-stream urine is collected

7 · Renal Clearance Test

🔴 What do you mean by renal clearance test? (DU-07Ju, RU-13Ju,12Ja); State the importance of renal clearance test; Short note: Renal clearance test; Why is clearance important for GFR?

Renal clearance test: It is the virtual volume of plasma (in ml) that is completely cleared of a particular substance by both kidneys in each minute.

Or, renal clearance may be defined as the virtual volume of plasma which contains the amount of that substance excreted through urine per minute.

🎯 Formula: Renal clearance (ml/min) = (Concentration of substance in urine × Volume of urine [ml/min]) ÷ Concentration of substance in plasma

Examples

ClearanceNormal value
Renal clearance of inulin (Cinulin)125 ml/min
Renal clearance of creatinine (Ccreatinine, CCr)70 – 140 ml/min
Renal clearance of urea (Curea)60 – 70 ml/min

Importance of plasma clearance

  1. It is a good marker of renal function
  2. Assessment of severity of renal impairment
  3. Measurement of GFR (glomerular filtration rate), RPF (renal plasma flow) and RBF (renal blood flow)
  4. Calculation of filtration fraction
  5. Monitoring of drug
  6. It can be used to quantify the basic functions of the kidney — glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption and tubular secretion

🔴 Write the clinical importance of serum creatinine (DU-11Ju,10Ju/Ja, SU-19Nv,17M); Short note: Serum creatinine (CU-15Ja,08Ju); Why serum creatinine is more specific than serum urea as a renal function test? (DU-14Ja,12Ju, RU-17Ju,16Ja); Why serum creatinine is more specific RFT? (DU-09/08Ju, RU-13Ja,10Ju); Why is serum creatinine estimation more valuable than blood urea? (DU-07Ju).

Creatinine

Creatinine is a breakdown product of creatine phosphate in muscle, and is usually produced at a fairly constant rate by the body (depending on muscle mass).

Normal level: 0.7 – 1.4 mg/dl (60 – 120 μmol/L).

Causes of increased serum creatinine level

Reduced excretion in: (1) Renal failure · (2) Competition for tubular secretion with some drugs (e.g. trimethoprim, cimetidine).

High production in: (1) Rhabdomyolysis · (2) Large muscle mass.

Ref: Davidson's 20e p.461; Lecture SSMC

Why serum creatinine is more specific than serum urea (⚠️ Very Important for VIVA)

Blood urea is a poor guide to renal excretory function as it varies with protein intake, liver metabolic capacity and renal perfusion.

Serum creatinine is more specific because it is produced from muscle at a constant rate and almost completely filtered at the glomerulus. If muscle mass remains constant, changes in creatinine concentration reflect changes in GFR (glomerular filtration rate).

Heat coagulation test

Principle: Coagulation of protein is caused by the denaturation of protein structure by heat. (Secondary, tertiary and quaternary structures are lost, but the primary structure remains intact.)

Requirements: A test tube · Bunsen burner · 5% acetic acid · Supplied urine (or protein solution).

Procedure:

  1. About two-thirds of a test tube is filled with supplied urine
  2. Then the upper one-third of the solution is heated slowly keeping the test tube at an inclined position
  3. Cloudiness appears, which becomes deep on addition of a few drops of 5% acetic acid (glacial acetic acid) — this indicates the presence of albumin
  4. If cloudiness vanishes on addition of acetic acid, then it is due to excess phosphate present in urine

Grading of cloudiness

CloudinessGrading
AbsentNil
Distinctly cloudy+ (normal)
Granular appearance++ (moderate)
Granule large, flocculent+++ (massive)

24-hour urinary protein (CU-13Ju)

24-hour urine protein measures the amount of protein released in urine over a 24-hour period. The test checks the function of the kidneys and detects disease. Urine samples are collected in one or more containers over 24 hours, kept in a cool environment, then sent to the lab for analysis. The test is simple and non-invasive.

Normal value: < 80 mg of protein per day.

Indications: Glomerulonephritis · Uncontrolled diabetes · High blood pressure · SLE (systemic lupus erythematosus) · UTI (urinary tract infection) · Multiple myeloma · Amyloidosis.

Disadvantages: (1) Does not show what kinds of protein are in the urine · (2) Does not show the cause of the protein · (3) Occasionally proteinuria is not a sign of kidney damage (specially for children) · (4) Other factors, such as extreme stress, vigorous exercise, dehydration, may influence results.

Microalbuminuria

Microalbuminuria describes the urinary excretion of small amounts of normal albumin protein (30 – 300 mg/day) which is not detected by conventional methods.

Significance of microalbuminuria

  1. Early sign of diabetic nephropathy
  2. Increased risk of atherosclerosis and cardiovascular mortality

What do you mean by Bence-Jones protein? (RU-17Nv, CU-14Ju); How is Bence-Jones protein detected in urine? (SU-10Ja); Short notes: Bence-Jones protein and its detection (DU-14Ju); Bence-Jones protein (DU-13Ja,10Ju, SU-20/18M,15/09Ja, RU-17Ja,15Ju,13Ja,10Ju, CU-10Ja).

Bence-Jones protein: It is an immunoglobulin light chain, either κ (kappa) or λ (lambda).

Found in various pathological conditions like

  • Multiple myeloma
  • Macroglobulinaemia
  • Amyloidosis
  • Lymphoma
  • Leukaemia

Ref: de Gruchy 5e pp.306–307

Detection of Bence-Jones protein in urine

  1. Heat test: Bence-Jones proteins have unique behaviour on heating. Urine containing Bence-Jones protein flocculates when heated slowly to 50–60 °C. The flocculated proteins dissolve on boiling, and reappear on cooling below 60 °C.
  2. Electrophoretic method: The most reliable means for detection is electrophoresis of a concentrated urine specimen on cellulose acetate. The monoclonal light chains usually migrate as a narrow band in the globulin region.

Ref: de Gruchy 5e pp.306–307

Reducing substances: substances that can reduce Benedict solution.

Most important urinary reducing substances

1. Sugar substances: Glucose, lactose, pentose, fructose & galactose.

2. Non-sugar substances: Homogentisic acid (present in alkaptonuria) · Nalidixic acid · Drugs — cephalosporin, ascorbic acid or aspirin.

B. Quantitative test: can be done using Benedict's quantitative reagent, but not usually done for practical purposes.

Benedict's test

Principle: Cupric salt is reduced by glucose in hot alkaline solution to cuprous salt, giving a visible colour.

Procedure:

  1. Take 5 ml of Benedict qualitative reagent in a test tube
  2. Heat the tube over a flame to expel any impurities
  3. Add 8 drops (0.5 ml) of urine & heat again for about 2 min
  4. Then allow cooling

Haematuria: presence of RBC in urine is called haematuria. It indicates bleeding from anywhere in the renal tract.

Causes of haematuria (V.V.I. for VIVA)

A. Renal causes — blood is intimately mixed with urine:

  • Acute glomerulonephritis (AGN)
  • Renal calculi
  • Infections — severe acute pyelonephritis, papillary necrosis, renal tuberculosis
  • Malignant hypertension, infarction, right heart failure
  • Renal cell carcinoma, tumours of renal pelvis
  • Polycystic kidney, solitary cyst
  • Trauma

B. Post-renal causes:

  1. Trauma to any part of urinary tract
  2. Impaction of stone in ureter or urethra
  3. Inflammation, e.g. cystitis, urethritis, etc.
  4. Neoplasm of ureter, urinary bladder and urethra

C. Pre-renal causes (systemic diseases) — blood intimately mixed with urine:

  • Haemorrhagic disorders e.g. haemophilia, thrombocytopenia, DIC (disseminated intravascular coagulation)
  • Sickle cell anaemia
  • Septicaemia, particularly meningococcal
  • Scurvy
  • Overdose of anticoagulant therapy

Investigation of a case of haematuria

1. Urine:

  • Microscopic examination of urine for RBC & RBC cast
  • Chemical examination of urine for Hb: Orthotolidine test · Benzidine test

2. Blood (routine): Hb% — reduced (due to blood loss) · ESR — increased · BT (bleeding time) & CT (clotting time) — may be increased if there is thrombocytopenia or haemophilia · WBC count — neutrophilic leukocytosis.

3. Special blood tests: Serum urea level · Serum creatinine level.

4. Imaging: IVU (intravenous urogram) · Plain X-ray of KUB (kidney–ureter–bladder) region.

  • WBC (suggestive of infection)
  • RBC — dysmorphic erythrocytes (phase contrast microscopy) (suggestive of glomerular bleeding)
  • RBC casts (suggestive of glomerular bleeding)
  • Abnormal epithelial cells (suggestive of tumour)

Nice to Know: Haematuria may be

Microscopic haematuria (DU-12Ju)

Management: depends upon the cause.

Nephrotic syndrome

It is a glomerular syndrome characterized by massive proteinuria (> 3.5 gm/day), hypoalbuminaemia (plasma albumin < 3 gm/dl), generalized oedema, hyperlipidaemia and lipiduria (lipid in the urine).

Ref: Robbins 10e pp.896,912

Causes of nephrotic syndrome (Very Important for VIVA)

1. Primary glomerular diseases:

  • Minimal-change disease (most common in children)
  • Membranous glomerulonephritis (most common in adults)
  • Membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis
  • Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis
  • IgA nephropathy

2. Systemic diseases:

  • Diabetes mellitus
  • Amyloidosis
  • Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)
  • Drugs (NSAIDs = non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, penicillamine, heroin)
  • Infections (malaria, syphilis, hepatitis B & C, HIV = human immunodeficiency virus)
  • Malignancy (carcinoma, lymphoma, myeloma)
  • Miscellaneous (bee sting allergy, hereditary nephritis)

Ref: Robbins 10e p.912; Harsh Mohan 7e p.648

Nephritic syndrome / AGN

Nephritic syndrome or acute glomerulonephritis (AGN) is a glomerular disease of the kidney characterized by inflammation in the glomeruli, manifested as haematuria (red blood cells and red cell casts in urine), proteinuria (usually subnephrotic range) with or without oedema, azotemia and hypertension.

Causes of nephritic syndrome (AGN) (Very Important for VIVA)

A. PRIMARY GLOMERULAR DISEASES

I. Acute proliferative glomerulonephritis:

  • Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (Group A β-haemolytic streptococcus)
  • Non-streptococcal acute glomerulonephritis (post-infectious):
    • Bacterial: staphylococcal endocarditis, pneumococcal pneumonia, meningococcaemia
    • Viral: hepatitis B & C virus, mumps, HIV, varicella, infectious mononucleosis
    • Parasitic: malaria, toxoplasmosis

II. Rapidly progressive (crescentic) glomerulonephritis (RPGN)

III. Membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis

IV. IgA nephropathy

B. SYSTEMIC DISEASES

  • Goodpasture syndrome (pulmonary haemorrhage associated with renal failure)
  • SLE (lupus nephritis)
  • Polyarteritis nodosa
  • Wegener granulomatosis (granulomatosis with polyangiitis)
  • Microscopic polyangiitis
  • Henoch–Schönlein purpura
  • Cryoglobulinaemia

Clinical features: (i) patient is usually a child of 5–10 years of age · (ii) haematuria (brown urine) · (iii) oedema and generalized fluid retention · (iv) hypertension · (v) oliguria · (vi) evidence of sore throat or skin infection. (Ref: Davidson's 23e p.392)

Anuria

Anuria is the (almost) total absence of urine (< 100 ml/day). Ref: Davidson's 23e p.391; Lecture SSMC

Causes of anuria:

1. Pre-renal causes: Traumatic shock · Severe haemorrhage · Extensive burns · Dehydration from vomiting, diarrhoea, etc. · Spinal anaesthesia.

When urinary output is less than 400 ml/day, it is called oliguria.

Urinary tract infection (UTI)

UTI is defined as the presence of > 10⁵ organisms/ml urine in the urinary tract in a mid-stream sample of urine (MSU).

Causes of UTI

  • Proteus
  • Staphylococcus saprophyticus or Staph. epidermidis
  • Enterococcus faecalis
  • Pseudomonas species
  • Klebsiella spp.

  4. Blood urea, serum electrolytes, serum creatinine
