# Ch 16 — The Central Nervous System     <i>Full-detail flashcards (Q→A) · All abbreviations expanded · Robbins 4-pillar answers</i>
### 🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of the Central Nervous System (boss-level overview)
The CNS chapter in Arif covers <b>six major disease groups</b>: <b>(1) Infective conditions</b> — meningitis (pyogenic, viral, tuberculous), meningoencephalitis (viral, fungal, parasitic), and focal suppurative lesions (brain abscess, subdural empyema, extradural abscess); <b>(2) Demyelinating diseases</b> — multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica, acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, central pontine myelinolysis; <b>(3) Degenerative diseases</b> — prion diseases (CJD), Alzheimer disease (AD), Parkinson disease (PD), Huntington disease (HD), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS); <b>(4) Intracranial haemorrhage</b> — traumatic and non-traumatic (hypertension, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, vascular malformations, aneurysms); <b>(5) Tumours of the CNS</b> — gliomas (astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma, ependymoma), meningioma, peripheral nerve sheath tumours (schwannoma, neurofibroma, MPNST), metastatic tumours; and <b>(6) Special characteristics of CNS tumours</b> — infiltrative growth, rare extracranial metastasis, location-dependent lethality.

## 2 · Meningitis & Meningoencephalitis
<b>🔴 Define meningitis and meningoencephalitis. How are they classified?</b>

<b>Meningitis:</b> an inflammatory process of the <b>leptomeninges</b> (pia mater + arachnoid mater) and the <b>cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)</b> within the subarachnoid space, usually caused by an infection. May also occur in response to a non-bacterial irritant (chemical meningitis).

<b>Classification of infectious meningitis (by etiology & clinical evolution):</b>

<table>
<tr><th>Type</th><th>Common Cause</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Acute pyogenic</strong> (suppurative)</td><td>Usually bacterial</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Aseptic</strong> (non-purulent)</td><td>Usually acute or subacute viral</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Chronic</strong></td><td>Tuberculous, spirochetal, or cryptococcal</td></tr>
</table>

<b>Non-infectious causes of meningitis:</b> chemical meningitis (non-bacterial irritant introduced into subarachnoid space), systemic autoimmune disease.

<b>Meningoencephalitis:</b> inflammation of <b>both the meninges AND the brain parenchyma</b> (meninges + encephalon = meningoencephalitis).

Robbins 10e p.1261

## 3 · Demyelinating Diseases of the CNS
<b>🔴 What are the demyelinating diseases of the CNS? (Important for VIVA)</b>

<b>Demyelinating diseases of the CNS:</b> acquired conditions characterised by <b>preferential damage to myelin with relative preservation of axons</b>.

Multiple sclerosis (MS) — the most common demyelinating disease; chronic-relapsing; plaques in periventricular/cerebellar/spinal cord white matter.
Neuromyelitis optica (Devic disease) — selective demyelination of optic nerves and spinal cord.
Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM) and acute necrotising haemorrhagic encephalomyelitis — post-infectious/post-vaccination; widespread perivenous demyelination.
Central pontine myelinolysis (osmotic demyelination syndrome) — rapid correction of hyponatraemia → demyelination of pontine basis.

Robbins 10e p.1269

## 4 · Degenerative Diseases of the CNS
<b>🔴 What are the degenerative (neurodegenerative) diseases of the CNS?</b>

<b>Neurodegenerative diseases:</b> disorders characterised by <b>progressive loss of particular groups of neurons</b> that often share functions. The common pathological process = accumulation of <b>protein aggregates</b> (hence also called "proteinopathies").

## 6 · Tumours of the CNS — Classification
<b>🔴 Classify tumours of the CNS. (RMU-21/19/17M, CU-10Ja, CMU-24/23M, RMU-24M)</b>

<b>Primary CNS (intracranial) tumours</b> may originate in the brain or spinal cord, or spread from another primary site (metastatic). Primary CNS tumours include tumours arising from constituent cells of the brain (with the sole exception of microglial cells) and from supporting tissues.

<b>Nice to Know: Where are metastatic tumour deposits in the brain commonly located?</b>

Metastatic tumour deposits in the brain are commonly <b>multiple</b>, <b>well-defined</b>, and usually located at the <b>grey-white matter junction</b> (watershed zone where blood supply is most vulnerable to microemboli).

Harsh Mohan 7e p.884

<b>🔴 What are the special characteristics of CNS tumours / brain tumours? (Very Important for VIVA — CMU-21Nv, 20Nv/M, 19/18/17M, 14Ju, 10Ja, BUP-22M; DU-23Nv, CMU-23Nv, CU-18Nv)</b>

<b>Differences between tumours of the nervous system and tumours of other parts of the body — four unique characteristics:</b>

Unique clinical course: some glial tumours with benign histologic features (low mitotic rate, cellular uniformity, slow growth) may still infiltrate large regions of the brain and lead to serious clinical deficits and poor prognosis.
Infiltrative behaviour → incomplete resection: because of their infiltrative growth, it is often not feasible to resect glial neoplasms completely without compromising neurologic function.
Location-dependent lethality: any neoplasm — even a benign meningioma — can have lethal consequences if located in a critical region (e.g., compressing the medulla → cardiorespiratory arrest).
Rare extracranial metastasis: even the most highly malignant gliomas rarely metastasise outside the CNS.

Robbins 10e pp.1293, 8th ed. p.1330

## 7 · Glioma — Astrocytoma, Oligodendroglioma, Ependymoma
<b>🔴 What is a glioma? Mention the types of astrocytoma. (CU-19Nv, CMU-24M)</b>

<b>Glioma:</b> all tumours arising from <b>neuroglia</b> (or more precisely, from neuroectodermal epithelial tissue). Gliomas are the <b>most common primary CNS tumours</b>, collectively accounting for <b>40% of all intracranial tumours</b>.

They include tumours arising from:

Astrocytes — astrocytomas and glioblastoma multiforme (GBM)
Oligodendrocytes — oligodendroglioma
Ependymal cells — ependymoma
Choroid plexus — choroid plexus papilloma

Gliomas spread to other parts of the CNS by <b>CSF dissemination</b> but rarely ever metastasise beyond the CNS.

Astrocytoma — the most common primary brain tumour

Found predominantly in late middle life (peak in 6th decade); mainly in cerebral hemispheres; in children/young adults → optic nerves, cerebellum, brainstem. Astrocytomas have a <b>tendency to progress from low grade to higher grades of anaplasia</b>.

Classification by infiltration pattern

<tr><th>Type</th><th>Subtypes</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>A. Infiltrating astrocytomas</strong></td><td>Diffuse astrocytoma, Anaplastic astrocytoma, Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM)</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>B. Non-infiltrating astrocytomas</strong></td><td>Pilocytic astrocytoma, Pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma, Brainstem glioma</td></tr>
</table>

Robbins 10e p.1293; BRS Pathology 5th ed. p.383; Harsh Mohan 7e p.879

<b>🔴 Describe the WHO grading of astrocytomas — Grades I to IV with morphology.</b>

WHO Grade I — Pilocytic Astrocytoma (Juvenile Pilocytic Astrocytoma)

Gross: usually cystic or solid and circumscribed.
Microscopy: predominantly composed of fusiform pilocytic astrocytes having unusually long, wavy fibrillary processes.

Also: <b>Pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma</b> — histologically pleomorphic and alarming but has favourable prognosis.

WHO Grade II — Well-Differentiated (Fibrillary) Astrocytoma

Gross: poorly defined, grey-white tumour of variable size; distorts underlying brain tissue and merges with surrounding tissue.
Microscopy: well-differentiated astrocytes separated by variable amounts of fibrillary background of astrocytic processes.

WHO Grade III — Anaplastic Astrocytoma

Gross: may not be distinguishable from low-grade astrocytoma.
Microscopy: features of anaplasia — hypercellularity, pleomorphism, nuclear hyperchromatism, mitoses. Another characteristic feature = proliferation of vascular endothelium.

WHO Grade IV — Glioblastoma Multiforme (GBM)

<b>Most aggressive of all astrocytomas.</b>

Gross: variegated appearance — some areas grey-white, others yellow and soft with foci of haemorrhages and necrosis. Surrounding normal brain distorted and infiltrated by yellow tumour tissue.
Microscopy:
Highly anaplastic and cellular; cell types show marked variation: fusiform cells, small poorly-differentiated round cells, pleomorphic cells, giant cells.
Frequent mitoses; scanty glial fibrils.
Areas of tumour necrosis around which tumour cells form pseudopalisading.
Microvascular endothelial proliferation is marked.

Harsh Mohan 7e p.879; Robbins 10e p.1293

<b>Short note: Oligodendroglioma.</b>

<b>Oligodendroglioma:</b> malignant tumour of <b>oligodendrocytes</b>; presents as a slow-growing tumour in the <b>middle-age group</b>, often involving the <b>frontal lobe</b> of the cerebral hemisphere. May present as seizures.

Gross morphology

Infiltrative tumours that form gelatinous, grey masses; may show cysts, focal haemorrhage, and calcification.

Microscopic morphology

"Fried egg" appearance: closely packed cells with large round nuclei surrounded by a clear halo of cytoplasm.
Tumour divided into groups of cells by delicate capillary strands.
Foci of calcification present in 90% of cases.

Robbins 10e p.1295; BRS Pathology 5th ed. p.384; Pathoma

<b>Short note: Ependymoma.</b>

<b>Ependymoma:</b> malignant tumour of <b>ependymal cells</b>, usually seen in the <b>4th ventricle</b>.

Key features

Perivascular pseudorosette is characteristic (pseudorosette because the central structure isn't part of the tumour; originally, pseudorosette meant any rosette that didn't have a truly empty lumen).
Tumour can result in hydrocephalus (obstruction of CSF flow by 4th ventricle mass).

Robbins 10e p.1293; BRS Pathology 5th ed. p.383; Pathoma

## 8 · Meningioma
<b>🔴 Short note: Meningioma. (DU-07Ja, SU-12Ju, CU-12Ja)</b>

<b>Meningioma:</b> the <b>second most common primary intracranial neoplasm</b>. Most cases are <b>benign and slow-growing</b>. Originates from <b>arachnoidal cells</b> of the meninges; is external to the brain and can often be successfully removed surgically. Can cause seizure by compression.

Uncommon in children; most often occurs <b>after 30 years of age</b>; shows moderate (<b>3:2</b>) <b>female predominance</b>.

Molecular genetics

Many show deletion, loss, or mutation in the <b>NF2 locus</b> (neurofibromatosis type 2 gene on chromosome 22).

Common sites of involvement

Convexities of the cerebral hemispheres
Dura over the lateral convexity
Wing of the sphenoid
Olfactory groove
Sella turcica
Foramen magnum

Morphology

<b>Gross:</b> usually rounded masses with well-defined dural bases that compress underlying brain but are <b>easily separated from it</b>. Extension into the overlying bone may be present. Usually encapsulated with thin, fibrous tissue.

<b>Microscopy:</b>

Whorled pattern of concentrically arranged spindle cells.
Laminated calcified Psammoma bodies may be present.

Robbins 10e p.1300; BRS Pathology 5th ed. p.384

## 9 · Peripheral Nerve Sheath Tumours (PNSTs)
<b>🔴 Short note: Peripheral nerve sheath tumours. (CU-16Ju)</b>

<b>Peripheral nerve sheath tumours (PNSTs):</b> a group of benign and malignant neoplasms — all likely arise from cells of <b>Schwann cell lineage</b>.

Three common PNSTs

Schwannoma: benign, slowly growing, encapsulated tumour arising from Schwann cells.
Neurofibroma: solitary or multiple tumours of peripheral nerves derived from Schwann cells; subtypes: localised cutaneous, diffuse, or plexiform.
Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumour (MPNST): most (≈85%) are high-grade; low-grade variants recognised. About half arise in NF1 patients and are assumed to result from malignant transformation of a plexiform neurofibroma.

<b>Association:</b> PNSTs have association with relatively common familial tumour syndromes: <b>NF1, NF2, and schwannomatosis</b>.

Robbins 10e p.1236; BRS Pathology 5th ed. p.385

<b>Short note: Schwannoma. (CMU-23M, 13Ju)</b>

<b>Schwannoma:</b> a <b>benign tumour</b> exhibiting Schwann cell differentiation; often arises directly from peripheral nerves. Component of <b>neurofibromatosis (NF)</b>.

Morphology

Gross: well-circumscribed, encapsulated masses that abut the associated nerve without invading it — a feature that simplifies surgical excision. Firm, grey masses.
Microscopy: admixture of dense and loose areas — Antoni-A areas (dense, organised) and Antoni-B areas (loose, myxoid).

Robbins 10e p.1237

<b>Write in brief about neurofibroma. (CU-20/13Ju, CMU-21M/20Nv)</b>

<b>Neurofibroma:</b> a <b>benign nerve sheath tumour</b> in the peripheral nervous system.

<b>Origin:</b> arises from <b>non-myelinating-type Schwann cells</b>. The neoplastic Schwann cells are admixed with perineurial-like cells, fibroblasts, mast cells, and CD34+ spindle cells. May be sporadic or NF1-associated.

Subtypes (by growth pattern)

Superficial cutaneous neurofibromas: often present as pedunculated nodules; seen isolated (if sporadic) or multiple (if NF1-associated).
Diffuse neurofibromas: present as a large plaque-like elevation of skin; typically NF1-associated.
Plexiform neurofibromas: found in deep or superficial locations in association with nerve roots or large nerves; uniformly NF1-associated. Risk of malignant transformation to MPNST.

Aetiology

NF1 gene (neurofibromatosis type 1) — tumour suppressor gene.
Schwann cells: non-myelinating Schwann cells are the neoplastic element.
Loss of heterozygosity leads to loss of tumour suppressor function → neurofibroma formation.

Robbins 10e p.1237
