🧠 Big Picture — The Breast (boss-level overview)

Master the triple classification ladder: (1) Diseases of the breast (congenital → inflammatory → benign epithelial → tumours) · (2) Tumour classification (benign vs malignant; malignant = non-invasive/in situ vs invasive) · (3) Breast carcinoma subclassification (WHO invasive types + molecular subtypes: Luminal A/B, HER2-enriched, Basal-like/triple-negative).

The clinical sequence a student must own: Inflammatory breast conditions (acute mastitis, Zuska disease, duct ectasia, fat necrosis, granulomatous mastitis) → Fibrocystic change (non-proliferative: cyst/fibrosis/adenosis) → Benign tumours (fibroadenoma = most common; phyllodes tumour; intraductal papilloma) → Carcinoma in situ (DCIS ~80%, LCIS ~20%) → Invasive carcinoma (IDC ~80%, ILC ~10%, + special types) → special presentations (Paget disease of nipple, inflammatory carcinoma) → Diagnosis (triple technique = palpation + mammography + FNAC) → Risk factors (hormonal + genetic) → Prognosis (ER/PR/HER2 + molecular subtype).

🎯 Mnemonics: Fibroadenoma = "the mobile, rubbery, well-circumscribed lump of young women (15–30)" · IDC = "hard, irregular, grating-sounding, chalky-streaked mass" · Luminal A = "ER+PR+HER2−" → good prognosis & endocrine-responsive · Basal-like = "ER−PR−HER2− (triple negative)" → worst prognosis · Gynaecomastia = "oestrogen excess / androgen failure".

1 · Diseases of the Breast (Classification)

🔴 Name the diseases of the breast. What are the inflammatory conditions of the breast? Classify tumours / breast carcinoma of the breast.

Diseases of the breast — four big groups

1. Congenital anomalies:

  • Milk-line remnants (supernumerary nipples or breasts) — most common congenital anomaly
  • Accessory axillary breast tissue
  • Congenital nipple inversion
  • Amastia (absence of the breast — occurs in both sexes; may be unilateral or bilateral)

2. Inflammatory disorders:

  • Acute mastitis
  • Squamous metaplasia of lactiferous ducts (also called recurrent subareolar abscess / periductal mastitis / Zuska disease)
  • Duct ectasia
  • Fat necrosis
  • Lymphocytic mastopathy (sclerosing lymphocytic lobulitis)
  • Granulomatous mastitis (e.g., granulomatosis with polyangiitis, sarcoidosis, tuberculosis)

3. Benign epithelial lesions:

  • Non-proliferative breast changes (fibrocystic changes): cyst, fibrosis, adenosis
  • Proliferative breast disease without atypia: epithelial hyperplasia, sclerosing adenosis, complex sclerosing lesion, papillomas, gynaecomastia (enlargement of the male breast)
  • Proliferative breast disease with atypia: atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH), atypical lobular hyperplasia (ALH)

4. Tumours of breast (see next flashcard).

2 · Classification of Breast Tumours

a. Stromal tumours:

  • Fibroadenoma — most common benign tumour
  • Phyllodes tumour (cystosarcoma phyllodes)

  • Lipomas

3 · Inflammatory Disorders of the Breast (Nice to Know)

🔴 Nice to Know — describe each inflammatory disorder of the breast: acute mastitis, squamous metaplasia of lactiferous ducts (Zuska disease), duct ectasia, fat necrosis, lymphocytic mastopathy, granulomatous mastitis.

1. Acute mastitis

Acute bacterial mastitis typically occurs during the first month of breastfeeding and is caused by local infection by Staphylococcus aureus or, less commonly, streptococci. The breast is erythematous and painful, and fever is often present.

2. Squamous metaplasia of lactiferous ducts (recurrent subareolar abscess / periductal mastitis / Zuska disease)

Women, and sometimes men, present with a painful erythematous subareolar mass that mimics a bacterial abscess. More than 90% of afflicted individuals are smokers.

3. Duct ectasia

Presents as a palpable periareolar mass often associated with thick, white nipple secretions and occasionally with skin retraction. Pain and erythema are uncommon and NOT associated with cigarette smoking.

4. Fat necrosis

The presentations are protean (many and varied) and may closely mimic cancer. May present as a painless palpable mass, skin thickening or retraction, or mammographic densities or calcifications. 📌 About half of affected women have a history of breast trauma or surgery.

5. Lymphocytic mastopathy (sclerosing lymphocytic lobulitis)

Presents with single or multiple hard palpable masses or mammographic densities. The masses are associated with areas of densely collagenized stroma (making needle biopsy difficult). Within the stroma are atrophic ducts and lobules with thickened basement membranes surrounded by a prominent lymphocytic infiltrate.

6. Granulomatous mastitis

Granulomatous inflammation of the breast may be a manifestation of systemic granulomatous diseases (e.g., granulomatosis with polyangiitis, sarcoidosis, tuberculosis) or of inflammatory/infectious disorders localised to the breast.

4 · Morphology of Non-Proliferative (Fibrocystic) Changes

Mention the morphology of non-proliferative breast changes (fibrocystic changes).

There are three principal morphologic changes: (a) cystic change, often with apocrine metaplasia; (b) fibrosis; and (c) adenosis.

1. Cysts

Cysts are lined either by a flattened atrophic epithelium or by metaplastic apocrine cells. The metaplastic cells have an abundant granular, eosinophilic cytoplasm and round nuclei, resembling the normal apocrine epithelium of sweat glands. Calcifications are common.

2. Fibrosis

Cysts frequently rupture, releasing secretory material into the adjacent stroma. The resulting chronic inflammation and fibrosis contribute to palpable nodularity of the breast.

3. Adenosis

Increase in the number of acini per lobule. It is a normal feature of pregnancy. In nonpregnant women, adenosis can occur as a focal change. The acini are lined by columnar epithelial cells, and calcifications are occasionally present within the lumens.

Robbins 10e p.1042

5 · Fibroadenoma of the Breast

🔴 Short note: Fibroadenoma of the breast. Give its morphology (gross + microscopic features). 🎯 Most common benign tumour of the female breast

Fibroadenoma (adenofibroma): the most common benign tumour of the female breast.

Age: most patients are between 15 to 30 years. Number: frequently multiple and bilateral.

Clinical features

Young women usually present with a palpable mass; older women with a mammographic density or clustered calcifications. The lesion is firm, rubbery, painless, well-circumscribed and freely mobile.

Morphology — Gross

  • Well-circumscribed, rubbery, freely movable nodules, usually in the upper-outer quadrant of the breast.
  • Size varies from <1 cm to large tumours that replace most of the breast.
  • Cut surface: firm, grayish-white and often contain slit-like spaces (formed by compressed ducts) lined by epithelium.
  • Clearly demarcated from the surrounding yellow adipose tissue.

Morphology — Histological

  • The stroma is usually delicate, cellular and often myxoid, resembling normal intralobular stroma.
  • The arrangement between fibrous overgrowth and ducts produces two patterns:
    • a. Intracanalicular pattern: stroma compresses and distorts ducts into slit-like spaces.
    • b. Pericanalicular pattern: encircling masses of fibrous stroma around the patent or dilated ducts.
  • In older women, the stroma typically becomes densely hyalinized and the epithelium atrophic.
🎯 Fibroadenoma = "young (15–30), mobile, rubbery, bilateral & multiple." Intracanalicular = compressed slit-like ducts; Pericanalicular = patent ducts ringed by stroma.

Robbins 10e p.1062 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.748 · BRS Pathology 5e p.311

6 · Invasive (Ductal) Carcinoma — Morphology

🔴 Give the gross and microscopic features of invasive ductal carcinoma (duct cell / infiltrating duct carcinoma) of the breast.

Invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) / infiltrating duct carcinoma-NOS (no other special type): the most common (70–80%) breast cancer, found more frequently in the left breast, often in the upper-outer quadrant. Its precursor lesion is ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS).

Gross

  • Tumour is irregular, at least 2 to 3 cm in size, a hard, cartilage-like mass.
  • Cut surface: gray-white to yellowish with chalky streaks and often extends irregularly into the surrounding fat.
  • There is a characteristic grating sound (similar to cutting a water chestnut) when cut or scraped.
  • Less frequently, carcinomas have a well-circumscribed border and a softer consistency.

Microscopic

  • Anaplastic tumour cells forming solid nests, cords, poorly-formed glandular structures and some intraductal foci.
  • Infiltration by these patterns of tumour cells into diffuse fibrous stroma and fat.
  • Invasion into perivascular and perineural spaces as well as lymphatic and vascular invasion.

Fig description: Infiltrating duct carcinoma-NOS — microscopic features include formation of solid nests, cords, gland-like structures and intraductal growth pattern of anaplastic tumour cells; there is infiltration of densely collagenised stroma by these cells in a haphazard manner.

🎯 IDC = "hard, irregular, grating, chalky-streaked mass; left breast & upper-outer quadrant; solid nests + cords + poorly-formed glands invading fibrous stroma."

Robbins 10e p.1055 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.752

7 · Paget Disease of the Nipple

🔴 Short note: Paget's disease of the nipple. (Important for VIVA)

Paget disease of the nipple: a rare manifestation of breast cancer (1% to 4% of cases) presenting as a unilateral erythematous eruption with a scale crust. Pruritus (itching) is common, and the lesion may be mistaken for eczema.

The nipple bears a crusted, scaly and eczematoid lesion with a palpable subareolar mass in about 50–60% of cases.

Underlying carcinoma association

Paget disease is often associated with an invasive or non-invasive ductal carcinoma of the underlying breast. Most patients with a palpable mass are found to have invasive (infiltrating) duct carcinoma, while those without a palpable breast lump are usually subsequently found to have intraductal carcinoma (DCIS).

Pathogenesis

Malignant cells (Paget cells) extend from the underlying ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) within the ductal system via the lactiferous sinuses into nipple skin without crossing the basement membrane. The malignant cells disrupt the normally tight squamous epithelial cell barrier, allowing extracellular fluid to seep out onto the nipple surface and form an oozing, scaly crust.

Morphology — Gross

  • The skin of the nipple and areola is crusted, fissured and ulcerated.
  • Have oozing, hyperaemia and oedema.

Morphology — Microscopic

  • Presence of Paget cells (malignant cells) singly or in small clusters in the epidermis.
  • Paget cells are larger than the epidermal cells, anaplastic, hyperchromatic, and usually surrounded by a clear zone or halo.

Diagnosis

  • Nipple biopsy
  • Cytologic preparation of exudate
🎯 Paget = "eczematoid nipple + large anaplastic Paget cells with a clear halo; ARISES from underlying DCIS that migrates WITHOUT crossing the basement membrane."

Robbins 10e p.1053 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.755 · BRS Pathology 5e p.312 · Prof. Khaleque p.134

8 · Inflammatory Carcinoma & Peau d'Orange

🔴 What do you mean by inflammatory carcinoma of the breast? What is peau d'orange? (Important for VIVA)

Inflammatory breast cancer

The term is used for breast cancers in which there is redness, oedema, tenderness and rapid enlargement.

  • Carcinoma of dermal lymphatics.
  • Poor prognosis, as most patients prove to have distant metastases (tumour already in lymph).
  • Presents as breast erythema, swelling, and skin thickening.
  • The oedematous skin is tethered to the breast by Cooper ligaments and mimics the surface of an orange peel — appearance referred to as peau d'orange.

Peau d'orange

In invasive (infiltrating) carcinoma, lymphatics may become so involved as to block the local area of skin drainage & cause lymphoedema & thickening of the skin. In such cases the oedematous skin is tethered to the breast by Cooper ligaments and mimics the surface of an orange peel — an appearance referred to as peau d'orange.

🎯 Peau d'orange = "blocked superficial lymphatics → lymphoedema + skin tethered by Cooper ligaments → orange-peel skin." Inflammatory carcinoma = poor prognosis (dermal lymphatic involvement).

Robbins 10e p.1058 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.755 · Pathoma Lecture notes

9 · Causes & Clinical Presentation of a Breast Lump

Enumerate the causes of a breast lump (differential diagnosis in a female — 25 / 38 / 20-year-old presentations). Give the clinical presentation of breast cancer.

Causes of a breast lump

  1. Fibrocystic disease of the breast
  2. Fibroadenoma of the breast
  3. Mastitis (acute & chronic)
  4. Breast abscess
  5. Fibroadenosis
  6. Traumatic fat necrosis
  7. Haematoma
  8. Lipoma
  9. Carcinoma of the breast (in situ and invasive)
  10. Lymphoma involving the breast

Clinical presentation of breast cancer (carcinoma of the breast)

  • (a) Presence of a painless hard lump.
  • (b) Breast becomes fixed and immobile, due to infiltration of suspensory ligaments.
  • (c) Retraction of skin, due to infiltration of suspensory ligaments.
  • (d) Retraction of nipple, due to infiltration and fibrosis of lactiferous ducts.
  • (e) Peau d'orange appearance of the skin (skin giving an appearance like that of the skin of an orange) due to obstruction of superficial lymphatics.

Robbins 10e p.1046, 1058

🔴 How will you proceed to diagnose a case of breast lump in a female (in the pathology laboratory)? Short note: Breast lump and diagnosis.

A. History taking

  1. Age
  2. Mode of presentation:
    • Mode of onset
    • Duration
    • Rate of growth
    • Pain (usually painless)
    • Discharge from nipple
    • Loss of weight
  3. Personal history
  4. Family history of breast and ovarian cancer

B. Examination

  1. General examination
  2. Local examination
    • a. Inspection:
      • Skin over the lump — colour & texture
      • Engorged vein
      • Nodules
      • Ulceration
      • Nipple — size & shape, surface, any discharge
    • b. Palpation:
      • Local temperature & tenderness
      • Situation
      • Number
      • Size & shape
      • Surface
      • Margin
      • Consistency
      • Fixity to the skin
      • Fixity to the breast tissue
      • Fixity to the underlying fascia and muscle
      • Fixity to the chest wall
      • Examination of lymph nodes

C. Investigations

  1. Mammography
  2. Ultrasonography
  3. MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) scan — for high-risk patients
  4. FNAC (fine needle aspiration cytology)
  5. Biopsy (image-guided core needle biopsy / excisional biopsy) followed by histopathology
  6. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for ER (oestrogen receptor), PR (progesterone receptor) & HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2) status
  7. Cytological examination of nipple discharge

10 · Risk Factors for Breast Cancer

🔴 Write down the risk factors involved in breast cancer. (V.V.I for VIVA & MCQ)
  1. Gender: 99% of affected patients are female.
  2. Age: incidence increases with increasing age (peaking at 70–80 years); rarely found before age 25 except in some familial cases.
  3. Germline mutations: inherited mutations in the p53, BRCA-1 (breast cancer susceptibility gene 1), or BRCA-2 (breast cancer susceptibility gene 2) tumour suppressor genes are associated with increased risk.
  4. First-degree relatives with breast cancer: first-degree relatives (mother, sister, daughter) of women with breast cancer have a 2- to 6-fold higher risk.
  5. Geographic and racial factors: incidence about 4–6 times higher in developed countries (North America, North Europe, Australia), low in developing countries of Asia and Africa.
  6. Early menarche and late menopause (↑ oestrogen exposure): menarche before age 11 ↑ risk by 20% compared to menarche after 14; late menopause also ↑ risk.
  7. Age at first pregnancy: nulliparity & first pregnancy after 30 ↑ risk.
  8. Benign breast disease: proliferative fibrocystic disease with atypical epithelial hyperplasia ↑ risk of invasive carcinoma.
  9. Oestrogen exposure: postmenopausal hormone replacement therapy (HRT) ↑ risk, particularly when oestrogen and a progestin are given together for years. Oral contraceptives are unlikely to increase risk.
  10. High breast density: potentially susceptible to neoplastic transformation; dense breasts also make detection more difficult by mammography.
  11. Radiation exposure: radiation of the chest (cancer therapy, atomic bomb exposure, nuclear accidents) → higher rate of breast cancer.
  12. History of breast cancer in one breast: associated with increased incidence in the opposite breast.
  13. Diet: large amounts of animal fats, high-calorie foods; cigarette smoking; alcohol consumption ↑ risk.
  14. Obesity: possibly due to production of oestrogens by adipose tissue.
  15. Exercise: a small protective effect for physically active women.
  16. Breast-feeding: the longer women breast-feed, the greater the reduction in risk.
  17. Environmental toxins: contaminants such as organochlorine pesticides could have oestrogenic effects in humans.
🎯 N.B. The MAJOR risk factors for breast cancer are HORMONAL and GENETIC.
🎯 Protective factors = breastfeeding, exercise; HRT/obesity/radiation/family history = risk-increasing.

Robbins 10e p.1046 · BRS Pathology 5e p.312 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.750

11 · Prognostic Factors of Breast Carcinoma

🔴 Outline the prognostic factors involved in breast carcinoma.

A. Major prognostic factors

  1. Invasive carcinoma vs in situ disease:
    • In situ carcinoma is confined to the ductal system and cannot metastasize — the great majority of DCIS will be cured with adequate treatment.
    • In contrast, at least half of invasive carcinomas will have metastasized locally or distantly at the time of diagnosis → poor prognosis.
  2. Distant metastases: once present, cure is unlikely.
  3. Lymph node metastases: axillary lymph node status is the MOST IMPORTANT prognostic factor for invasive carcinoma in the absence of distant metastasis. The less the lymph node metastasis, the more the chance of good prognosis.
  4. Tumour size: the second most important prognostic factor, independent of lymph node status. Bigger size → worse prognosis.
  5. Locally advanced disease: carcinomas invading skin or skeletal muscle are usually large and may be difficult to treat surgically.
  6. Inflammatory carcinoma: breast cancer presenting with breast swelling and skin thickening due to dermal lymphatic involvement has a particularly poor prognosis.
  7. Lymphovascular invasion: tumour cells within vascular spaces (lymphatics or small capillaries) in about half of all invasive carcinomas. Strongly associated with lymph-node metastases; a poor prognostic factor for overall survival in women without lymph-node metastases, and a risk factor for local recurrence.

B. Minor prognostic factors

  1. Molecular subtype: determined by expression of ER (oestrogen receptor) and HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2) and proliferation — an important prognostic factor.
  2. Special histologic types: survival rate with some special invasive carcinomas (tubular, mucinous, lobular, papillary, adenoid cystic) is greater than with cancers of no special type.
  3. Histologic grade: all invasive carcinomas graded using the Nottingham Histologic Score → grade I, II, III (highly correlated with disease-free and overall survival).
  4. Proliferative rate: high proliferation → poorer prognosis but may respond better to chemotherapy.
  5. Estrogen and progesterone receptors (ER/PR): oestrogen promotes breast cancer; presence/absence of hormone receptors helps predict response to endocrine therapy. Patients with high levels of ER and PR have a slightly better prognosis.
  6. HER2/neu overexpression: associated with poorer survival, but its main importance is as a predictor of response to targeted therapy (agents targeting this receptor).
  7. DNA content: aneuploid tumours (abnormal DNA indices) have a slightly worse prognosis.
  8. Response to neoadjuvant therapy: subgroups achieving a pathologic complete response have a >95% long-term survival.
🎯 Most important = axillary LN status; second = tumour size. Poor-prognosis flags = distant metastasis, inflammatory carcinoma, lymphovascular invasion, HER2+.

Robbins 10e p.1058 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.755

🔴 Discuss the role of ER, PR & HER2 in the prognosis of breast carcinoma. (V.V.I for VIVA)

ER / PR (oestrogen / progesterone receptors): tumours that express both ER & PR (or only ER or PR) have a better prognosis, because they respond well to endocrine therapy.

HER2 / neu (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2): presence of HER2/neu is associated with a poorer prognosis, because tumours that overexpress HER2 tend to metastasize earlier.

13 · Short Note on Breast Cancer

Short note: Breast cancer.

Breast cancer: carcinoma of the breast is the most common non-skin malignancy in women and is second only to lung cancer as a cause of cancer deaths. A woman who lives to age 90 has a one in eight chance of developing breast cancer.

  • Site of involvement: occurs most frequently in the upper outer quadrant of the breast. Occurs more often in the left breast than the right, and is bilateral in about 4% of cases.
  • Sites of metastasis: mainly axillary lymph nodes, lung, liver, and bone.
  • Risk factors: pleas write from above — N.B. The major risk factors are hormonal and genetic.
  • Classification of breast cancer: write from above.

14 · The Male Breast — Gynaecomastia

🔴 What do you mean by gynaecomastia? What are its causes? Short note: Gynaecomastia. (Important for VIVA)

Gynaecomastia: enlargement of the male breast. It may be unilateral or bilateral and presents as a button-like subareolar enlargement. In advanced cases, the swelling can simulate the adolescent female breast.

Pathogenesis

Gynaecomastia occurs as a result of an imbalance between oestrogens (which stimulate breast tissue) and androgens (which counteract these effects).

Causes

  1. Pubertal gynaecomastia (young boys between 13 and 17 years)
  2. Failure of the androgenic functions of the testis (in older males)
  3. Cirrhosis of liver
  4. Testicular tumours
  5. Pituitary tumours
  6. Carcinoma of the lung
  7. Exogenous oestrogen therapy, as in carcinoma of the prostate
  8. Testicular atrophy in Klinefelter's syndrome (secondary gynaecomastia)
  9. Drugs: alcohol, marijuana, heroin, antiretroviral therapy, anabolic steroids (used by some athletes and body-builders), and some psychoactive agents
  10. Idiopathic gynaecomastia (without any obvious cause)
🎯 Gynaecomastia = "oestrogen ↑ / androgen ↓ imbalance." Remember the drug list: alcohol, marijuana, heroin, ARVs, anabolic steroids, psychoactive agents.
