🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of the Kidney & Urinary Tract

The kidney's core job is filtration → reabsorption → secretion → excretion. Every renal disease ultimately disrupts this pipeline. Organize it by location:

🔴 Glomerular diseases — the big two syndromes: Nephritic syndrome (haematuria, hypertension, oliguria, mild proteinuria) vs Nephrotic syndrome (massive proteinuria ≥3.5 g/day, hypoalbuminaemia, oedema, hyperlipidaemia, lipiduria). Primary glomerulopathies: Minimal Change Disease (MCD), Membranous Nephropathy, Membranoproliferative GN (MPGN), Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), IgA Nephropathy, Rapidly Progressive (Crescentic) GN (RPGN). Secondary: SLE nephritis, Diabetic Nephropathy, Goodpasture, vasculitis-associated. Hereditary: Alport syndrome, Fabry disease.

📌 Tubulo-interstitial diseases — Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN), Pyelonephritis (acute + chronic), Drug-induced nephropathy.

📌 Vascular diseases — Nephrosclerosis, Renal Artery Stenosis, Thrombotic Microangiopathies (TTP/HUS).

🎯 Cystic diseases — ADPKD (Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease), ARPKD (Autosomal Recessive PKD), Simple cysts.

🎯 Tumours — Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC, clear-cell most common), Wilms Tumour (nephroblastoma, most common paediatric renal tumour), Urothelial carcinoma, Angiomyolipoma, Oncocytoma.

📌 Obstructive uropathy & stones — Calcium oxalate (70%), Struvite/staghorn (infection), Uric acid, Cystine. Bladder: Transitional cell carcinoma (risk: smoking, aryl amines, Schistosoma haematobium).

1 · Renal Histology & Filtration Barrier

Describe the structure of a nephron and the three components of the glomerular filtration barrier.

The parenchyma of each kidney contains approximately 1 million nephrons. A nephron has 5 major parts: (1) Glomerular capsule (glomerulus + Bowman's capsule), (2) Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT), (3) Loop of Henle, (4) Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT), (5) Collecting ducts.

Glomerular Filtration Barrier — 3 components (inside → out):

  1. Fenestrated endothelial cells lining the capillary loops
  2. Glomerular Basement Membrane (GBM) — endothelial cells rest on this
  3. Filtration slit pores between the foot processes (pedicels) of visceral epithelial cells (podocytes) external to the GBM

The glomerulus performs complex filtration from capillaries into the urinary space.

2 · Classification of Kidney Diseases

🔴 Name and classify the diseases of the kidney.

Comprehensive classification:

  1. Congenital & developmental anomalies — Agenesis, Hypoplasia, Ectopic kidney, Horseshoe kidney, Polycystic kidney
  2. Glomerular diseases — Nephritic syndrome, Nephrotic syndrome, Hereditary (Fabry disease, Alport syndrome), Systemic (SLE nephritis, Henoch-Schönlein Purpura, Diabetic nephropathy, Amyloidosis)
  3. Tubular & interstitial diseases — Acute Tubular Injury/Necrosis (ATI/ATN), Tubulointerstitial nephritis, Pyelonephritis & UTI, Drug-induced nephropathy, Others (urate nephropathy, hypercalcaemia & nephrocalcinosis, light-chain cast nephropathy/"myeloma kidney")
  4. Vascular diseases — Nephrosclerosis, Renal artery stenosis, Thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP, HUS), Atherosclerotic ischaemic renal disease, Atheroembolic renal disease, Sickle cell nephropathy, Renal infarcts
  5. Cystic diseases — Simple cyst, Acquired cystic disease, Polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD, ARPKD), Cystic diseases of renal medulla (Medullary sponge kidney, Nephronophthisis), Multicystic renal dysplasia
  6. Urinary tract obstruction (obstructive uropathy)
  7. Urolithiasis (renal calculi/stones)
  8. Tumours — Benign (papillary adenoma, angiomyolipoma, oncocytoma, mesoblastic nephroma) · Malignant — Primary: RCC (adenocarcinoma), Urothelial carcinoma of renal pelvis, Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma) · Secondary: Blood-borne metastases (lung, breast, stomach)

4 · Clinical Manifestations of Renal Diseases

🔴 What are the clinical manifestations of renal diseases?

11 major clinical presentations:

  1. Azotaemia — elevation of Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine levels (biochemical abnormality)
  2. Uraemia — clinical signs & symptoms of azotaemia with biochemical abnormalities
  3. Nephritic syndrome
  4. Nephrotic syndrome
  5. Asymptomatic haematuria or proteinuria
  6. Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) (formerly acute renal failure)
  7. Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) (formerly chronic renal failure)
  8. End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) — GFR <5% of normal
  9. Renal tubular defects — polyuria, nocturia, electrolyte disorders (e.g. metabolic acidosis)
  10. Urinary tract obstruction/infection — bacteriuria + pyuria; pyelonephritis or cystitis
  11. Nephrolithiasis (renal stones) — renal colic, haematuria
🎯 Azotaemia = biochemical (↑BUN + creatinine) alone; Uraemia = biochemical + clinical symptoms.

5 · Classification of Glomerular Diseases

🔴 Classify glomerular diseases into primary, secondary, and hereditary. List the key terminologies.

A) Primary Glomerular Diseases

Minimal-Change Disease (lipoid nephrosis) · Membranous Glomerulonephritis · Membranoproliferative GN (MPGN) · Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) · IgA Nephropathy (Berger disease) · Chronic GN · Acute GN · Rapidly Progressive (Crescentic) GN (RPGN)

🎯 Primary = "3 M-F-I-C-A" → MCD, Membranous, MPGN, FSGS, IgA nephropathy, Chronic GN, Acute GN/RPGN. Or mnemonic: "3M Formula-1 CAR" → Minimal change, Membranous, MPGN, FSGS, IgA, RPGN/Crescentic.

B) Secondary Glomerular Diseases

SLE (Lupus Nephritis) · Diabetic Nephropathy · Amyloidosis · Goodpasture Syndrome · Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN) · Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA, formerly Wegener) · Microscopic Polyangiitis · Henoch-Schönlein Purpura (HSP) · Cryoglobulinaemia · Systemic Infectious Diseases (Bacterial: endocarditis, syphilis, leprosy; Viral: HBV, HCV, HIV; Parasitic: P. falciparum malaria, filariasis)

C) Hereditary Disorders

Alport Syndrome · Thin Basement Membrane Disease · Fabry Disease

6 · Nephritic Syndrome (Acute Glomerulonephritis)

🔴 Define nephritic syndrome / Acute Glomerulonephritis (AGN). What are its causes?

Definition: A glomerular disease characterised by inflammation in the glomeruli, manifested as haematuria (red blood cells and RBC casts in urine), proteinuria (usually subnephrotic range), with or without oedema, azotaemia, and hypertension.

Causes of Nephritic Syndrome (AGN):

A. Primary Glomerular Diseases:

  1. Acute proliferative GN: (a) Post-streptococcal GN (Group A β-haemolytic streptococcus) · (b) Non-streptococcal acute GN (post-infectious): Bacterial (staphylococcal endocarditis, pneumococcal pneumonia, meningococcaemia); Viral (HBV, HCV, mumps, HIV, varicella, infectious mononucleosis); Parasitic (malaria, toxoplasmosis)
  2. RPGN (Rapidly Progressive Crescentic GN)
  3. Membranoproliferative GN
  4. IgA Nephropathy

B. Systemic Diseases: Goodpasture syndrome (pulmonary haemorrhage + renal failure), SLE (Lupus nephritis), PAN, GPA (Wegener), Microscopic Polyangiitis, HSP, Cryoglobulinaemia.

Robbins 10e p.906 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.648 · Arif pp.229–230

🔴 Describe the pathogenesis of Post-Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis (PSGN).

Causative agent: Group A β-haemolytic streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes).

Lag period: 1–4 weeks after streptococcal pharyngitis (sore throat) or skin infection (infected scabies/impetigo).

PSGN is an immunologically mediated disease — three mechanisms:

  1. Circulating immune complex: Antibody against M-protein of streptococcal cell wall → Ag-Ab complex in circulation → deposition in glomeruli
  2. In-situ immune complex: Streptococcal antigen settles in glomeruli → antibody formed → reacts with settled Ag in glomeruli
  3. Anti-GBM mechanism: Bacterial enzyme (proteinase) alters GBM → Ab against altered GBM → Ag-Ab reaction

Final common pathway: Ag-Ab complexes implanted in GBM → activation of classical complement pathway → formation of Chemotactic factors + Membrane Attack Complex (MAC) → aggregation of neutrophils & macrophages → release of enzymes → injury to epithelial, endothelial & mesangial cells → AGN.

Robbins 10e p.906 · Arif pp.230–231

🔴 Describe the morphology (gross + microscopic) of Acute Diffuse Proliferative GN.

Macroscopic (Gross):

  • Kidney is enlarged
  • Surface is smooth, pale with red haemorrhagic spots scattered all over ("flea-bitten kidney")

Microscopic:

  • Enlarged, hypercellular glomeruli
  • Combination of proliferation, swelling, and leukocyte infiltration obliterates the capillary lumens
  • May be interstitial oedema and inflammation
  • Tubules often contain RBC casts

Robbins 10e p.908 · Arif pp.231

🔴 Mention the clinical features and complications of AGN.

Clinical Features:

  • Malaise · Fever (low-grade) · Nausea
  • Oliguria (reduced urine output)
  • Haematuria — smoky or cola-coloured urine
  • Mild proteinuria (usually <1 g/day)
  • Periorbital oedema
  • Mild to moderate hypertension

Complications (Important for VIVA):

  1. Acute renal failure
  2. Chronic glomerulonephritis
  3. Chronic renal failure
  4. Electrolyte & acid-base disturbance (hyperkalaemia, metabolic acidosis, hyponatraemia)
  5. Hypertensive encephalopathy
  6. Left ventricular failure
  7. Papillary necrosis

Robbins 10e p.909 · Arif pp.231–232

Write about the prognosis of AGN.

Children: >95% eventually recover completely with conservative therapy. A small minority (<1%) become severely oliguric, develop RPGN, and a few may progress to chronic GN (chronic renal failure).

Adults: About 60% recover promptly; some develop RPGN; others develop chronic GN.

Robbins 10e p.909 · Arif p.232

7 · Nephrotic Syndrome

🔴 Define nephrotic syndrome. Enumerate its causes. Differentiate primary causes in children vs adults.

Definition: A glomerular syndrome characterised by massive proteinuria (≥3.5 g/day), hypoalbuminaemia (plasma albumin <3 g/dl), generalised oedema, hyperlipidaemia, and lipiduria.

Causes of Nephrotic Syndrome:

1. Primary Glomerular Diseases:

  • Minimal-change disease (most common in children)
  • Membranous glomerulonephritis (most common in adults)
  • Membranoproliferative GN
  • Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)
  • IgA nephropathy

2. Systemic Diseases: Diabetes mellitus, Amyloidosis, SLE, Drugs (NSAIDs, penicillamine, heroin), Infections (malaria, syphilis, HBV, HCV, HIV), Malignancy (carcinoma, lymphoma, myeloma), Miscellaneous (bee sting allergy, hereditary nephritis).

🎯 Children → primary glomerulonephritis most common; most frequent = MCD (65%). Adults → systemic diseases (DM, amyloidosis, SLE) more frequent; most common primary = Membranous GN (40%).

Robbins 10e pp.896, 912 · Harsh Mohan 7e p.648 · Arif pp.232–233

What are the laboratory findings in nephrotic syndrome?
  1. Urinalysis (R/M/E): Massive proteinuria (albumin +++/++++); casts: hyaline & granular casts may be present
  2. 24-hour urinary total protein: >3.5 g
  3. Albumin/globulin ratio: Reduced
  4. Serum cholesterol & triglycerides: Increased (hyperlipidaemia)
  5. Serum C₃: Normal
  6. Total serum calcium: Decreased
  7. Renal biopsy: In suitable cases

9 · Minimal Change Disease (Lipoid Nephrosis)

🔴 Write about Minimal Change Disease (lipoid nephrosis). Why is it called lipoid nephrosis?

Definition: The prototype of nephrotic syndrome. Characterised by diffuse effacement of foot processes of podocytes on electron microscopy, with glomeruli appearing virtually normal by light microscopy.

Peak incidence: 2–6 years of age. Dramatic response to corticosteroid therapy (highly steroid-sensitive).

Etiology & Pathogenesis:

Involves some immune dysfunction → elaboration of a cytokine that damages visceral epithelial cells (podocytes) → proteinuria. Ultrastructural changes point to primary visceral epithelial cell injury + loss of glomerular polyanions. Defects in charge barrier contribute to proteinuria.

Morphology:

  • Gross: Kidney pale/yellowish, slightly larger & heavier than normal
  • Microscopy (LM): No visible basement membrane changes; lipid accumulation in renal tubular cells
  • EM: Fused/effaced foot processes of podocytes (pathognomonic finding)

Why "lipoid nephrosis"? Proximal tubular cells are laden with lipid and protein, reflecting tubular reabsorption of lipoproteins passing through diseased glomeruli → historical term.

Prognosis: Excellent — despite massive proteinuria, renal function remains good; excellent long-term prognosis for children and adults.

Robbins 10e p.914 · BRS Pathology 5e p.265 · Arif pp.237

10 · Membranous Nephropathy

What is membranous nephropathy? Describe its morphology.

Definition: A common cause of the nephrotic syndrome in adults. Characterised by diffuse thickening of the glomerular capillary wall due to accumulation of deposits containing immunoglobulin along the subepithelial side of the basement membrane.

Morphology:

  • LM: Uniform diffuse thickening of the glomerular capillary wall
  • EM: GBM markedly thickened by intramembranous and epimembranous (subepithelial) immune complex deposits appearing as irregular spikes protruding from the GBM → "spike and dome" appearance
  • IF: Granular deposits of immunoglobulins and various amounts of complement along the GBM

Robbins 10e p.913 · BRS Pathology 5e p.265 · Arif p.237

11 · IgA Nephropathy (Berger Disease)

🔴 Write a short note on IgA nephropathy (Berger disease).

Definition: One of the most common causes of recurrent microscopic or gross haematuria and the most common glomerular disease worldwide. Characterised by deposition of IgA in the mesangium.

Pathogenesis:

  1. Genetic or acquired abnormality of immune system
  2. Increased IgA synthesis in response to respiratory or GI infection (virus, bacteria, food protein)
  3. IgA₁ and IgA₁-containing immune complexes trapped in the mesangium
  4. Activation of alternate complement pathway
  5. Glomerular injury

Robbins 10e p.920 · Arif p.238

12 · Chronic Glomerulonephritis

What are the causes of chronic glomerulonephritis?

Chronic GN refers to end-stage glomerular disease that may result from specific types of GN or develop without antecedent history of any well-recognised acute GN form.

Primary glomerular diseases that lead to chronic GN:

  • Post-streptococcal GN
  • RPGN (Rapidly Progressive GN)
  • Membranous nephropathy
  • MPGN (Membranoproliferative GN)
  • IgA nephropathy
  • FSGS (Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis)

Robbins 9e p.925 · Arif p.238

13 · Diabetic Nephropathy

🔴 Write a short note on diabetic nephropathy. What are the lesions and morphology?

Definition: The conglomerate of lesions that often occur concurrently in the diabetic kidney. It is one of the leading causes of chronic kidney failure. Advanced/end-stage kidney disease occurs in as many as 40% of both Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes.

Glomerular Syndromes in Diabetic Nephropathy:

  1. Non-nephrotic proteinuria
  2. Nephrotic syndrome
  3. Chronic renal failure

Diabetes also causes hyalinising arteriolar sclerosis, increased susceptibility to pyelonephritis, papillary necrosis, and various tubular lesions.

Three Categories of Lesions:

  1. Glomerular lesions
  2. Renal vascular lesions (principally arteriosclerosis)
  3. Pyelonephritis including necrotising papillitis

Morphology:

  • Basement membrane markedly thickened
  • Diffuse or nodular mesangial accumulations of basement membrane-like material (Kimmelstiel-Wilson nodules = nodular glomerulosclerosis — pathognomonic)

Robbins 10e pp.923, 1118 · BRS Pathology 5e p.265 · Arif pp.238–239

14 · Acute Tubular Injury / Necrosis (ATI / ATN)

🔴 Define acute tubular injury (ATI) / acute tubular necrosis (ATN). Give its types, pathogenesis, and morphology.

Definition: A clinicopathologic entity characterised by damage to tubular epithelial cells and an acute decline in renal function, often associated with shedding of granular casts and tubular cells into the urine. It is the most common cause of acute kidney injury (AKI) and may produce oliguria (<400 mL/day).

Types & Causes:

1. Ischaemic ATI (decreased/interrupted blood flow): Hypotension & shock (hypovolaemic), severe trauma, acute pancreatitis, HUS (Haemolytic Uraemic Syndrome), TTP (Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura), DIC (Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation).

2. Nephrotoxic ATI (direct toxic injury): Drugs (gentamycin, other antibiotics), radiographic contrast agents, heavy metals (mercury), organic solvents (carbon tetrachloride).

3. Combined ischaemic & nephrotoxic ATI: Haemoglobinuria (mismatched blood transfusion, haemolytic crises), Myoglobinuria (severe skeletal muscle injuries).

Pathogenesis (common pathway):

Two critical events: (1) Tubular injury & (2) Persistent severe disturbances in blood flow. → Tubular backleak of filtrate + sloughed cells + interstitial inflammation → decreased GFR → decreased urine output.

Morphology:

Injury or necrosis of segments of tubules (typically proximal tubules), proteinaceous casts in distal tubules, and interstitial oedema.

Clinical Features:

Electrolyte abnormalities · Acidosis · Uraemia · Fluid overload with oliguria.

Robbins Basic Pathology 10e pp.562, 567–569 · Robbins 10e p.924 · Arif pp.239–240

15 · Pyelonephritis

🔴 Define pyelonephritis. What are its types, etiology, and routes of infection?

Definition: Inflammation affecting the tubules, interstitium, and renal pelvis. One of the most common diseases of the kidney.

Types:

1. Acute pyelonephritis — suppurative inflammation caused by bacterial infection; associated with UTI.

2. Chronic pyelonephritis — more complex; bacterial infection + other factors (vesicoureteral reflux, obstruction) predispose to repeat episodes.

Etiology:

E. coli (most common) · Proteus · Klebsiella · Enterobacter · Streptococcus faecalis · Staphylococci · Polyoma virus · Cytomegalovirus · Adenovirus.

Routes of Infection:

  1. Ascending infection from lower UTI (most common)
  2. Haematogenous infection (through bloodstream)

Pathogenesis of Ascending Infection:

  1. Colonisation of organisms in distal urethra & introitus (in females)
  2. Entrance from urethra to bladder during catheterisation / instrumentation
  3. Urinary obstruction + stasis favours bacterial multiplication
  4. Vesico-ureteral reflux → bacteria ascend into renal pelvis
  5. Intra-renal reflux → passage deep into renal parenchyma through open ducts at papillae tips

Robbins 10e pp.927–928 · Arif pp.240–241

What are the predisposing factors and complications of acute pyelonephritis?

Predisposing Factors:

  1. Urinary tract obstruction (congenital or acquired)
  2. Instrumentation (most commonly catheterisation)
  3. Vesicoureteral reflux
  4. Pregnancy
  5. Gender & age — females more affected; males increase with prostatic hypertrophy
  6. Pre-existing renal lesions
  7. Diabetes mellitus
  8. Immunosuppression & immunodeficiency

Complications:

  • Papillary necrosis (mainly in diabetics, sickle cell disease, UT obstruction)
  • Pyonephrosis (total/near-complete obstruction, especially high in urinary tract)
  • Perinephric abscess
What are the urinary findings?

Urinary Findings:

Acute: Protein = trace amount; Microscopy = many leucocytes (pyuria), leucocyte/neutrophil casts (pus casts), some RBCs, epithelial cells, bacteria (bacteriuria); Urine culture = positive.

Chronic: Protein in small amount; pus cells.

Robbins 10e pp.928–930 · Arif pp.241

16 · Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)

Define UTI. Why is it more common in females? Name the common pathogens.

Definition: Presence and multiplication of bacteria in the urinary tract.

Types:

Upper tract: Renal parenchyma + renal pelvis · Lower tract: Bladder

Common Pathogens:

Gram-negative: E. coli (80–90% in young females), Proteus, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella

Gram-positive: S. saprophyticus (young females), S. epidermidis (prosthetic devices), S. aureus, E. faecalis

Others: M. tuberculosis, Chlamydia, Mycoplasma, Leptospira; Fungus: Candida; Parasites: T. vaginalis, E. vermicularis, S. haematobium, W. bancrofti

Why More Common in Females:

  • Shorter urethra
  • Absence of antibacterial properties found in prostatic fluid
  • Close proximity to anus
  • Hormonal changes affecting bacterial adherence to mucosa
  • Urethral trauma during sexual intercourse

Robbins 8e p.939 · Arif pp.241–242

17 · Acute Renal Failure (ARF) / Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

🔴 Define acute renal failure / AKI. Classify its causes. Describe the stages.

Definition: A sudden, usually reversible loss of renal function developing over days to weeks, usually accompanied by reduced urine volume. Characterised by rapid decline in GFR (within hours to days) with dysregulation of fluid/electrolyte balance and retention of metabolic waste products (urea, creatinine).

Causes (Prerenal / Renal / Postrenal):

A. Prerenal: (1) Systemic: Heart failure, blood/fluid loss · (2) Local: Renal artery occlusion/stenosis, diseases affecting arterioles

B. Renal (intrinsic): (1) ATN / toxic / septic RF (85%) · (2) Glomerular disease (primary or component of systemic disease) · (3) Interstitial disease

C. Postrenal: Obstruction — stones, tumour, prostatic enlargement

Stages:

  1. Initiating stage
  2. Maintenance stage — Oliguria, uraemia, progressive rise of blood creatinine, hyperkalaemia, metabolic acidosis, salt & water overload
  3. Recovery stage — Gradual increase in urine volume (may reach 3 L/day); blood urea & creatinine return toward normal

Davidson 21e pp.482–483 · Robbins 10e p.898 · Arif pp.242–243

A girl of 6 years has watery diarrhoea and then cessation of micturition. Explain the mechanisms involved.

Pathogenesis (stepwise):

  1. Acute watery diarrhoea → loss of plenty of fluid
  2. Acute hypovolaemia
  3. Several autoregulatory mechanisms come into play
  4. When persistent hypovolaemia is present → these mechanisms fail
  5. Severe prolonged underperfusion of kidneys → acute decline of GFR → low volume, highly concentrated urine
  6. More severe and persistent hypovolaemia → Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN)
  7. ATN → Acute Renal Failure
  8. Cessation of micturition (anuria)

20 · Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC)

🔴 Write about RCC: definition, risk factors, morphology, and clinical features.

Definition: Derived from renal tubular epithelium (adenocarcinoma). Represents 80–85% of all primary malignant renal neoplasms and 2–3% of all adult cancers.

Risk Factors / Causes:

  • Tobacco smoking
  • Male gender
  • Obesity (particularly in women)
  • Hypertension
  • Unopposed oestrogen therapy
  • Exposure to asbestos, petroleum products, heavy metals
  • ESRD, chronic kidney disease, acquired cystic disease, tuberous sclerosis

Morphology — Gross:

  • Arises in any portion of kidney; more commonly affects the poles
  • Clear cell carcinoma: Solitary, spherical; yellow to orange to grey-white tissue; areas of cystic softening, haemorrhage (fresh or old)
  • Papillary RCC: Necrosis, haemorrhage, cystic degeneration; tend to be bilateral and multiple
  • Chromophobe RCC: Grossly tan-brown

Morphology — Microscopic:

Type%Key Features
Clear cell carcinoma65%Rounded/polygonal cells; abundant clear or granular cytoplasm
Papillary RCC10–15%Cuboidal/low columnar cells in papillae with fibrovascular cores; clear or pink cytoplasm
Chromophobe RCC5%Clear, flocculent cytoplasm; very prominent cell membranes; nuclei surrounded by halos of clear cytoplasm

Clinical Features (Very Important for VIVA):

  1. Haematuria (>50% of cases) — painless haematuria is characteristic
  2. Costovertebral (flank) pain + palpable mass
  3. Constitutional symptoms (fever, malaise, weakness, weight loss)
  4. Polycythaemia (EPO production)
  5. Paraneoplastic syndromes (hypercalcaemia, hypertension, Cushing syndrome, feminisation/masculinisation)
  6. Wide metastases: lungs & bones (most common), then lymph nodes, liver, adrenals, brain
🎯 Classic triad: Painless haematuria + palpable abdominal mass + dull flank pain

Robbins Basic Pathology 10e pp.578–580 · Robbins 10e pp.948–950 · Arif pp.244–246

A male 55 yr with haematuria, loin pain, palpable lumbar mass. Diagnosis?

Likely diagnosis: Renal Cell Carcinoma.

Investigations:

Investigations?
  1. Urine R/E — RBC; Cytology — malignant cells may be present
  2. Blood urea, serum creatinine — renal functional status
  3. USG of whole abdomen
  4. Intravenous urography (IVU)
  5. Plain X-ray KUB — may show calcification
  6. Contrast CT scan of abdomen
  7. Chest X-ray / CT chest — lung metastasis ("cannon ball")
  8. Isotope renogram — functional status of kidneys
  9. Isotope bone scan — bone metastasis

Arif pp.245–246

21 · Wilms Tumour (Nephroblastoma)

🔴 Write a short note on Wilms tumour: pathogenesis, morphology, and clinical features.

Definition: The most common primary renal tumour of childhood. Usually occurs between 2–5 years of age. ~5–10% involve both kidneys.

Pathogenesis:

  • Associated gene at chromosome 11p13
  • Risk increased with three congenital malformations:
    1. WAGR syndrome — Wilms tumour, Aniridia, Genitourinary anomalies, Mental Retardation
    2. Denys-Drash syndrome — Wilms tumour, ambiguous genitalia, nephropathy
    3. Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome — macrosomia, omphalocele, visceromegaly, macroglossia, hypoglycaemia

Morphology — Gross:

  • Large, solitary, well-circumscribed mass
  • 10% bilateral or multicentric at diagnosis
  • Cut section: soft, homogeneous, tan to grey; foci of haemorrhage, cyst formation, necrosis

Morphology — Microscopic:

  • Different stages of nephrogenesis
  • Classic triphasic combination: blastemal + stromal + epithelial cell types (in vast majority)
  • Epithelial differentiation: abortive tubules or glomeruli
  • Stromal cells: fibrotic or myxoid; may show skeletal muscle differentiation
  • May show heterologous elements: squamous/mucinous epithelium, smooth muscle, adipose tissue, cartilage, osteoid, neurogenic tissue
  • ~5% show anaplasia

Robbins 10e p.480 · Arif pp.246–247

22 · Renal Stones (Urolithiasis)

🔴 Classify renal stones. What is a staghorn calculus? Name common causes and who is most affected.

Types of Renal Calculi (4 main types):

Type%Key Features
Calcium oxalate & phosphate~70%Most common; composed of calcium oxalate or mixed calcium oxalate + phosphate
Magnesium ammonium phosphate (triple / struvite stones)~15%Formed after infection by urea-splitting bacteria (e.g. Proteus, some staphylococci); alkaline urine → precipitation; form staghorn calculi occupying large portions of renal pelvis
Uric acid stones5–10%Common in hyperuricaemia (e.g. gout)
Cystine stones1–2%Genetic defect in renal reabsorption of amino acids → cystinuria

Staghorn calculi: Large stones occupying large portions of the renal pelvis, almost always a consequence of infection (struvite).

Common Causes of Renal Stone Formation:

Gout · Hypercalciuria · Hypercalcaemia · Cystinuria · Primary hyperoxaluria · Hypercholesterolaemia.

Who Is Most Affected:

  • Men affected more than women
  • Peak age: 20–30 years
  • Familial and hereditary predisposition
  • Inborn errors of metabolism (gout, cystinuria, primary hyperoxaluria)

Robbins Basic Pathology 10e p.576 · Robbins 10e p.947 · Arif pp.247–248

23 · Urinary Bladder — Cystitis & Tumours

Write short notes on acute and chronic cystitis. What are the predisposing factors and symptoms?

Etiology (Causative Organisms):

1. Coliform bacteria: E. coli, Proteus, Klebsiella, Enterobacter, Tuberculous cystitis, Chlamydia, Mycoplasma

2. Fungus: Candida albicans

3. Parasite: Schistosoma haematobium

4. Virus: Adenovirus

Predisposing Factors:

Bladder calculi · Urinary obstruction · Diabetes mellitus · Instrumentation · Immunodeficiency · Radiation cystitis

Symptoms (clinical triad):

  1. Frequency (may need to urinate every 15–20 minutes in acute cases)
  2. Lower abdominal / suprapubic pain
  3. Dysuria (pain or burning on urination)

What are the complications of bladder stone and the tumours of the urinary bladder?

Complications of Bladder Calculus:

Bladder outlet obstruction · Infection · Anuria & retention of urine · Haematuria · Chronic bladder dysfunction · Bladder cancer.

Tumours of Urinary Bladder:

A. Primary:

  • Urothelial (transitional) tumours: Noninvasive urothelial tumours, Infiltrating urothelial carcinoma, Variants (nested, microcystic, micropapillary, plasmacytoid, sarcomatoid, giant cell, poorly differentiated, lipid-rich, clear cell)
  • Adenocarcinoma
  • Squamous cell carcinoma
  • Mixed carcinoma
  • Small cell carcinoma
  • Sarcomas

B. Secondary: Direct extension from cervix, uterus, prostate, rectum; Lymphoma (systemic disease component).

Risk Factors for Bladder Carcinoma:

  • Cigarette smoking (most important risk factor)
  • Industrial exposure to aryl amines
  • Schistosoma haematobium infection (most common cause of squamous cell bladder carcinoma)
  • Long-term use of analgesics
  • Heavy long-term cyclophosphamide exposure
  • Prior bladder radiation

Enumerate the causes of urinary tract obstruction (obstructive uropathy).

Causes organised by category:

  1. Congenital anomalies: Posterior urethral valves, urethral strictures, meatal stenosis, bladder neck obstruction, ureteropelvic junction narrowing/obstruction, severe vesicoureteral reflux
  2. Urinary calculi
  3. Benign prostatic hypertrophy (BPH)
  4. Tumours: Carcinoma of prostate, bladder tumours, retroperitoneal lymphoma, carcinoma of cervix/uterus
  5. Inflammation: Prostatitis, ureteritis, urethritis, retroperitoneal fibrosis
  6. Intrinsic: Sloughed renal papilla, blood clots
  7. Other: Pregnancy, uterine prolapse, cystocele
  8. Functional: Neurogenic (spinal cord damage / diabetic neuropathy), other functional abnormalities of ureter/bladder
