# Ch 17 — The Eye     <i>Full-detail flashcards (Q→A) · one short question per card · Robbins 4-pillar answers</i>
### 🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of the Eye (boss-level overview)
The eye is organised from <b>anterior → posterior</b>: conjunctiva → cornea → lens → retina → choroid. Disease can strike any layer. This chapter covers:
<b>1.</b> <b>Conjunctival diseases</b> — scarring, pinguecula, pterygium, neoplasms (squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), conjunctival intra-epithelial neoplasia (CIN), mucoepidermoid carcinoma, nevi, melanoma).<br>
<b>2.</b> <b>Corneal diseases</b> — keratitis & ulcers, band keratopathy, keratoconus, Fuchs endothelial dystrophy, stromal dystrophies.<br>
<b>3.</b> <b>Orbital neoplasms</b> — primary (capillary haemangioma, lymphangioma, cavernous haemangioma, pleomorphic adenoma of lacrimal gland, dermoid cyst, neurilemmoma, orbital lymphoma) and secondary/metastatic (prostatic carcinoma, neuroblastoma, Wilms tumour).<br>
<b>4.</b> <b>Eyelid malignancies</b> — basal cell carcinoma (BCC), sebaceous carcinoma.<br>
<b>5.</b> <b>Retinal diseases</b> — detachment, vascular disease (hypertensive, diabetic, retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), radiation, vasculitis, sickle-cell retinopathy, artery/vein occlusion), age-related macular degeneration (AMD), retinitis pigmentosa, retinitis due to Candida, neoplasms (retinoblastoma (Rb), retinal lymphoma).<br>
<b>6.</b> <b>Retinoblastoma</b> — most common primary intraocular malignancy of children; RB gene on chromosome 13q14.<br>
<b>7.</b> <b>Cataract</b> — congenital & acquired lenticular opacities (galactosemia, diabetes mellitus (DM), Wilson disease, drugs, radiation, trauma, age-related).<br>
<b>8.</b> <b>Glaucoma</b> — elevated intra-ocular pressure (IOP) → visual field loss & optic-nerve cupping; classified as primary (open-angle = most common; angle-closure) and secondary.

## 1 · Conjunctival & Corneal Diseases
<b>What are the conjunctival diseases?</b>

Conjunctival scarring — due to chronic inflammation (trachoma, Stevens-Johnson syndrome), chemical burns, or surgical trauma.
Pinguecula — yellowish elevated plaque on the bulbar conjunctiva (nasal side); associated with UV exposure.
Pterygium — wing-shaped fibrovascular growth extending from conjunctiva onto the cornea; associated with UV light and chronic irritation.
Neoplasms — SCC, CIN, mucoepidermoid carcinoma, nevi, melanoma (see next cards).

Robbins 10e p.1310

<b>What is the most common malignant conjunctival tumour?</b>

<b>Conjunctival squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)</b> — arises from the conjunctival epithelium, often at the limbus. Risk factors: UV exposure, HIV/immunosuppression.

Robbins 10e p.1310

<b>What is conjunctival intra-epithelial neoplasia (CIN)?</b>

A <b>precursor / pre-malignant lesion</b>; graded I–III based on thickness of epithelium involved. May progress to invasive SCC.

Robbins 10e p.1310

<b>Distinguish mucoepidermoid carcinoma from pure SCC of the conjunctiva.</b>

<b>Mucoepidermoid carcinoma</b> contains mucus-secreting (goblet) cells interspersed with squamous cells. It is <b>more aggressive</b> with a higher recurrence rate than pure SCC.

Robbins 10e p.1310

<b>What are conjunctival nevi and conjunctival melanoma?</b>

<b>Conjunctival nevi:</b> benign melanocytic naevi; common in young adults; usually pigmented, may show cystic changes.

<b>Conjunctival melanoma:</b> malignant melanoma that may arise from a pre-existing nevus, CIN, or de novo. Can metastasise haematogenously and via lymphatics.

Robbins 10e p.1310

## 2 · Corneal Diseases (detail)
<b>What are the diseases of the cornea?</b>

Keratitis and ulcers — infectious (bacterial, fungal, viral — especially HSV) or non-infectious. Corneal ulceration can be sight-threatening if it perforates.
Corneal degenerations and dystrophies — band keratopathy, keratoconus, Fuchs endothelial dystrophy, stromal dystrophies.

Robbins 10e p.1311

<b>What is band keratopathy?</b>

<b>Calcium phosphate deposition</b> in Bowman layer of the cornea, forming a horizontal "band" across the inter-palpebral zone. Associated with chronic eye disease, hypercalcaemia, and renal failure.

Robbins 10e p.1311

<b>What is keratoconus?</b>

Non-inflammatory, progressive <b>thinning and cone-shaped protrusion</b> of the cornea. Usually bilateral; often presents in adolescence. Leads to irregular astigmatism.

Robbins 10e p.1311

<b>What is Fuchs endothelial dystrophy?</b>

Progressive loss of corneal <b>endothelial cells → stromal oedema → epithelial bullae</b> ("bullous keratopathy"). Usually bilateral; more common in <b>women</b>. May require corneal transplant (penetrating keratoplasty) in advanced stages.

Robbins 10e p.1311

<b>What are corneal stromal dystrophies?</b>

Group of <b>inherited</b> corneal opacities affecting the stroma. Includes <b>granular, lattice, and macular</b> dystrophies. Typically bilateral, symmetric, and slowly progressive.

Robbins 10e p.1311

## 3 · Orbital Neoplasms
<b>What is the most common orbital tumour of infancy?</b>

<b>Capillary haemangioma</b> — benign vascular proliferation of capillary-sized vessels. Usually involutes spontaneously by age 5–7 yr.

Robbins 10e p.1307

<b>What is the most common orbital tumour in adults?</b>

<b>Cavernous haemangioma</b> — slowly progressive proptosis. Well-encapsulated; surgical excision is curative.

Robbins 10e p.1307

<b>What is a lymphangioma of the orbit?</b>

Benign lymphatic-channel tumour. May enlarge and cause proptosis. Can undergo spontaneous haemorrhage → <b>chocolate cyst</b>.

Robbins 10e p.1307

<b>What is pleomorphic adenoma of the lacrimal gland?</b>

Benign mixed tumour of the lacrimal gland (analogous to salivary-gland pleomorphic adenoma). Slowly growing, painless superolateral proptosis. Risk of <b>malignant transformation</b> if incompletely excised.

Robbins 10e p.1307

<b>What is the most common congenital orbital mass in children?</b>

<b>Dermoid cyst</b> — congenital choristoma containing ectodermal derivatives (skin, hair follicles, sebaceous glands).

Robbins 10e p.1307

<b>What is a neurilemmoma (schwannoma) of the orbit?</b>

Benign nerve-sheath tumour arising from Schwann cells. Well-encapsulated, slow-growing.

Robbins 10e p.1307

<b>What is orbital lymphoma?</b>

Typically <b>Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL)</b>, most commonly MALT lymphoma. Painless, gradually progressive proptosis. Bilateral involvement possible.

Robbins 10e p.1307

<b>Name the secondary (metastatic) orbital neoplasms.</b>

Metastatic prostatic carcinoma — in older males; often bilateral.
Metastatic neuroblastoma — in children; may cause "raccoon eyes" (periorbital ecchymosis).
Metastatic Wilms tumour (nephroblastoma) — paediatric renal malignancy that can metastasise to the orbit.

Robbins 10e p.1307

## 4 · Eyelid Malignancies
<b>What is the most common eyelid malignancy?</b>

<b>Basal cell carcinoma (BCC)</b> — slow-growing, locally invasive but rarely metastasises. Typically on the <b>lower eyelid</b>. Pearly, translucent nodule with telangiectasia and central ulceration ("rodent ulcer"). Risk factors: UV exposure, fair skin, chronic sun damage.

Robbins 10e p.1308

<b>What is sebaceous carcinoma of the eyelid?</b>

<b>Second most common</b> eyelid malignancy. Arises from <b>Meibomian glands</b> (tarsal plate) or sebaceous glands. More aggressive than BCC; may metastasise. Can mimic chalazion or chronic blepharitis → high index of suspicion needed.

🎯 Bottom eyelid = BCC (most common) · Upper eyelid = Sebaceous carcinoma (think Meibomian gland origin).

Robbins 10e p.1308

## 5 · Retinal Diseases
<b>What is retinal detachment?</b>

Separation of the <b>neurosensory retina</b> from the underlying retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Two types: <b>rhegmatogenous</b> (retinal break → vitreous seeps under retina) and <b>exudative/tractional</b> (secondary to inflammation, tumour, or proliferative membranes).

Robbins 10e p.1319

<b>What are the retinal vascular diseases?</b>

Hypertensive retinopathy — arteriolar narrowing, flame-shaped haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, hard exudates, papilloedema (grade IV).
Diabetic retinopathy — non-proliferative (microaneurysms, dot-and-blot haemorrhages, hard exudates, cotton-wool spots) and proliferative (neovascularisation → vitreous haemorrhage → tractional detachment).
Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) — vasoproliferative disorder of immature retinal vasculature in premature infants. May lead to retinal detachment and blindness.
Radiation retinopathy — damage to retinal vasculature following ionising radiation.
Retinal vasculitis — inflammation of retinal vessels; idiopathic or secondary to systemic disease.
Sickle-cell retinopathy — proliferative retinal neovascularisation in sickle-cell disease due to retinal ischaemia from vascular occlusion.
Retinal artery/vein occlusion — CRAO/BRAO → painless sudden vision loss; CRVO → "blood and thunder" fundus.

Robbins 10e p.1319

<b>What is age-related macular degeneration (AMD)?</b>

Leading cause of irreversible vision loss in the <b>elderly (>50 yr)</b>. Two forms: <b>dry (atrophic)</b> — drusen accumulation, RPE atrophy; <b>wet (neovascular/exudative)</b> — choroidal neovascularisation (CNV) → subretinal haemorrhage & scarring.

Robbins 10e p.1319

<b>What is retinitis pigmentosa?</b>

Group of <b>inherited</b> (usually autosomal dominant) retinal dystrophies characterised by progressive <b>night blindness</b>, visual field constriction ("tunnel vision"), and eventual blindness. Photoreceptor (rod > cone) degeneration.

Robbins 10e p.1319

<b>What causes Candida retinitis?</b>

Endogenous <i>Candida</i> endophthalmitis — white, fluffy chorioretinal lesions. Seen in <b>immunocompromised patients</b> (IV drug users, ICU patients, neutropenic patients).

Robbins 10e p.1319

<b>What is retinal lymphoma?</b>

Primary intraocular lymphoma, usually <b>diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL)</b>; typically in elderly, immunocompromised patients.

Robbins 10e p.1319

## 6 · Retinoblastoma (Rb)
<b>What is retinoblastoma? Which chromosome is involved?</b>

The <b>most common primary intraocular malignancy of children</b>. Diagnosed usually <b>prior to 4 years of age</b>. Bilateral in ~30% (hereditary); unilateral in ~70% (sporadic). Gene: <b>RB gene on chromosome 13q14</b>.

🎯 "13" → unlucky number for Rb patients. Two-hit hypothesis (Knudson).

Robbins 10e p.1324

<b>Explain the two-hit hypothesis in retinoblastoma.</b>

<b>Two mutations of the RB gene</b> on chromosome 13q14 lead to neoplastic proliferation.

Hereditary form: first mutation inherited (germline); second acquired (somatic). Bilateral, earlier onset, higher risk of second primary tumours (e.g., osteosarcoma).
Sporadic form: both mutations acquired (somatic, "two-hit" in same cell). Unilateral, later onset.

Robbins 10e p.1324

<b>Describe the morphology of retinoblastoma.</b>

Pathology of both hereditary and sporadic type is identical.
Tumours may contain both undifferentiated and differentiated elements.
Undifferentiated elements: collections of small round cells with hyperchromatic nuclei ("small blue round cell tumour" of the eye).
Viable tumour cells encircle tumour blood vessels; zones of necrosis in avascular areas.
Focal zones of dystrophic calcification are characteristic — visible on CT scans.

Robbins 10e p.1324

<b>What are the clinical features of retinoblastoma?</b>

Leukocoria ("white pupillary reflex") — most common presenting sign.
Strabismus (squint) — second most common presentation.
May present with painful glaucoma, proptosis, or systemic metastases (optic nerve → CNS; bone; liver).
Extension: along the optic nerve into CNS; haematogenous spread to bone, liver, lungs.

Robbins 10e p.1324

## 7 · Cataract
<b>Define cataract. What are the congenital causes?</b>

<b>Cataract:</b> opacification of the crystalline lens — may be congenital or acquired. Most common cause of preventable blindness worldwide.

<b>Congenital causes:</b> Genetic syndromes (Down, Turner), intra-uterine infections (rubella, toxoplasmosis, CMV), metabolic disorders (galactosemia), trauma.

Robbins 10e p.1313

<b>What metabolic diseases cause cataracts?</b>

Galactosemia — galactitol accumulation → osmotic swelling → "oil-droplet" cataract in infants.
Diabetes mellitus — sorbitol accumulation (via aldose reductase) → snowflake cataract.
Wilson disease — copper deposition → "sunflower cataract" (pathognomonic).

Robbins 10e p.1313

<b>What is the most common cause of acquired cataract?</b>

<b>Age-related nuclear sclerosis</b> — progressive hardening and yellowing of the lens nucleus. The most common cause overall.

Robbins 10e p.1313

<b>Which drugs cause cataracts? Describe the morphology.</b>

<b>Corticosteroids</b> → posterior subcapsular cataract (most common drug cause). Other drugs: chlorpromazine, amiodarone. Radiation (ionising or UV) and trauma also cause cataracts.

<b>Morphology:</b> lens becomes <b>white and opaque</b> (mature cataract). Histologically: lens fibres swell, lose nuclei, form eosinophilic <b>Morgagnian globules</b> in the cortex.

Robbins 10e p.1313

## 8 · Glaucoma
<b>Define glaucoma. What determines IOP?</b>

<b>Glaucoma:</b> a collection of diseases characterised by distinctive changes in the <b>visual field</b> and in the <b>cup of the optic nerve</b>, most associated with <b>elevated intra-ocular pressure (IOP)</b>.

IOP is determined by the balance between <b>aqueous humour production</b> (ciliary body) and <b>drainage</b> (trabecular meshwork → Schlemm canal).

Robbins 10e p.1314

<b>Describe the pathogenesis of glaucomatous optic nerve damage.</b>

↑IOP → <b>compression of retinal ganglion cell axons</b> at the optic disc → ischaemic damage → progressive optic neuropathy → visual field loss (arcuate scotoma, nasal constriction → eventually "tunnel vision" and blindness).

Robbins 10e p.1314

<b>What is primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG)?</b>

<b>Most common type overall</b>. Gradual, painless obstruction of the trabecular meshwork → slow ↑IOP. Peripheral visual field loss first; central vision preserved until late. <b>"Silent thief of sight."</b>

Robbins 10e p.1314

<b>What is primary angle-closure glaucoma (PACG)?</b>

Acute or chronic obstruction by the iris physically covering the trabecular meshwork. Predisposed by shallow anterior chamber, hypermetropia. Acute episode: sudden severe eye pain, headache, nausea, blurred vision, halos, ↑↑IOP, fixed dilated pupil, red eye — <b>ophthalmic emergency</b>.

Robbins 10e p.1314

<b>What causes secondary glaucoma?</b>

<b>Secondary open-angle:</b> exfoliation material, pigment dispersion, traumatic hyphaema, neovascularisation, corticosteroid use.

<b>Secondary angle-closure:</b> lens swelling (intumescent cataract), iris neovascularisation (rubeosis iridis), synechiae, tumours pushing iris forward.

🎯 Open-angle = painless, chronic, "silent". Angle-closure = painful, acute, "emergency".

Robbins 10e p.1314

## 🔁 Rapid-Fire MCQ & Viva Sweep
<b>The Eye — True/False & one-liners (test yourself, then reveal)</b>

Retinoblastoma is the most common primary intraocular malignancy of children (T/F).

<b>True.</b> Diagnosed usually before age 4 years.

The RB gene is located on chromosome 13q14. Two mutations are required for retinoblastoma to develop (T/F).

<b>True.</b> Knudson's "two-hit" hypothesis.

Leukocoria (white pupillary reflex) is the most common presenting sign of retinoblastoma (T/F).

<b>True.</b> Strabismus (squint) is second most common.

Focal zones of dystrophic calcification are NOT characteristic of retinoblastoma (T/F).

<b>False.</b> Focal dystrophic calcification IS characteristic and visible on CT scans.

Capillary haemangioma of the orbit is the most common orbital tumour in adults (T/F).

<b>False.</b> Most common in infancy/childhood. Cavernous haemangioma is most common in adults.

Cavernous haemangioma is the most common orbital tumour in adults (T/F).

<b>True.</b> Slowly progressive, well-encapsulated; surgical excision is curative.

Pleomorphic adenoma of the lacrimal gland is analogous to pleomorphic adenoma of the salivary gland (T/F).

<b>True.</b> Both are benign mixed tumours with risk of malignant transformation if incompletely excised.

Neuroblastoma and Wilms tumour can metastasise to the orbit (T/F).

<b>True.</b> Neuroblastoma may cause "raccoon eyes."

BCC is the most common eyelid malignancy, while sebaceous carcinoma is the most common benign eyelid tumour (T/F).

<b>False.</b> BCC is the most common <b>malignancy</b>; sebaceous carcinoma is the <b>second most common malignancy</b>.

Sebaceous carcinoma of the eyelid arises from Meibomian glands (T/F).

<b>True.</b> Meibomian glands are modified sebaceous glands within the tarsal plate.

ROP affects premature infants and can lead to retinal detachment and blindness (T/F).

<b>True.</b> Vasoproliferative disorder of immature retinal vasculature.

Diabetic retinopathy: neovascularisation characterises the proliferative stage (T/F).

<b>True.</b> Proliferative → neovascularisation → vitreous haemorrhage → tractional detachment.

Cataract is a lenticular opacity; the most common acquired cause is age-related nuclear sclerosis (T/F).

<b>True.</b> Most common cause of acquired cataract worldwide.

"Sunflower cataract" is associated with which metabolic disease?

<b>Wilson disease</b> — copper deposition in the lens.

"Oil-droplet" cataract is characteristic of which metabolic disorder?

<b>Galactosemia</b> — galactitol accumulation in the lens.

Posterior subcapsular cataract is associated with corticosteroid use (T/F).

<b>True.</b> Most common drug cause of posterior subcapsular cataract.

POAG is the most common type of glaucoma (T/F).

<b>True.</b> Painless, chronic, "silent thief of sight."

Acute angle-closure glaucoma is an ophthalmic emergency (T/F).

<b>True.</b> Sudden severe pain, fixed dilated pupil, markedly elevated IOP.
