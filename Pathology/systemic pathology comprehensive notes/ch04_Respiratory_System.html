🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of the Respiratory System (boss-level overview)

🫁 The respiratory system spans conducting airways → respiratory bronchioles → alveoli → pleura. Disease can affect any level:

📌 Classification (Arif pp. 88–89):

  • 1. Congenital anomalies — pulmonary hypoplasia, foregut cysts, sequestration, tracheo-oesophageal fistula, congenital lobar overinflation
  • 2. Atelectasis (collapse) — resorption, compression, contraction
  • 3. Pulmonary oedema — cardiogenic (haemodynamic) vs non-cardiogenic
  • 4. Acute lung injury (ALI) & ARDS — "shock lung"
  • 5. Obstructive lung diseases — 🔴 COPD (emphysema + chronic bronchitis), asthma, bronchiectasis → ↓FEV₁/FVC (<0.7)
  • 6. Restrictive / interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) — pneumoconioses, sarcoidosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hypersensitivity pneumonitis → ↓TLC with normal FEV₁/FVC
  • 7. Vascular diseases — pulmonary embolism (PE), pulmonary infarction, pulmonary hypertension
  • 8. Pulmonary infections — 🔴 pneumonia (lobar, broncho), lung abscess, pulmonary TB
  • 9. Lung tumours — 🔴 bronchogenic carcinoma (90–95% of malignancies), carcinoid, hamartoma; secondary mets > primary
  • 10. Pleural diseases — effusion, pneumothorax, mesothelioma

🎯 One-liner link: Smoking is the single unifying risk factor connecting COPD, emphysema, chronic bronchitis, and bronchogenic carcinoma.

1 · Causes of Haemoptysis

Mention the causes of haemoptysis.

🔴 Haemoptysis = coughing up blood from the respiratory tract. Causes are grouped into three categories (VIVA favourite):

A. Inflammatory

  • Bronchitis
  • Bronchiectasis
  • Pulmonary tuberculosis (TB)
  • Lung abscess
  • Suppurative pneumonia

B. Neoplastic

  • Bronchial carcinoma
  • Bronchial adenoma (carcinoid tumour)

C. Others

  • Pulmonary embolism (PE)
  • Acute left ventricular failure (LVF)
  • Mitral stenosis
  • Trauma
  • Foreign bodies
  • Primary pulmonary hypertension
  • Haemorrhagic diathesis (coagulopathy)

Ref: Harsh Mohan 7e p.480; Arif pp. 90

2 · Atelectasis (Collapse)

Define and describe atelectasis (collapse). What are its types?

Atelectasis (collapse) refers either to incomplete expansion of the lungs (neonatal atelectasis) or to the collapse of previously inflated lung, producing areas of relatively airless pulmonary parenchyma.

Types:

  1. Resorption (obstruction) atelectasis — consequence of complete airway obstruction → air trapped distal alveoli is absorbed, without impairment of blood flow. Airway obstruction caused by mucus plugs (asthma, chronic bronchitis, postoperative states), aspiration of foreign bodies, intrabronchial tumours.
  2. Compression atelectasis — significant volumes of fluid (transudate, exudate, blood), tumour, or air (pneumothorax) accumulate in the pleural cavity → compresses lung.
  3. Contraction atelectasis — focal or generalised pulmonary or pleural fibrosis prevents full lung expansion.

⚠️ Significant atelectasis reduces oxygenation and predisposes to infection.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.675; Arif p. 90–91

3 · Pulmonary Oedema

Define and classify pulmonary oedema. What are the causes?

Pulmonary oedema is the accumulation of excessive interstitial fluid in the alveoli, resulting from either haemodynamic disturbances (cardiogenic) or increased capillary permeability due to microvascular injury (non-cardiogenic).

Classification and causes:

1. Haemodynamic oedema

i) Increased hydrostatic pressure (↑pulmonary venous pressure):

  • Left-sided heart failure (most common)
  • Volume overload
  • Pulmonary vein obstruction

ii) Decreased oncotic pressure:

  • Hypoalbuminaemia
  • Nephrotic syndrome
  • Liver disease
  • Protein-losing enteropathies

iii) Lymphatic obstruction (rare).

2. Oedema due to alveolar wall injury (microvascular/epithelial injury)

i) Direct injury:

  • Infections (bacterial pneumonia)
  • Inhaled gases (high concentration O₂, smoke)
  • Liquid aspiration (gastric contents, near-drowning)
  • Radiation
  • Lung trauma

ii) Indirect injury:

  • Systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) — sepsis, burns, pancreatitis, extensive trauma
  • Blood transfusion-related
  • Drugs: bleomycin, methadone, amphotericin B, heroin, cocaine, kerosene, paraquat

3. Oedema of undetermined origin

  • High altitude
  • Neurogenic (CNS trauma)

Ref: Robbins 10e p.675; Arif pp. 91–92

4 · Acute Lung Injury (ALI) & Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)

🔴 Define ALI and ARDS. What conditions are associated? What are the clinical features?

Acute lung injury (ALI) = abrupt onset of hypoxaemia and bilateral pulmonary oedema in the absence of cardiac failure (non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema).

ARDS = a manifestation of severe ALI; a clinical syndrome of progressive respiratory insufficiency caused by diffuse alveolar damage (DAD) in the setting of sepsis, severe trauma, or diffuse pulmonary infection.

🎯 Synonyms: "shock lung," diffuse alveolar damage, acute alveolar injury.

Both ARDS and ALI are associated with inflammation-associated increases in pulmonary vascular permeability, oedema, and epithelial cell death. Histological hallmark = diffuse alveolar damage (DAD).

Associated conditions:

  1. Infection: sepsis, diffuse pulmonary infections (viral, Mycoplasma, Pneumocystis pneumonia, miliary TB), gastric aspiration
  2. Physical/injury: mechanical trauma (head injuries, pulmonary contusions, near-drowning, fat embolism), burns, ionising radiation
  3. Inhaled irritants: O₂ toxicity, smoke, irritant gases/chemicals
  4. Chemical injury: heroin/methadone overdose, acetylsalicylic acid, barbiturate overdose, paraquat
  5. Haematological: transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
  6. Others: pancreatitis, uraemia, cardiopulmonary bypass, hypersensitivity reactions

Clinical features:

  • Profound dyspnoea and tachypnoea
  • Cyanosis and hypoxaemia
  • Respiratory failure
  • Diffuse bilateral infiltrates on chest X-ray

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 676–678; Arif pp. 92–93

5 · Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)

🔴 Define obstructive lung disease. List the obstructive lung diseases. Define COPD and its manifestations.

Obstructive lung diseases are characterised by an increase in resistance to airflow due to diffuse airway disease affecting any level of the respiratory tract. An FEV₁/FVC ratio <0.7 generally indicates obstructive disease.

Common obstructive lung diseases:

  1. COPD — emphysema + chronic bronchitis
  2. Bronchial asthma
  3. Bronchiectasis

COPD (Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease): Defined by WHO as "a common, preventable and treatable disease characterised by persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow limitation that is due to airway and/or alveolar abnormalities caused by exposure to noxious particles or gases." Currently the 4th leading cause of death in the world.

Characteristics: marked decrease in FEV₁ and an increased or normal forced vital capacity (FVC) → decreased FEV₁:FVC ratio.

Two major clinicopathologic manifestations: (1) Emphysema and (2) Chronic bronchitis. These are often found together because they share the same major aetiologic factor — cigarette smoking.

Etiological factors for COPD:

  1. Cigarette smoking (most important)
  2. Poor lung development early in life
  3. Environmental and occupational pollutants
  4. Airway hyperresponsiveness
  5. Genetic polymorphisms (e.g. α₁-antitrypsin deficiency)

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 675, 678; BRS Pathology 5e p.202; Arif pp. 93–94

6 · Emphysema

🔴 Define and classify emphysema. Describe the types.

Emphysema: A chronic obstructive airway disease characterised by irreversible enlargement of airspaces distal to the terminal bronchiole, accompanied by destruction of their walls without obvious fibrosis. Also: dilation of air spaces with destruction of alveolar walls and lack of elastic recoil.

Necessary lung anatomy (pulmonary acinus):

The part of the lung distal to a terminal bronchiole is called an acinus (lobule = cluster of acini). An acinus has three parts: respiratory bronchioles → alveolar ducts → alveolar sacs (alveoli).

Types of emphysema (4 major types):

TypeAffected areaKey points
1. Centriacinar (centrilobular)Central/proximal parts of the acinus (respiratory bronchioles); distal alveoli spared95% of cases; predominant in heavy smokers; often associated with chronic bronchitis
2. Panacinar (panlobular)Uniformly enlarged from respiratory bronchiole to terminal alveoliAssociated with α₁-antitrypsin deficiency; exacerbated by smoking
3. Distal acinar (paraseptal)Proximal acinus normal; distal part predominantly involvedUnderlies many cases of spontaneous pneumothorax in young adults
4. IrregularAcinus irregularly involved; almost invariably associated with scarringAirspace enlargement with fibrosis

🔴 Describe the etiopathogenesis of pulmonary emphysema.

Clinically significant emphysema is largely confined to smokers and patients with α₁-antitrypsin deficiency. Four mechanisms:

  1. Toxic injury and inflammation: Cigarette smoke damages respiratory epithelium → inflammation → parenchymal destruction. Inflammatory mediators (leukotriene B₄, IL-8, TNF) are released by epithelial cells and macrophages → attract inflammatory cells, amplify inflammation, induce structural changes.
  2. Protease–antiprotease imbalance: Inflammatory and epithelial cells release proteases (neutrophil elastase, cathepsin, matrix metalloproteinases [MMPs]) that break down connective tissue. In emphysema patients, there is relative deficiency of protective antiproteases (α₁-antitrypsin).
  3. Oxidative stress: Substances in tobacco smoke, alveolar damage, and inflammatory cells all produce oxidants → tissue damage, endothelial dysfunction, inflammation.
  4. Infection: Bacterial and/or viral infections do not initiate destruction but acutely exacerbate existing disease.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.680; Arif pp. 95–96

Mention the clinical features and causes of death of emphysema. What is pulmonary bullae?

Clinical features of emphysema:

  1. Dyspnoea (with characteristic "pursed-lip breathing")
  2. Obvious use of accessory muscles of respiration
  3. Cough with scanty mucoid sputum
  4. Barrel-shaped, hyperresonant chest
  5. Weight loss
  6. Expiratory airflow limitation (best measured through spirometry — key to diagnosis)

Patients are called "pink puffers" — they remain well oxygenated and have tachypnoea.

Cor pulmonale and eventually congestive heart failure (related to secondary pulmonary vascular hypertension; poor prognosis).

Causes of death:

  1. Respiratory acidosis and coma
  2. Acute respiratory failure
  3. Rupture of subpleural blebs → fatal pneumothorax

Nice to Know: Pulmonary bullae

Advanced cases show subpleural bullae — air-filled cyst-like structures >1 cm diameter, formed by rupture of adjacent air spaces. They are a common cause of spontaneous pneumothorax.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.682; Harsh Mohan 7e p.461; Arif pp. 96–97

7 · Chronic Bronchitis

🔴 Define chronic bronchitis. Describe its pathogenesis and clinical features.

Chronic bronchitis: Defined clinically as persistent cough with sputum production for at least 3 months in at least 2 consecutive years in the absence of any other identifiable cause.

Pathogenesis:

  1. Chronic irritation by inhaled substances (prolonged cigarette smoking in 90% of patients, atmospheric pollutants, dusts from grain/cotton/silica, chemicals & fumes)
  2. Proteases released from neutrophils (neutrophil elastase, cathepsin, MMP-4) impair ciliary movement
  3. Inhibits alveolar macrophage function
  4. Hypertrophy and hyperplasia of mucus-secreting submucosal glands and goblet cells
  5. Hypersecretion of mucus in the airways
  6. Acute and chronic inflammatory responses (neutrophils, lymphocytes, macrophages) → long-standing inflammation → bronchiolar wall fibrosis
  7. Thickened, oedematous bronchial wall
  8. Considerable obstruction of airways by mucus plugs with sputum overproduction

Bacterial, viral, and mycoplasmal infections do not initiate chronic bronchitis but occur secondary to it — they trigger acute exacerbations.

Clinical features:

  • Persistent cough with copious sputum production (90% smoker)
  • Recurrent respiratory infections
  • Dyspnoea (mild; more on exertion)
  • Hypercapnia, hypoxaemia, and mild cyanosis
  • Features of right heart failure (cor pulmonale)
  • Patients are called "blue bloaters" due to cyanosis and oedema
  • Chest X-ray shows enlarged heart with prominent vessels

⚠️ Long-standing chronic bronchitis commonly leads to pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale, and death due to heart failure.

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 680, 682; Arif pp. 97–99

Mention the treatment options of COPD.

Treatment of COPD:

  1. Smoking cessation (most important intervention)
  2. Oxygen therapy
  3. Long-acting bronchodilators with inhaled corticosteroids
  4. Antibiotics (for acute exacerbations)
  5. Physical therapy
  6. Bullectomy (for large bullae)
  7. Lung volume reduction surgery and lung transplantation (selected patients)

⚠️ Even with intervention, COPD often progresses and frequently proves fatal.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.682; Arif p. 99

8 · Bronchial Asthma

🔴 Define and classify asthma. What are the clinical features?

Asthma: A heterogeneous disease, usually characterised by chronic airway inflammation and variable expiratory airflow obstruction producing symptoms such as wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and cough, which vary over time and in intensity. Characterised by reversible bronchoconstriction caused by airway hyper-responsiveness.

Clinical features:

  • Recurrent episodes of wheezing (in response to allergen exposure)
  • Cough (particularly at night and/or in the early morning)
  • Breathlessness
  • Chest tightness

Classification:

1. Extrinsic (allergic/atopic) asthma — most common type; classic IgE-mediated (Type I) hypersensitivity reaction. Begins in childhood, family history of allergy. Triggered by dusts, pollens, cockroach or animal dander, moulds, foods.

2. Intrinsic (non-atopic) asthma — begins in adult life; no history of allergy. Triggers: respiratory infections (rhinovirus, parainfluenza, RSV), irritants (smoke, fumes), cold air, stress, exercise, drugs (aspirin/NSAIDs).

🔴 Describe the pathogenesis of atopic (extrinsic) asthma. What are the mediators?

Atopic asthma is caused by IgE-mediated (Type I) hypersensitivity reaction to environmental allergens in genetically predisposed individuals. Includes an early-phase (immediate hypersensitivity) reaction and a late-phase reaction.

Pathogenesis sequence:

  1. Initial sensitisation to inhaled antigens (house dust, pollens, danders, moulds)
  2. Stimulates Th₂ cells → release IL-4 & IL-5
  3. IL-4 promotes IgE production by B cells and mast-cell growth; IL-5 promotes eosinophil growth and activation
  4. Subsequent allergen exposure → IgE-coated mast cells → cross-linking → release chemical mediators
  5. Direct stimulation of subepithelial vagal (parasympathetic) receptors → bronchoconstriction

Mediators of the asthmatic response:

A. Major role in bronchospasm:

  • Leukotrienes C₄, D₄, E₄: prolonged bronchoconstriction, increased vascular permeability, increased mucus secretion
  • Acetylcholine (parasympathetic nerves): smooth-muscle constriction via muscarinic receptors
  • IL-5: peripheral blood eosinophilia
  • Galectin-10 (GAL10): released from eosinophils → forms Charcot-Leyden crystals → strong inducers of inflammation and mucus production

B. Minor role in bronchospasm: histamine (bronchoconstriction), prostaglandin D₂ (bronchoconstriction + vasodilatation), platelet-activating factor (platelet aggregation).

C. Others: IL-4, IL-13, TNF, eotaxin (CCL11), neuropeptides, nitric oxide, bradykinin, endothelins.

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 684; Arif pp. 100–102

🔴 Mention the histologic findings of asthma. What is airway remodelling?

Histologic findings of asthma:

  1. Occlusion of bronchi and bronchioles by thick, tenacious mucus plugs containing shed epithelium
  2. Curschmann spirals — result from extrusion of mucus plugs from subepithelial mucous gland ducts or bronchioles
  3. Charcot-Leyden crystals — composed of the eosinophil-derived protein galectin-10

"Airway remodelling" (additional findings):

  • Thickening of airway wall
  • Sub-basement membrane fibrosis (deposition of type-I and type-III collagens)
  • Increased vascularity
  • Increased size of submucosal glands and number of goblet cells
  • Hypertrophy and/or hyperplasia of bronchial wall muscle with increased extracellular matrix

Ref: Robbins 10e p.686; Arif pp. 102

9 · Bronchiectasis

🔴 Define bronchiectasis. Enumerate its causes and describe the pathogenesis.

Bronchiectasis: A disorder in which destruction of smooth muscle and elastic tissue by inflammation stemming from persistent or severe infections leads to permanent dilation of bronchi and bronchioles. Defined as abnormal, irreversible dilatation of the bronchi and bronchioles caused by chronic infection with inflammation and necrosis of the bronchial wall.

Causes / aetiology:

A) Congenital/hereditary (predispose to chronic infections):

  1. Cystic fibrosis (CF)
  2. Intralobar sequestration of the lung
  3. Immunodeficiency states
  4. Ciliary dysfunction syndromes — primary ciliary dyskinesia (immotile cilia syndrome); Kartagener syndrome (bronchiectasis + situs inversus + sinusitis)

B) Acquired:

  1. Post-infectious — severe necrotising pneumonia (M. tuberculosis, S. aureus, H. influenzae, Pseudomonas, adenovirus, HIV, Aspergillus)
  2. Bronchial obstruction — tumours, enlarged lymph nodes, foreign bodies, mucus impaction
  3. Immune disorders — rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, IBD, post-transplantation
  4. Idiopathic (up to 50%)

Pathogenesis:

Bronchial obstruction (mucus impaction, tumours, foreign bodies) → impaired clearing mechanisms → pooling of secretions → infections → inflammation with necrosis & fibrosis → eventual permanent dilatation of airways.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.687; Davidson's 23e p.578; Arif pp. 102–103

What is Kartagener syndrome? Describe its presentation and complications of bronchiectasis.

Kartagener syndrome

A ciliary dysfunction syndrome manifested by sinusitis + bronchiectasis + situs inversus (sometimes with hearing loss and male sterility). Caused by a defect in dynein arms of cilia — an autosomal recessive syndrome (primary ciliary dyskinesia). Impaired ciliary activity → infection in sinuses and bronchi + disturbed embryogenesis → situs inversus. Male infertility is an important manifestation.

Presentation of bronchiectasis:

  • Severe, persistent cough
  • Foul-smelling, sometimes bloody sputum expectoration
  • Dyspnoea and orthopnoea (severe cases)
  • Pleuritic chest pain
  • Haemoptysis (may be massive)
  • Recurrent pneumonia

Complications:

  • Clubbing of fingers
  • Metastatic brain abscess
  • Amyloidosis
  • Cor pulmonale

Morphology (gross):

Usually affects lower lobes bilaterally. Airways dilated up to 4× normal size. Types: cylindrical (most common — tube-like), fusiform (spindle-shaped), saccular (rounded sac-like), varicose (irregular). Cut surface shows characteristic honey-combed appearance.

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 687–688; Harsh Mohan 7e p.465; Arif pp. 103–104

10 · Restrictive Lung Diseases & Interstitial Lung Diseases (ILDs)

What is restrictive lung disease? Give examples. What are interstitial lung diseases?

Restrictive lung diseases: A group of disorders characterised by reduced expansion of lung parenchyma and decreased total lung capacity (TLC). Both TLC and FEV₁ decrease proportionately → normal FEV₁/FVC ratio.

Two broad kinds:

  1. Chest wall disorders — severe obesity, pleural diseases, kyphoscoliosis, neuromuscular diseases (poliomyelitis)
  2. Chronic interstitial and infiltrative diseases — pneumoconioses, interstitial fibrosis

Interstitial lung diseases (ILDs): A heterogeneous group of disorders characterised predominantly by inflammation and fibrosis of the lung interstitium associated with restrictive pulmonary function. Clinical features: dyspnoea, tachypnoea, end-inspiratory crackles, cyanosis. CT: nodules, irregular lines, ground-glass shadows. Eventually → secondary pulmonary hypertension → cor pulmonale.

Major categories of chronic ILDs:

  1. Fibrosing: usual interstitial pneumonia (idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [IPF]), nonspecific interstitial pneumonia, cryptogenic organising pneumonia, connective tissue disease-associated, pneumoconiosis, drug reactions, radiation pneumonitis
  2. Granulomatous: sarcoidosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis
  3. Eosinophilic
  4. Smoking-related: desquamative interstitial pneumonia, respiratory bronchiolitis-ILD
  5. Others: Langerhans cell histiocytosis, pulmonary alveolar proteinosis, lymphoid interstitial pneumonia

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 678, 688; Arif pp. 104–105

11 · Pneumoconiosis

Define pneumoconiosis. Classify lung diseases caused by air pollutants.

Pneumoconiosis: The non-neoplastic lung reaction to inhalation of mineral dusts encountered in the workplace, including disease induced by chemical fumes and vapours. Encompasses chronic fibrosing diseases of the lung resulting from exposure to organic and inorganic particulates.

12 · Pulmonary Embolism & Infarction

Describe pulmonary embolism and infarction — morphology, clinical features, and diagnosis.

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is the major cause of sudden death in hospitalised (bedridden) patients. Occlusion of the pulmonary artery is nearly always embolic; main source = deep vein thrombi of the leg (e.g. popliteal vein) in >95% of cases.

Morphology:

Macroscopic: Pulmonary infarct is classically haemorrhagic — appears as a raised, red-blue area in early stages.

Microscopic: Ischaemic necrosis of lung substance within the area of haemorrhage, affecting alveolar walls, bronchioles, and vessels.

Clinical features:

  • Large embolus in main pulmonary artery → instantaneous death
  • Small emboli → transient chest pain, cough, pulmonary haemorrhage

Complications (if untreated):

  • Pulmonary hypertension
  • Pulmonary vascular sclerosis
  • Chronic cor pulmonale

Laboratory diagnosis:

  • Serum D-dimer measurement
  • Ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) scanning
  • Chest X-ray (may be normal; wedge-shaped infiltrate after 12–36 hours)
  • CT pulmonary angiography (definitive diagnosis)

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 702; Arif pp. 106–107

13 · Pneumonia

🔴 Define and classify pneumonia. What are the aetiologic agents?

Pneumonia: Any infection of the lung parenchyma. An inflammatory process of infectious origin affecting the pulmonary parenchyma. Defined as acute inflammation of the lung parenchyma distal to the terminal bronchioles. "Consolidation" = the term for the gross and radiologic appearance of lungs in pneumonia.

Classification:

A) Anatomical (morphological): (1) Lobar pneumonia, (2) Bronchopneumonia.

B) Aetiologic agents:

  1. Bacterial (most common): Streptococcus pneumoniae, H. influenzae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila, Chlamydia pneumoniae/psittaci, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, M. tuberculosis, Moraxella catarrhalis
  2. Viral: influenza, parainfluenza, measles, RSV, coronavirus, CMV, mumps, rhinovirus, adenovirus, rubeola, varicella
  3. Fungal: Candida, actinomycosis, histoplasma
  4. Protozoal: Entamoeba histolytica, Toxoplasma gondii, Pneumocystis carinii
  5. Mycoplasmal: Mycoplasma pneumoniae
  6. Chlamydial: Chlamydia pneumoniae
  7. Rickettsial: typhus & Q-fever (Coxiella burnetii)

C) Host reaction: fibrinous, suppurative, serofibrinous.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.705; Arif pp. 107–108

🔴 Classify pneumonia syndrome (CAP, nosocomial, aspiration, chronic, immunocompromised).

Community-acquired acute pneumonia (CAP):

Streptococcus pneumoniae (most common cause of CAP — V.V.I.), H. influenzae, S. aureus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, C. pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila, Moraxella catarrhalis, Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas spp., C. psittaci, Coxiella burnetii (Q-fever), viruses (RSV, influenza A/B, parainfluenza, measles, adenovirus, coronavirus).

Predisposing conditions: extremes of age, chronic diseases (CHF, COPD, DM), immunodeficiency, absent splenic function (sickle cell, post-splenectomy).

Healthcare-associated pneumonia:

S. aureus (MSSA & MRSA), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, S. pneumoniae.

Nosocomial (hospital-acquired) pneumonia:

Gram-negative rods, Enterobacteriaceae (Klebsiella, E. coli), Pseudomonas spp., S. aureus (usually penicillin-resistant).

Aspiration pneumonia:

Anaerobic oral flora (Bacteroides, Prevotella, Fusobacterium, Peptostreptococcus) + aerobic bacteria (S. pneumoniae, S. aureus, H. influenzae, P. aeruginosa).

Chronic pneumonia:

Nocardia, Actinomyces, granulomatous (M. tuberculosis, atypical mycobacteria, Histoplasma, Coccidioides, Blastomyces).

Necrotising pneumonia and lung abscess:

Anaerobic bacteria (extremely common), S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, type 3 pneumococcus.

Pneumonia in the immunocompromised host:

CMV, Pneumocystis jirovecii, MAI (Mycobacterium avium-intracellulare), invasive aspergillosis, invasive candidiasis.

Routes of entry: (1) inhalation, (2) aspiration from naso/oropharynx, (3) haematogenous spread, (4) direct spread from adjacent infection.

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 705–706; Arif pp. 108–110

🔴 Define lobar pneumonia and bronchopneumonia. Discuss their pathogenesis.

Lobar pneumonia: Acute bacterial infection resulting in fibrino-suppurative consolidation of a large portion of a lobe or an entire lobe. Lower lobes most commonly affected.

Bronchopneumonia: Acute bacterial infection characterised by patchy consolidation of the lung (3–4 cm diameter), more often multilobar, often bilateral & basal.

Pathogenesis / predisposing factors of bacterial pneumonia:

A) Factors altering lung defence mechanisms:

  1. Altered consciousness — oropharyngeal aspiration (coma, cranial trauma, seizures, stroke, drug overdose, alcoholism)
  2. Loss/suppression of cough reflex (anaesthesia, neuromuscular disorders)
  3. Dysfunction of mucociliary apparatus — smoking, viral infections, hot/corrosive gases, genetic defects (immotile cilia syndrome)
  4. Accumulation of secretions (CF, bronchial obstruction)
  5. Impaired macrophage phagocytic/bactericidal activity (alcohol, tobacco smoke, anoxia, O₂ intoxication)
  6. Pulmonary congestion and oedema

B) Factors lowering host resistance: chronic diseases, immunologic deficiency, immunosuppressive agents, leukopenia.

14 · Lung Abscess

🔴 Short note: Lung abscess — causes, organisms, morphology, clinical features, complications.

Lung abscess: A localised area of suppuration within the lung parenchyma that leads to parenchymal destruction, manifested radiologically as a cavity with air-fluid level. A local suppurative process that produces necrosis of lung tissue.

Causes:

  1. Aspiration of infective material (most frequent) — risk factors: suppressed cough reflexes (alcohol intoxication, opioid abuse, coma, anaesthesia, seizures), severe dysphagia, protracted vomiting, poor dental hygiene
  2. Antecedent primary lung infection (postpneumonic — S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, pneumococcus)
  3. Septic embolism (thrombophlebitis, right-sided endocarditis vegetations)
  4. Bronchial obstruction (bronchogenic carcinoma, adenoma, foreign body)
  5. Local spread (oesophagus, spine, pleural cavity, liver/subphrenic abscess)
  6. Haematogenous spread (pelvic abscess, pyaemia, septicaemia)
  7. Miscellaneous (traumatic penetrations)

Causative organisms:

Aerobic/anaerobic streptococci, S. aureus, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella, Proteus, oral anaerobes (Bacteroides, Fusobacterium, Peptococcus).

Morphology:

Abscesses: few mm to 5–6 cm cavities; single or multiple. Histology: suppurative destruction of lung parenchyma within the central area of cavitation. Continued infection → large, poorly demarcated, fetid, green-black, multilocular cavities = gangrene of the lung. Chronic cases: fibroblastic proliferation → fibrous wall.

Clinical features:

  • Cough with copious foul-smelling purulent or sanguineous sputum
  • Fever, chest pain, weight loss
  • Occasional haemoptysis
  • Clubbing of fingers and toes
  • Radiographic evidence of fluid-filled cavity

Complications:

  • Extension of infection into pleural cavity
  • Haemorrhage
  • Brain abscess or meningitis (from septic emboli)
  • Secondary amyloidosis (chronic cases)

Ref: Robbins 10e p.711; Harsh Mohan 7e p.457; Arif pp. 114–115

15 · Pulmonary Tuberculosis (TB)

🔴 What are the types of pulmonary TB? Describe the pathogenesis of primary and secondary TB.

🫁 Pulmonary TB is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It is a leading cause of infectious death worldwide.

Types:

  1. Primary pulmonary TB — develops in a previously unexposed/unsensitised person
  2. Secondary pulmonary TB — develops in a previously sensitised host (reactivation or reinfection)

Pathogenesis — depends on two immune responses: (1) acquired cellular immunity, (2) delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH).

Primary infection:

  1. Inhalation of droplet nuclei containing tubercle bacilli → reach alveoli
  2. Non-specifically taken up by alveolar macrophages — but inactivated macrophages cannot destroy organisms
  3. Organisms multiply → macrophages burst → spread
  4. More macrophages & monocytes extravasate (also inactivated)
  5. Spread to regional (hilar) lymph nodes → lymphangitis → lymphadenitis
  6. Primary (Ghon) complex forms
  7. Haematogenous spread → miliary tuberculosis (affects various organs)

Secondary infection (re-infection):

  1. Cell-mediated immunity → sensitised T-cells secrete lymphokines
  2. Lymphokines activate macrophages → capable of killing and digesting bacilli
  3. Accumulation of activated macrophages at the lesion
  4. Macrophages transform into epithelioid cells
  5. Aggregation of epithelioid cells → giant cells (Langhans-type) + fibrosis
  6. Formation of tubercle → caseous necrosis → soft tubercle = hallmark of TB

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 368–370; Arif pp. 116–117

🔴 What is primary tuberculosis? What is Ghon's focus and Ghon's complex? What are the fates of primary TB?

Primary tuberculosis: The form of disease that develops in a previously unexposed and therefore unsensitised person. About 5% develop clinically significant disease; 90–95% remain asymptomatic. Source of organism is exogenous.

Primary sites of TB: (1) Lungs (mainly), (2) Intestine, (3) Skin, (4) Oropharyngeal, (5) Lymphoidal.

Ghon's complex

The lesion of primary TB, consisting of:

  1. Ghon's focus: the primary site of infection in the lung (sub-pleural region — lower part of upper lobe or upper part of lower lobe)
  2. Lymphangitis
  3. Hilar lymphadenitis (inflammation of hilar lymph nodes)

Yes, primary TB can occur more than once — in elderly and profoundly immunosuppressed (who lose their immunity; source is exogenous). Most people go on to have latent disease.

Fates of primary pulmonary TB:

  1. Complete resolution
  2. Healing with fibrosis & calcification
  3. Progressive pulmonary TB (or disseminated miliary TB)
  4. Progresses to secondary TB (re-infection)

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 369–371; Arif pp. 117–118

🔴 What is secondary TB? Mention the sites and differences between primary and secondary TB.

Sites of primary TB: sub-pleural region (lower part of upper lobe or upper part of lower lobe).

Sites of secondary TB: apex of the upper lobe of one or both lungs — high O₂ tension promotes bacterial growth.

Define miliary tuberculosis. Describe the pathology/morphology of pulmonary TB.

Miliary TB: Bacilli within macrophages spread to regional hilar lymph nodes → blood stream → distributes organisms to almost all parts of the body. Most prominent in liver, bone marrow, spleen, adrenals, meninges, kidneys, fallopian tubes & epididymis — but can involve any organ. Occurs when body immunity fails to control primary infection.

Pathology / morphology of pulmonary TB:

Primary TB:

  • Focal lesion in lung = Ghon's focus
  • Lymphangitis and hilar lymph node enlargement
  • Ghon's focus + nodal involvement = Ghon's complex
  • Gradually heals → calcified (Ghon's body)
  • If uncontained → bloodstream → miliary TB

Secondary TB — Gross:

  • Lesion at apex of one or both lungs
  • Starts as small focus of consolidation, usually <3 cm
  • Regional nodes develop similar tubercular foci

Secondary TB — Microscopic:

  1. Accumulation of activated macrophages → tubercle formation
  2. Organised aggregation of activated macrophages = epithelioid cells (pale, pink, granular cytoplasm)
  3. Epithelioid cells fuse → giant cells (40–50 μm): Langhans-type (peripheral nuclei) or foreign-body type (haphazard nuclei)
  4. Peripheral collar of fibroblasts, lymphocytes, macrophages surrounds the granuloma
  5. Central caseous necrosis → soft tubercle = characteristic hallmark of TB

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 371, 373; Arif pp. 119–120

Describe the formation of TB granuloma (soft tubercle) — immunopathogenesis.

Formation of tubercle / immunopathogenesis of TB granuloma:

  1. Causative agent (infectious or immunological) at the site
  2. Polymorphonuclear cells (neutrophils) arrive first
  3. After 24 hours, macrophages arrive
  4. When the inciting agent is poorly degradable/particulate, macrophages engulf → process → present to T lymphocytes → T-cell activation
  5. Responding T-cells produce IL-2 (activates other T-cells, perpetuating the response) and IFN-γ (activates macrophages → epithelioid cells & multinucleated giant cells)
  6. Accumulation of lymphocytes, fibroblasts, plasma cells
  7. Caseation necrosis occurs
  8. → Caseating granuloma / soft tubercle

Morphology of tubercle (microscopic): Organised aggregation of activated macrophages = epithelioid cells (pale, pink, granular cytoplasm) → fuse to form giant cells (40–50 μm; Langhans-type or foreign-body type) → peripheral collar of fibroblasts, lymphocytes, macrophages → central caseous necrosis = soft tubercle.

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 100, 371; Arif pp. 120–121

Mention the clinical features of pulmonary TB and how to diagnose it.

Clinical features of pulmonary TB:

  • Systemic: malaise, anorexia, weight loss, fever
  • Fever: low grade and remittent (appears late afternoon, subsides)
  • Night sweats
  • Persistent cough for ≥3 weeks with sputum (initially mucoid, later purulent)
  • Haemoptysis in about 50% of cases
  • Pleuritic chest pain
  • Extrapulmonary manifestations depend on organ involved

Diagnosis of pulmonary TB:

  1. History and physical examination
  2. Radiologic findings: consolidation/cavitation in lung apices
  3. Sputum for AFB (acid-fast bacilli) staining and culture — gold standard (culture on solid agar: 3–6 weeks; liquid media: ~2 weeks)
  4. ESR — high
  5. PCR / GeneXpert — can detect as few as 10 organisms (vs >10,000 for AFB smear) — confirmatory test
  6. FNAC and biopsy of lymph node (if present)
  7. Antibiotic susceptibility test (C/S)
  8. MT test / tuberculin skin test (not diagnostic by itself)

Staining: Ziehl-Neelsen (Z-N) stain. Culture: Lowenstein-Jensen (LJ) medium. Why early morning sample: secretions build up overnight → more bacilli.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.371; Arif pp. 121–122

Short note: Mantoux / tuberculin test.

Mantoux (tuberculin) test: Detects delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH) to M. tuberculosis antigens. No diagnostic value alone — indicates past or recent infection when positive. Becomes positive 2–4 weeks after infection.

  • Reagent: purified protein derivative (PPD). WHO recommended: PPD-RT-23 with Tween 80
  • Dose: 5 tuberculin units (TU) — 0.1 ml
  • Technique: intradermal injection on flexor aspect of forearm
  • Reading: after 72 hours (3rd day); induration measured in mm

Interpretation:

  • Positive: induration >10 mm (person infected but doesn't differentiate infection vs active disease)
  • Negative: <6 mm
  • Doubtful: 6–9 mm (may be M. tuberculosis or atypical mycobacteria)

False positive:

  • Prior BCG (Bacillus Calmette-Guerin) vaccination
  • Atypical mycobacterium infection

False negative:

  • Viral infections (HIV, measles, chickenpox)
  • Sarcoidosis, malnutrition, malignancy, Hodgkin lymphoma
  • Immunosuppression, overwhelming active TB, incorrect injection

🔴 Write down the morphology of bronchogenic squamous cell carcinoma. Describe its pathogenesis.

Pathogenesis: Chronic smoking → chronic irritation of columnar ciliated epithelium → squamous metaplasia (columnar cells replaced by stratified squamous epithelium) → dysplasia → carcinoma in situ → invasive squamous cell carcinoma.

Morphology — Macroscopic:

  • Location: central (most often around the hilum)
  • Types of growth: fungating, infiltrative, cauliflower-like
  • Colour: grey-white
  • Consistency: firm to hard

  • Keratinisation (keratin pearls) and/or intercellular bridges
  • Varying grades: well-differentiated, moderately-differentiated, poorly-differentiated

🔴 Write down the morphology of small cell carcinoma of lung. Why is it most dangerous?

Small cell carcinoma (formerly "oat cell" carcinoma): ~15% of all bronchogenic carcinoma.

Characteristics:

  • 99% smokers — strongest smoking association
  • Arise from neuroendocrine cells (Kulchitsky cells)
  • Highly malignant, rapidly growing (no known pre-invasive phase)
  • Early metastasis; inoperable at presentation
  • Usually responds to chemo/radiotherapy but rarely cured by surgery
  • Poor prognosis: survival 3 months to 1 year

Morphology — Macroscopic:

  • Central/hilar (most will have metastasised to hilar/mediastinal lymph nodes by diagnosis)
  • Colour: pale-grey

    • High mitotic count

Pneumothorax

Pneumothorax = air or gas in the pleural cavities. May be spontaneous, traumatic, or therapeutic.

Types:

  1. Spontaneous: any pulmonary disease causing alveolar rupture
    • Open pneumothorax
    • Closed pneumothorax
    • Tension pneumothorax — defect acts as a flap valve: air enters during inspiration but cannot escape during expiration → progressively increasing pressures → can compress vital mediastinal structures and contralateral lung
  2. Traumatic: penetrating chest injury, thoracic surgery
  3. Therapeutic / Iatrogenic: during pleural fluid aspiration
  4. Idiopathic in many cases

Most common causes of pneumothorax: (1) Emphysema, (2) Asthma, (3) Tuberculosis.

🎯 Define "hepatisation" in pneumonia. What is the most common cause of spontaneous pneumothorax in young adults? What is Kartagener syndrome?
