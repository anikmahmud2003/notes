🧠 Big Picture — Diseases & Tumours of the Skin (boss-level overview)

The skin is the largest organ of the body, built of two layers — epidermis + dermis (overlying the subcutis). Almost every skin disease can be filed under six big headings:

1. Disorders of pigmentation & melanocytes (Vitiligo · Albinism · Freckle · Melasma · Lentigo · Nevus=benign · Melanoma=malignant) · 2. Disorders of epidermal maturation (Ichthyosis, Urticaria) · 3. Inflammatory dermatoses — acute (Urticaria/hives, acute eczematous dermatitis, erythema multiforme) & chronic (Psoriasis, Seborrheic dermatitis, Lichen planus) · 4. Blistering (bullous) diseases — inflammatory (Pemphigus, Bullous pemphigoid, Dermatitis herpetiformis) & non-inflammatory (Epidermolysis bullosa, Porphyria) · 5. Disorders of epidermal appendages & infections (Acne, Rosacea; warts, Molluscum, Impetigo, superficial fungal infections) · 6. Tumours & tumour-like lesions.

Tumour triangles to remember: benign (squamous papilloma, seborrheic keratosis, keratoacanthoma, nevus) → pre-malignant (actinic/solar keratosis, Bowen disease, xeroderma pigmentosum, erythroplasia of Queyrat, leukoplakia, radiodermatitis, dysplastic nevus) → malignant (BCC=basal cell carcinoma "rodent ulcer" [most common], SCC=squamous cell carcinoma "Marjolin ulcer" [2nd most common], malignant melanoma=MM [most deadly], Merkel cell carcinoma). Locally malignant: BCC + dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans.

Robbins 10e, Ch 25 (The Skin), pp. 1133–1169 · Arif 15e (2025) Vol-2, pp.336–347

1 · Normal Structure of the Skin

Describe the normal structure of the skin (epidermis + dermis) and its cell types.

Normal skin = 2 layers: the epidermis (surface) and the dermis (deeper), lying over the subcutis (subcutaneous fat). The lining epithelium of skin is keratinized stratified squamous epithelium.

EPIDERMIS — 5 layers (base → surface)

  1. Stratum basale (stratum germinativum) — the basal (germinative) layer.
  2. Stratum spinosum — prickle-cell layer. (Basal + spinosum together = "basal layer".)
  3. Stratum granulosum — granular layer.
  4. Stratum lucidum — present exclusively in the palms and soles.
  5. Stratum corneum — superficial layer of dead keratinocytes, devoid of nuclei.

Four types of cells in the epidermis

CellKey feature
1. Squamous epithelial cells (keratinocytes)Main structural cells of the epidermis
2. MelanocytesResponsible for the production of melanin
3. Langerhans cellsIntraepidermal dendritic cells; bone-marrow-derived cells of the mononuclear–phagocytic system (i.e. antigen-presenting cells)
4. Merkel cellsNeuroendocrine/touch receptor cells

DERMIS — 2 parts

  1. Superficial — papillary dermis (contains papillary blood vessels; forms dermal papillae).
  2. Deeper — reticular dermis.

The dermis contains the cutaneous appendages (adnexal structures): sweat glands (eccrine & apocrine), sebaceous glands, hair follicles, arrectores pilorum (arrector pili muscles) and nails. The reticular dermis also holds the arrector pili muscle, hair root, apocrine and eccrine ducts/glands, as seen in the labelled skin section.

🎯 Epidermal layers base→surface: "Can Some Girls Let Curls Stay" → Corneum · Lucidum · Granulosum · Spinosum · (Germinativum). Lucidum = only in palms & soles (L for "L"imited / LUxury).

4 · Psoriasis (Chronic Inflammatory Dermatosis)

Short note: Psoriasis of the skin. (CMU-24M)

Psoriasis: a chronic inflammatory dermatosis affecting about 2% of the population.

Characteristics

  • Usually appears first between 15 and 30 years of age.
  • Lesions: brownish-red papules and plaques, sharply demarcated, covered with fine silvery-white scales.
  • When scales are removed by gentle scraping, fine bleeding points appear → "Auspitz sign".
  • Common sites: scalp, upper back, sacral region, and extensor surfaces of the extremities — especially knees and elbows.
  • In ~25% of cases, peculiar pitting of the nails is seen.
  • Psoriatic arthritis resembling rheumatoid arthritis occurs in ~5% of cases, but rheumatoid factor (RF) is absent.

Histopathology (fully developed lesions)

  • Acanthosis with regular downgrowth of rete ridges to almost the same dermal level, with thickening of their lower portion.
  • Elongation & oedema of the dermal papillae with broadening of their tips.
  • Suprapapillary thinning of the stratum malpighii.
  • Absence of the granular cell layer.
  • Prominent parakeratosis.
  • Presence of "Munro microabscesses" in the parakeratotic horny layer — diagnostic of psoriasis.
🎯 Psoriasis histo = REPAIR (Regular elongation of rete ridges + Absent granular layer + Munro microabscess + Parakeratosis + Auspitz sign). Joint = seronegative arthritis (RF absent).

6 · Tumours & Tumour-like Lesions of the Skin

Merged VIVA question — the classification of skin tumours covers all of: classify skin tumour, name surface epidermal tumours, malignant tumours of skin, name 3 malignant neoplasms, classify 5 common tumours of skin.

Origin of skin tumours: the skin is the largest organ of the body. Tumours & tumour-like lesions may arise from: surface epidermis, epidermal appendages, and dermal tissues. Each may give rise to benign tumours & tumour-like lesions, pre-malignant lesions, and malignant tumours. 7 · Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC / Rodent Ulcer)

Short note: Basal cell carcinoma / rodent ulcer. Give its etiology, pathogenesis and morphology. (CMU-23M/20Ju/19Nv, RMU-23M/19/18Nv, BUP-21Nv/M, SU-13Ja, DU-10/08Ju, RU-17Nv/Ja)

Basal cell carcinoma (BCC, "rodent ulcer"): malignant proliferation of the basal cell (stem cell) of the epidermis.

It is the most common of all malignant skin tumours, arising from the basal layer of the epidermis and its appendages. Typically a locally invasive, slow-growing tumour of middle age that rarely metastasises (i.e. a locally malignant tumour).

Sites (important for VIVA)

  • Chronic sun-exposed sites in lightly pigmented elderly adults.
  • Most commonly on the face (90%), usually above a line from the angle of the mouth up to the ear lobule.

Aetiology (etiology)

  • i) Age: mostly after 40 years.
  • ii) Race: lightly skinned races (little melanin) are more susceptible than Black individuals.
  • iii) Precancerous conditions: Xeroderma pigmentosum (XP), basal cell nevus syndrome.
  • iv) Environmental factor: chronic sun exposure (UV=ultraviolet radiation → DNA damage).

Morphology

Macroscopic

  1. Ulcerated (most common pattern): a slow-growing small nodule undergoes central ulceration with pearly, raised, rolled margins. The tumour enlarges by burrowing and destroying tissues locally like a rodent → hence "rodent ulcer".
  2. Non-ulcerated (less frequent pattern): pearly and shiny papules, often containing dilated (telangiectatic) vessels.

Microscopic

Tumour cells resemble those of the normal basal cell layer of the epidermis. Two growth patterns:

  • Multifocal growth (superficial spreading BCC): extends over the skin surface several square centimetres or more.
  • Nodular lesions (nodular BCC): growing downwards deeply into the dermis.

Metastasis

Rarely metastasises (locally malignant tumour).

🎯 BCC = "Basal Cell = Burrowing Cancer" — locally destructive, rarely spreads, rodent-ulcer with pearly rolled margins, face/upper sun sites, age >40, light skin.

Robbins 10e p.1147; Harsh Mohan 7e p.775

8 · Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC / Marjolin Ulcer)

Short note: Squamous cell carcinoma (Marjolin ulcer). Give its morphology. (CMU/RMJ-style VIVA)

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC, "Marjolin ulcer"): the second most common skin cancer, arising on sun-exposed sites in older people.

Incidence: higher in men than in women.

Pathogenesis (etiopathogenesis)

The most important cause of cutaneous SCC is DNA damage induced by exposure to UV light. Tumour incidence is proportional to the degree of lifetime sun exposure.

Predisposing factors (12)

  1. Prolonged sun exposure
  2. Immunosuppressive therapy
  3. Industrial carcinogens (tars and oils)
  4. Chronic ulcers and draining osteomyelitis
  5. Old burn scars (Marjolin ulcers)
  6. Arsenic exposure
  7. Psoriasis
  8. HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) infection
  9. Ionizing radiation
  10. (In the oral cavity) tobacco and betel-nut chewing
  11. Precancerous conditions — xeroderma pigmentosum, actinic keratosis
  12. Human papillomavirus (HPV)

Sites

May arise on any part of the skin and mucous membranes lined by squamous epithelium. Most common locations: the face, pinna of the ears, back of the hands, and mucocutaneous junctions such as the lips, anal canal and glans penis.

Morphology

Macroscopic — two patterns

  • More common: an ulcerated growth with elevated and indurated margin.
  • Less often: a raised fungating (polypoid) growth without ulceration.

Microscopic

  • SCC in situ (CIS): cells with atypical (enlarged and hyperchromatic) nuclei involving all levels of the epidermis.
  • Invasive SCC: variable degrees of differentiation — from tumours of polygonal cells in orderly lobules with numerous large areas of keratinization, to highly anaplastic neoplasms showing only abortive, single-cell keratinization (dyskeratosis).

Investigations

  1. Skin biopsy (shave biopsy, punch biopsy, or excisional biopsy)
  2. USG (ultrasonography) / CT (computed tomography) scan to identify nodal disease
  3. FNAC (fine-needle aspiration cytology) from the lymph node
  4. MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) to identify local extension
🎯 SCC = "Sun + Scar → Cancer". Marjolin ulcer = SCC in old burn scar. Sun-exposed sites, men>women, keratinizing cells, metastasises (unlike BCC). N.B.: SCC may arise in skin, oral cavity, oesophagus, lungs, urinary bladder, salivary gland, cervix.

Robbins 10e p.1146; Harsh Mohan 7e p.774

Probable diagnosis: Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) — ulcerative lesion on a chronic/sun-exposed site with regional (inguinal) lymph-node metastasis.

9 · Nevus (Benign Melanocytic Tumour)

Short note: Nevus / Melanocytic nevus (common mole). (DU-03S)

Nevus: the term denotes a benign tumour of melanocytes. Most melanocytic nevi are acquired.

Types of nevus

  1. Congenital nevus
  2. Blue nevus
  3. Spindle and epithelioid cell nevus (Spitz nevus)
  4. Halo nevus
  5. Dysplastic nevus (may transform into malignant melanoma)

Morphology

Gross

Tan to brown, uniformly pigmented, small (usually <6 mm across), solid regions of relatively flat (macules) to elevated skin (papules) with well-defined, rounded borders.

Microscopic

  • Nevus cells may be epithelioid, cuboidal, fusiform or dendritic.
  • Nuclei are uniform and rounded in contour, contain inconspicuous nucleoli, and show little or no mitotic activity.
🎯 Nevus = NEVer malignant (unless it becomes dysplastic → melanoma). Small (<6 mm), symmetric, uniform nuclei, no mitosis.

Robbins 10e p.1136; BRS Pathology 5e p.344

10 · Malignant Melanoma (MM)

Short note: Malignant melanoma / Melanoma. Give its morphology. (Very Important for VIVA; CU-16/14/12Ju, CMU-21M/15Ju)

Malignant melanoma (MM): the most deadly of all skin cancers, arising from melanocytes or nevus cells.

Incidence: most common in fair-skinned persons; can occur at all ages.

Predisposing factors

  1. Excessive exposure of white skin to sunlight
  2. Persistent change in the appearance of a mole
  3. Presence of a pre-existing naevus (especially dysplastic naevus)
  4. Family history of melanoma in a patient with atypical mole
  5. Higher age of the patient
  6. Exposure to certain carcinogens

Sites

  • Skin (men: upper back; women: back and the leg); other locations — face, soles, palms and nail-beds.
  • Oral and anogenital mucosa (oropharynx, gastrointestinal and genitourinary tracts).
  • Oesophagus · Conjunctiva · Orbit · Meninges.

Clinical features

  1. Usually asymptomatic.
  2. Itching or pain may be early manifestations.
  3. Majority of lesions are greater than 10 mm in diameter.
  4. Change in colour, size, or shape in a pigmented lesion is the most consistent clinical sign.

Clinical warning signs — the ABCDEs of melanoma

  • Asymmetry
  • B — Irregular Borders
  • C — Change in Colour
  • D — Increasing Diameter
  • E — Evolution / change over time, especially if rapid

Other warning features

  • Pigmented lesions >6 mm in diameter
  • Any change in appearance
  • New onset of itching or pain

Growth pattern

  1. Radial growth (initial phase): growth in all directions but predominantly lateral, within the epidermis and papillary zone of the dermis. During this stage melanomas do NOT metastasise; clinical cure is frequent.
  2. Vertical growth (later phase): growth extends into the reticular dermis or beyond. Prognosis varies with the depth of the lesion. During this phase, lymphatic or haematogenous metastasis may occur.

Morphology

Macroscopic

  • Striking variation in colour — shades of black, brown, red, dark blue, and gray.
  • Occasionally zones of white or flesh-coloured hypopigmentation appear.
  • Borders are irregular and often notched.

Microscopic

  • Melanoma cells are larger than normal with large nuclei, irregular contours and prominent red (eosinophilic) nucleoli.
  • The appearance of the tumour cells is similar in radial and vertical phases.
🎯 Melanoma mnemonics: ABCDE = Asymmetry, Border, Colour, Diameter, Evolution. Growth = "Radial first (safe), Vertical later (dangerous)". Most deadly skin cancer, fair skin, UV exposure.

Robbins 10e p.1139; BRS Pathology 5e p.347; Harsh Mohan 7e p.778
