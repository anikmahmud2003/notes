# Ch 04 — Respiratory System     <i>Full-detail flashcards (Q→A) · All abbreviations expanded · Robbins 4-pillar answers</i>
### 🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of the Respiratory System (boss-level overview)
🫁 The respiratory system spans conducting airways → respiratory bronchioles → alveoli → pleura. Disease can affect <b>any level</b>:
📌 <b>Classification (Arif pp. 88–89):</b>
- 1. Congenital anomalies — pulmonary hypoplasia, foregut cysts, sequestration, tracheo-oesophageal fistula, congenital lobar overinflation
- 2. Atelectasis (collapse) — resorption, compression, contraction
- 3. Pulmonary oedema — cardiogenic (haemodynamic) vs non-cardiogenic
- 4. Acute lung injury (ALI) & ARDS — "shock lung"
- 5. Obstructive lung diseases — 🔴 COPD (emphysema + chronic bronchitis), asthma, bronchiectasis → ↓FEV₁/FVC (<0.7)
- 6. Restrictive / interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) — pneumoconioses, sarcoidosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hypersensitivity pneumonitis → ↓TLC with normal FEV₁/FVC
- 7. Vascular diseases — pulmonary embolism (PE), pulmonary infarction, pulmonary hypertension
- 8. Pulmonary infections — 🔴 pneumonia (lobar, broncho), lung abscess, pulmonary TB
- 9. Lung tumours — 🔴 bronchogenic carcinoma (90–95% of malignancies), carcinoid, hamartoma; secondary mets > primary
- 10. Pleural diseases — effusion, pneumothorax, mesothelioma
🎯 <b>One-liner link:</b> Smoking is the single unifying risk factor connecting COPD, emphysema, chronic bronchitis, and bronchogenic carcinoma.
Robbins 10e, Ch 15 (Lung), pp. 673–727 · Arif Guide 2025 Vol-2, pp. 88–135

## 1 · Causes of Haemoptysis
<b>Mention the causes of haemoptysis.</b>

🔴 <b>Haemoptysis = coughing up blood from the respiratory tract.</b> Causes are grouped into three categories (VIVA favourite):

A. Inflammatory

Bronchitis
Bronchiectasis
Pulmonary tuberculosis (TB)
Lung abscess
Suppurative pneumonia

B. Neoplastic

Bronchial carcinoma
Bronchial adenoma (carcinoid tumour)

C. Others

Pulmonary embolism (PE)
Acute left ventricular failure (LVF)
Mitral stenosis
Trauma
Foreign bodies
Primary pulmonary hypertension
Haemorrhagic diathesis (coagulopathy)

Ref: Harsh Mohan 7e p.480; Arif pp. 90

## 2 · Atelectasis (Collapse)
<b>Define and describe atelectasis (collapse). What are its types?</b>

<b>Atelectasis (collapse)</b> refers either to incomplete expansion of the lungs (neonatal atelectasis) or to the collapse of previously inflated lung, producing areas of relatively airless pulmonary parenchyma.

Types:

Resorption (obstruction) atelectasis — consequence of complete airway obstruction → air trapped distal alveoli is absorbed, without impairment of blood flow. Airway obstruction caused by mucus plugs (asthma, chronic bronchitis, postoperative states), aspiration of foreign bodies, intrabronchial tumours.
Compression atelectasis — significant volumes of fluid (transudate, exudate, blood), tumour, or air (pneumothorax) accumulate in the pleural cavity → compresses lung.
Contraction atelectasis — focal or generalised pulmonary or pleural fibrosis prevents full lung expansion.

⚠️ Significant atelectasis reduces oxygenation and predisposes to infection.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.675; Arif p. 90–91

## 3 · Pulmonary Oedema
<b>Define and classify pulmonary oedema. What are the causes?</b>

<b>Pulmonary oedema</b> is the accumulation of excessive interstitial fluid in the alveoli, resulting from either haemodynamic disturbances (cardiogenic) or increased capillary permeability due to microvascular injury (non-cardiogenic).

Classification and causes:

1. Haemodynamic oedema

<b>i) Increased hydrostatic pressure (↑pulmonary venous pressure):</b>

Left-sided heart failure (most common)
Volume overload
Pulmonary vein obstruction

<b>ii) Decreased oncotic pressure:</b>

Hypoalbuminaemia
Nephrotic syndrome
Liver disease
Protein-losing enteropathies

<b>iii) Lymphatic obstruction (rare).</b>

2. Oedema due to alveolar wall injury (microvascular/epithelial injury)

<b>i) Direct injury:</b>

Infections (bacterial pneumonia)
Inhaled gases (high concentration O₂, smoke)
Liquid aspiration (gastric contents, near-drowning)
Radiation
Lung trauma

<b>ii) Indirect injury:</b>

Systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) — sepsis, burns, pancreatitis, extensive trauma
Blood transfusion-related
Drugs: bleomycin, methadone, amphotericin B, heroin, cocaine, kerosene, paraquat

3. Oedema of undetermined origin

High altitude
Neurogenic (CNS trauma)

Ref: Robbins 10e p.675; Arif pp. 91–92

## 4 · Acute Lung Injury (ALI) & Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)
<b>🔴 Define ALI and ARDS. What conditions are associated? What are the clinical features?</b>

<b>Acute lung injury (ALI)</b> = abrupt onset of hypoxaemia and bilateral pulmonary oedema in the absence of cardiac failure (non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema).

<b>ARDS</b> = a manifestation of <b>severe ALI</b>; a clinical syndrome of progressive respiratory insufficiency caused by diffuse alveolar damage (DAD) in the setting of sepsis, severe trauma, or diffuse pulmonary infection.

🎯 Synonyms: "shock lung," diffuse alveolar damage, acute alveolar injury.

<i>Both ARDS and ALI are associated with inflammation-associated increases in pulmonary vascular permeability, oedema, and epithelial cell death. Histological hallmark = <b>diffuse alveolar damage (DAD)</b>.</i>

Associated conditions:

Infection: sepsis, diffuse pulmonary infections (viral, Mycoplasma, Pneumocystis pneumonia, miliary TB), gastric aspiration
Physical/injury: mechanical trauma (head injuries, pulmonary contusions, near-drowning, fat embolism), burns, ionising radiation
Inhaled irritants: O₂ toxicity, smoke, irritant gases/chemicals
Chemical injury: heroin/methadone overdose, acetylsalicylic acid, barbiturate overdose, paraquat
Haematological: transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
Others: pancreatitis, uraemia, cardiopulmonary bypass, hypersensitivity reactions

Clinical features:

Profound dyspnoea and tachypnoea
Cyanosis and hypoxaemia
Respiratory failure
Diffuse bilateral infiltrates on chest X-ray

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 676–678; Arif pp. 92–93

## 5 · Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD)
<b>🔴 Define obstructive lung disease. List the obstructive lung diseases. Define COPD and its manifestations.</b>

<b>Obstructive lung diseases</b> are characterised by an increase in resistance to airflow due to diffuse airway disease affecting any level of the respiratory tract. An <b>FEV₁/FVC ratio <0.7</b> generally indicates obstructive disease.

Common obstructive lung diseases:

COPD — emphysema + chronic bronchitis
Bronchial asthma
Bronchiectasis

<b>COPD (Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease):</b> Defined by WHO as "a common, preventable and treatable disease characterised by persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow limitation that is due to airway and/or alveolar abnormalities caused by exposure to noxious particles or gases." Currently the <b>4th leading cause of death in the world</b>.

Characteristics: marked decrease in FEV₁ and an increased or normal forced vital capacity (FVC) → decreased FEV₁:FVC ratio.

<b>Two major clinicopathologic manifestations:</b> (1) Emphysema and (2) Chronic bronchitis. <i>These are often found together because they share the same major aetiologic factor — cigarette smoking.</i>

Etiological factors for COPD:

Cigarette smoking (most important)
Poor lung development early in life
Environmental and occupational pollutants
Airway hyperresponsiveness
Genetic polymorphisms (e.g. α₁-antitrypsin deficiency)

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 675, 678; BRS Pathology 5e p.202; Arif pp. 93–94

## 6 · Emphysema
<b>🔴 Define and classify emphysema. Describe the types.</b>

<b>Emphysema:</b> A chronic obstructive airway disease characterised by <b>irreversible enlargement of airspaces distal to the terminal bronchiole</b>, accompanied by destruction of their walls without obvious fibrosis. Also: dilation of air spaces with destruction of alveolar walls and lack of elastic recoil.

Necessary lung anatomy (pulmonary acinus):

The part of the lung distal to a <b>terminal bronchiole</b> is called an <b>acinus</b> (lobule = cluster of acini). An acinus has three parts: respiratory bronchioles → alveolar ducts → alveolar sacs (alveoli).

Types of emphysema (4 major types):

<table>
<tr><th>Type</th><th>Affected area</th><th>Key points</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>1. Centriacinar (centrilobular)</strong></td><td>Central/proximal parts of the acinus (respiratory bronchioles); distal alveoli spared</td><td><span class="hot">95% of cases</span>; predominant in heavy smokers; often associated with chronic bronchitis</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>2. Panacinar (panlobular)</strong></td><td>Uniformly enlarged from respiratory bronchiole to terminal alveoli</td><td>Associated with <span class="hot">α₁-antitrypsin deficiency</span>; exacerbated by smoking</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>3. Distal acinar (paraseptal)</strong></td><td>Proximal acinus normal; distal part predominantly involved</td><td>Underlies many cases of <strong>spontaneous pneumothorax</strong> in young adults</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>4. Irregular</strong></td><td>Acinus irregularly involved; almost invariably associated with scarring</td><td>Airspace enlargement with fibrosis</td></tr>
</table>

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 675, 679; Arif pp. 94–95

<b>🔴 Describe the etiopathogenesis of pulmonary emphysema.</b>

Clinically significant emphysema is largely confined to <b>smokers</b> and patients with <b>α₁-antitrypsin deficiency</b>. Four mechanisms:

Toxic injury and inflammation: Cigarette smoke damages respiratory epithelium → inflammation → parenchymal destruction. Inflammatory mediators (leukotriene B₄, IL-8, TNF) are released by epithelial cells and macrophages → attract inflammatory cells, amplify inflammation, induce structural changes.
Protease–antiprotease imbalance: Inflammatory and epithelial cells release proteases (neutrophil elastase, cathepsin, matrix metalloproteinases [MMPs]) that break down connective tissue. In emphysema patients, there is relative deficiency of protective antiproteases (α₁-antitrypsin).
Oxidative stress: Substances in tobacco smoke, alveolar damage, and inflammatory cells all produce oxidants → tissue damage, endothelial dysfunction, inflammation.
Infection: Bacterial and/or viral infections do not initiate destruction but acutely exacerbate existing disease.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.680; Arif pp. 95–96

<b>Mention the clinical features and causes of death of emphysema. What is pulmonary bullae?</b>

Clinical features of emphysema:

Dyspnoea (with characteristic "pursed-lip breathing")
Obvious use of accessory muscles of respiration
Cough with scanty mucoid sputum
Barrel-shaped, hyperresonant chest
Weight loss
Expiratory airflow limitation (best measured through spirometry — key to diagnosis)

Patients are called <b>"pink puffers"</b> — they remain well oxygenated and have tachypnoea.

<b>Cor pulmonale</b> and eventually congestive heart failure (related to secondary pulmonary vascular hypertension; poor prognosis).

Causes of death:

Respiratory acidosis and coma
Acute respiratory failure
Rupture of subpleural blebs → fatal pneumothorax

Nice to Know: Pulmonary bullae

Advanced cases show <b>subpleural bullae</b> — air-filled cyst-like structures >1 cm diameter, formed by rupture of adjacent air spaces. They are a common cause of <b>spontaneous pneumothorax</b>.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.682; Harsh Mohan 7e p.461; Arif pp. 96–97

## 7 · Chronic Bronchitis
<b>🔴 Define chronic bronchitis. Describe its pathogenesis and clinical features.</b>

<b>Chronic bronchitis:</b> Defined clinically as <b>persistent cough with sputum production for at least 3 months in at least 2 consecutive years</b> in the absence of any other identifiable cause.

Pathogenesis:

Chronic irritation by inhaled substances (prolonged cigarette smoking in 90% of patients, atmospheric pollutants, dusts from grain/cotton/silica, chemicals & fumes)
Proteases released from neutrophils (neutrophil elastase, cathepsin, MMP-4) impair ciliary movement
Inhibits alveolar macrophage function
Hypertrophy and hyperplasia of mucus-secreting submucosal glands and goblet cells
Hypersecretion of mucus in the airways
Acute and chronic inflammatory responses (neutrophils, lymphocytes, macrophages) → long-standing inflammation → bronchiolar wall fibrosis
Thickened, oedematous bronchial wall
Considerable obstruction of airways by mucus plugs with sputum overproduction

<i>Bacterial, viral, and mycoplasmal infections do not initiate chronic bronchitis but occur secondary to it — they trigger acute exacerbations.</i>

Clinical features:

Persistent cough with copious sputum production (90% smoker)
Recurrent respiratory infections
Dyspnoea (mild; more on exertion)
Hypercapnia, hypoxaemia, and mild cyanosis
Features of right heart failure (cor pulmonale)
Patients are called "blue bloaters" due to cyanosis and oedema
Chest X-ray shows enlarged heart with prominent vessels

⚠️ Long-standing chronic bronchitis commonly leads to pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale, and <b>death due to heart failure</b>.

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 680, 682; Arif pp. 97–99

<b>Mention the treatment options of COPD.</b>

Treatment of COPD:

Smoking cessation (most important intervention)
Oxygen therapy
Long-acting bronchodilators with inhaled corticosteroids
Antibiotics (for acute exacerbations)
Physical therapy
Bullectomy (for large bullae)
Lung volume reduction surgery and lung transplantation (selected patients)

⚠️ Even with intervention, COPD often progresses and frequently proves fatal.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.682; Arif p. 99

## 8 · Bronchial Asthma
<b>🔴 Define and classify asthma. What are the clinical features?</b>

<b>Asthma:</b> A heterogeneous disease, usually characterised by chronic airway inflammation and <b>variable expiratory airflow obstruction</b> producing symptoms such as wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and cough, which vary over time and in intensity. Characterised by reversible bronchoconstriction caused by airway hyper-responsiveness.

Clinical features:

Recurrent episodes of wheezing (in response to allergen exposure)
Cough (particularly at night and/or in the early morning)
Breathlessness
Chest tightness

Classification:

<b>1. Extrinsic (allergic/atopic) asthma</b> — most common type; classic <b>IgE-mediated (Type I) hypersensitivity</b> reaction. Begins in childhood, family history of allergy. Triggered by dusts, pollens, cockroach or animal dander, moulds, foods.

<b>2. Intrinsic (non-atopic) asthma</b> — begins in adult life; no history of allergy. Triggers: respiratory infections (rhinovirus, parainfluenza, RSV), irritants (smoke, fumes), cold air, stress, exercise, drugs (aspirin/NSAIDs).

<b>🔴 Describe the pathogenesis of atopic (extrinsic) asthma. What are the mediators?</b>

Atopic asthma is caused by <b>IgE-mediated (Type I) hypersensitivity</b> reaction to environmental allergens in genetically predisposed individuals. Includes an <b>early-phase</b> (immediate hypersensitivity) reaction and a <b>late-phase</b> reaction.

Pathogenesis sequence:

Initial sensitisation to inhaled antigens (house dust, pollens, danders, moulds)
Stimulates Th₂ cells → release IL-4 & IL-5
IL-4 promotes IgE production by B cells and mast-cell growth; IL-5 promotes eosinophil growth and activation
Subsequent allergen exposure → IgE-coated mast cells → cross-linking → release chemical mediators
Direct stimulation of subepithelial vagal (parasympathetic) receptors → bronchoconstriction

Mediators of the asthmatic response:

<b>A. Major role in bronchospasm:</b>

Leukotrienes C₄, D₄, E₄: prolonged bronchoconstriction, increased vascular permeability, increased mucus secretion
Acetylcholine (parasympathetic nerves): smooth-muscle constriction via muscarinic receptors
IL-5: peripheral blood eosinophilia
Galectin-10 (GAL10): released from eosinophils → forms Charcot-Leyden crystals → strong inducers of inflammation and mucus production

<b>B. Minor role in bronchospasm:</b> histamine (bronchoconstriction), prostaglandin D₂ (bronchoconstriction + vasodilatation), platelet-activating factor (platelet aggregation).

<b>C. Others:</b> IL-4, IL-13, TNF, eotaxin (CCL11), neuropeptides, nitric oxide, bradykinin, endothelins.

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 684; Arif pp. 100–102

<b>🔴 Mention the histologic findings of asthma. What is airway remodelling?</b>

Histologic findings of asthma:

Occlusion of bronchi and bronchioles by thick, tenacious mucus plugs containing shed epithelium
Curschmann spirals — result from extrusion of mucus plugs from subepithelial mucous gland ducts or bronchioles
Charcot-Leyden crystals — composed of the eosinophil-derived protein galectin-10

"Airway remodelling" (additional findings):

Thickening of airway wall
Sub-basement membrane fibrosis (deposition of type-I and type-III collagens)
Increased vascularity
Increased size of submucosal glands and number of goblet cells
Hypertrophy and/or hyperplasia of bronchial wall muscle with increased extracellular matrix

Ref: Robbins 10e p.686; Arif pp. 102

## 9 · Bronchiectasis
<b>🔴 Define bronchiectasis. Enumerate its causes and describe the pathogenesis.</b>

<b>Bronchiectasis:</b> A disorder in which destruction of smooth muscle and elastic tissue by inflammation stemming from persistent or severe infections leads to <b>permanent dilation of bronchi and bronchioles</b>. Defined as abnormal, irreversible dilatation of the bronchi and bronchioles caused by chronic infection with inflammation and necrosis of the bronchial wall.

Causes / aetiology:

<b>A) Congenital/hereditary (predispose to chronic infections):</b>

Cystic fibrosis (CF)
Intralobar sequestration of the lung
Immunodeficiency states
Ciliary dysfunction syndromes — primary ciliary dyskinesia (immotile cilia syndrome); Kartagener syndrome (bronchiectasis + situs inversus + sinusitis)

<b>B) Acquired:</b>

Post-infectious — severe necrotising pneumonia (M. tuberculosis, S. aureus, H. influenzae, Pseudomonas, adenovirus, HIV, Aspergillus)
Bronchial obstruction — tumours, enlarged lymph nodes, foreign bodies, mucus impaction
Immune disorders — rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, IBD, post-transplantation
Idiopathic (up to 50%)

Pathogenesis:

Bronchial obstruction (mucus impaction, tumours, foreign bodies) → impaired clearing mechanisms → pooling of secretions → infections → inflammation with necrosis & fibrosis → eventual permanent dilatation of airways.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.687; Davidson's 23e p.578; Arif pp. 102–103

<b>What is Kartagener syndrome? Describe its presentation and complications of bronchiectasis.</b>

Kartagener syndrome

A ciliary dysfunction syndrome manifested by <b>sinusitis + bronchiectasis + situs inversus</b> (sometimes with hearing loss and male sterility). Caused by a defect in dynein arms of cilia — an autosomal recessive syndrome (primary ciliary dyskinesia). Impaired ciliary activity → infection in sinuses and bronchi + disturbed embryogenesis → situs inversus. Male infertility is an important manifestation.

Presentation of bronchiectasis:

Severe, persistent cough
Foul-smelling, sometimes bloody sputum expectoration
Dyspnoea and orthopnoea (severe cases)
Pleuritic chest pain
Haemoptysis (may be massive)
Recurrent pneumonia

Complications:

Clubbing of fingers
Metastatic brain abscess
Amyloidosis
Cor pulmonale

Morphology (gross):

Usually affects lower lobes bilaterally. Airways dilated up to 4× normal size. Types: cylindrical (most common — tube-like), fusiform (spindle-shaped), saccular (rounded sac-like), varicose (irregular). Cut surface shows characteristic <b>honey-combed appearance</b>.

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 687–688; Harsh Mohan 7e p.465; Arif pp. 103–104

## 10 · Restrictive Lung Diseases & Interstitial Lung Diseases (ILDs)
<b>What is restrictive lung disease? Give examples. What are interstitial lung diseases?</b>

<b>Restrictive lung diseases:</b> A group of disorders characterised by <b>reduced expansion of lung parenchyma and decreased total lung capacity (TLC)</b>. Both TLC and FEV₁ decrease proportionately → <b>normal FEV₁/FVC ratio</b>.

Two broad kinds:

Chest wall disorders — severe obesity, pleural diseases, kyphoscoliosis, neuromuscular diseases (poliomyelitis)
Chronic interstitial and infiltrative diseases — pneumoconioses, interstitial fibrosis

<b>Interstitial lung diseases (ILDs):</b> A heterogeneous group of disorders characterised predominantly by <b>inflammation and fibrosis of the lung interstitium</b> associated with restrictive pulmonary function. Clinical features: dyspnoea, tachypnoea, end-inspiratory crackles, cyanosis. CT: nodules, irregular lines, ground-glass shadows. Eventually → secondary pulmonary hypertension → cor pulmonale.

Major categories of chronic ILDs:

Fibrosing: usual interstitial pneumonia (idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [IPF]), nonspecific interstitial pneumonia, cryptogenic organising pneumonia, connective tissue disease-associated, pneumoconiosis, drug reactions, radiation pneumonitis
Granulomatous: sarcoidosis, hypersensitivity pneumonitis
Eosinophilic
Smoking-related: desquamative interstitial pneumonia, respiratory bronchiolitis-ILD
Others: Langerhans cell histiocytosis, pulmonary alveolar proteinosis, lymphoid interstitial pneumonia

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 678, 688; Arif pp. 104–105

## 11 · Pneumoconiosis
<b>Define pneumoconiosis. Classify lung diseases caused by air pollutants.</b>

<b>Pneumoconiosis:</b> The non-neoplastic lung reaction to inhalation of mineral dusts encountered in the workplace, including disease induced by chemical fumes and vapours. Encompasses chronic fibrosing diseases of the lung resulting from exposure to organic and inorganic particulates.

## 12 · Pulmonary Embolism & Infarction
<b>Describe pulmonary embolism and infarction — morphology, clinical features, and diagnosis.</b>

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is the <b>major cause of sudden death in hospitalised (bedridden) patients</b>. Occlusion of the pulmonary artery is nearly always embolic; main source = <b>deep vein thrombi of the leg</b> (e.g. popliteal vein) in >95% of cases.

Morphology:

<b>Macroscopic:</b> Pulmonary infarct is classically <b>haemorrhagic</b> — appears as a raised, red-blue area in early stages.

<b>Microscopic:</b> Ischaemic necrosis of lung substance within the area of haemorrhage, affecting alveolar walls, bronchioles, and vessels.

Clinical features:

Large embolus in main pulmonary artery → instantaneous death
Small emboli → transient chest pain, cough, pulmonary haemorrhage

Complications (if untreated):

Pulmonary hypertension
Pulmonary vascular sclerosis
Chronic cor pulmonale

Laboratory diagnosis:

Serum D-dimer measurement
Ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) scanning
Chest X-ray (may be normal; wedge-shaped infiltrate after 12–36 hours)
CT pulmonary angiography (definitive diagnosis)

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 702; Arif pp. 106–107

## 13 · Pneumonia
<b>🔴 Define and classify pneumonia. What are the aetiologic agents?</b>

<b>Pneumonia:</b> Any infection of the lung parenchyma. An inflammatory process of infectious origin affecting the pulmonary parenchyma. Defined as acute inflammation of the lung parenchyma distal to the terminal bronchioles. "Consolidation" = the term for the gross and radiologic appearance of lungs in pneumonia.

Classification:

<b>A) Anatomical (morphological):</b> (1) Lobar pneumonia, (2) Bronchopneumonia.

<b>B) Aetiologic agents:</b>

Bacterial (most common): Streptococcus pneumoniae, H. influenzae, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila, Chlamydia pneumoniae/psittaci, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, M. tuberculosis, Moraxella catarrhalis
Viral: influenza, parainfluenza, measles, RSV, coronavirus, CMV, mumps, rhinovirus, adenovirus, rubeola, varicella
Fungal: Candida, actinomycosis, histoplasma
Protozoal: Entamoeba histolytica, Toxoplasma gondii, Pneumocystis carinii
Mycoplasmal: Mycoplasma pneumoniae
Chlamydial: Chlamydia pneumoniae
Rickettsial: typhus & Q-fever (Coxiella burnetii)

<b>C) Host reaction:</b> fibrinous, suppurative, serofibrinous.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.705; Arif pp. 107–108

<b>🔴 Classify pneumonia syndrome (CAP, nosocomial, aspiration, chronic, immunocompromised).</b>

Community-acquired acute pneumonia (CAP):

<b>Streptococcus pneumoniae</b> (most common cause of CAP — V.V.I.), H. influenzae, S. aureus, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, C. pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila, Moraxella catarrhalis, Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonas spp., C. psittaci, Coxiella burnetii (Q-fever), viruses (RSV, influenza A/B, parainfluenza, measles, adenovirus, coronavirus).

<i>Predisposing conditions: extremes of age, chronic diseases (CHF, COPD, DM), immunodeficiency, absent splenic function (sickle cell, post-splenectomy).</i>

Healthcare-associated pneumonia:

S. aureus (MSSA & MRSA), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, S. pneumoniae.

Nosocomial (hospital-acquired) pneumonia:

Gram-negative rods, Enterobacteriaceae (Klebsiella, E. coli), Pseudomonas spp., S. aureus (usually penicillin-resistant).

Aspiration pneumonia:

Anaerobic oral flora (Bacteroides, Prevotella, Fusobacterium, Peptostreptococcus) + aerobic bacteria (S. pneumoniae, S. aureus, H. influenzae, P. aeruginosa).

Chronic pneumonia:

Nocardia, Actinomyces, granulomatous (M. tuberculosis, atypical mycobacteria, Histoplasma, Coccidioides, Blastomyces).

Necrotising pneumonia and lung abscess:

Anaerobic bacteria (extremely common), S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, S. pyogenes, type 3 pneumococcus.

Pneumonia in the immunocompromised host:

CMV, Pneumocystis jirovecii, MAI (Mycobacterium avium-intracellulare), invasive aspergillosis, invasive candidiasis.

<i>Routes of entry: (1) inhalation, (2) aspiration from naso/oropharynx, (3) haematogenous spread, (4) direct spread from adjacent infection.</i>

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 705–706; Arif pp. 108–110

<b>🔴 Define lobar pneumonia and bronchopneumonia. Discuss their pathogenesis.</b>

<b>Lobar pneumonia:</b> Acute bacterial infection resulting in <b>fibrino-suppurative consolidation of a large portion of a lobe or an entire lobe</b>. Lower lobes most commonly affected.

<b>Bronchopneumonia:</b> Acute bacterial infection characterised by <b>patchy consolidation of the lung</b> (3–4 cm diameter), more often multilobar, often bilateral & basal.

Pathogenesis / predisposing factors of bacterial pneumonia:

<b>A) Factors altering lung defence mechanisms:</b>

Altered consciousness — oropharyngeal aspiration (coma, cranial trauma, seizures, stroke, drug overdose, alcoholism)
Loss/suppression of cough reflex (anaesthesia, neuromuscular disorders)
Dysfunction of mucociliary apparatus — smoking, viral infections, hot/corrosive gases, genetic defects (immotile cilia syndrome)
Accumulation of secretions (CF, bronchial obstruction)
Impaired macrophage phagocytic/bactericidal activity (alcohol, tobacco smoke, anoxia, O₂ intoxication)
Pulmonary congestion and oedema

<b>B) Factors lowering host resistance:</b> chronic diseases, immunologic deficiency, immunosuppressive agents, leukopenia.

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 704–705; Arif pp. 110

## 14 · Lung Abscess
<b>🔴 Short note: Lung abscess — causes, organisms, morphology, clinical features, complications.</b>

<b>Lung abscess:</b> A localised area of suppuration within the lung parenchyma that leads to parenchymal destruction, manifested radiologically as a <b>cavity with air-fluid level</b>. A local suppurative process that produces necrosis of lung tissue.

Causes:

Aspiration of infective material (most frequent) — risk factors: suppressed cough reflexes (alcohol intoxication, opioid abuse, coma, anaesthesia, seizures), severe dysphagia, protracted vomiting, poor dental hygiene
Antecedent primary lung infection (postpneumonic — S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, pneumococcus)
Septic embolism (thrombophlebitis, right-sided endocarditis vegetations)
Bronchial obstruction (bronchogenic carcinoma, adenoma, foreign body)
Local spread (oesophagus, spine, pleural cavity, liver/subphrenic abscess)
Haematogenous spread (pelvic abscess, pyaemia, septicaemia)
Miscellaneous (traumatic penetrations)

Causative organisms:

Aerobic/anaerobic streptococci, S. aureus, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella, Proteus, oral anaerobes (Bacteroides, Fusobacterium, Peptococcus).

Morphology:

Abscesses: few mm to 5–6 cm cavities; single or multiple. Histology: suppurative destruction of lung parenchyma within the central area of cavitation. Continued infection → large, poorly demarcated, fetid, green-black, multilocular cavities = <b>gangrene of the lung</b>. Chronic cases: fibroblastic proliferation → fibrous wall.

Clinical features:

Cough with copious foul-smelling purulent or sanguineous sputum
Fever, chest pain, weight loss
Occasional haemoptysis
Clubbing of fingers and toes
Radiographic evidence of fluid-filled cavity

Complications:

Extension of infection into pleural cavity
Haemorrhage
Brain abscess or meningitis (from septic emboli)
Secondary amyloidosis (chronic cases)

Ref: Robbins 10e p.711; Harsh Mohan 7e p.457; Arif pp. 114–115

## 15 · Pulmonary Tuberculosis (TB)
<b>🔴 What are the types of pulmonary TB? Describe the pathogenesis of primary and secondary TB.</b>

🫁 Pulmonary TB is caused by <b>Mycobacterium tuberculosis</b>. It is a leading cause of infectious death worldwide.

Types:

Primary pulmonary TB — develops in a previously unexposed/unsensitised person
Secondary pulmonary TB — develops in a previously sensitised host (reactivation or reinfection)

Pathogenesis — depends on two immune responses: (1) acquired cellular immunity, (2) delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH).

<b>Primary infection:</b>

Inhalation of droplet nuclei containing tubercle bacilli → reach alveoli
Non-specifically taken up by alveolar macrophages — but inactivated macrophages cannot destroy organisms
Organisms multiply → macrophages burst → spread
More macrophages & monocytes extravasate (also inactivated)
Spread to regional (hilar) lymph nodes → lymphangitis → lymphadenitis
Primary (Ghon) complex forms
Haematogenous spread → miliary tuberculosis (affects various organs)

<b>Secondary infection (re-infection):</b>

Cell-mediated immunity → sensitised T-cells secrete lymphokines
Lymphokines activate macrophages → capable of killing and digesting bacilli
Accumulation of activated macrophages at the lesion
Macrophages transform into epithelioid cells
Aggregation of epithelioid cells → giant cells (Langhans-type) + fibrosis
Formation of tubercle → caseous necrosis → soft tubercle = hallmark of TB

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 368–370; Arif pp. 116–117

<b>🔴 What is primary tuberculosis? What is Ghon's focus and Ghon's complex? What are the fates of primary TB?</b>

<b>Primary tuberculosis:</b> The form of disease that develops in a <b>previously unexposed and therefore unsensitised person</b>. About 5% develop clinically significant disease; 90–95% remain asymptomatic. Source of organism is exogenous.

<b>Primary sites of TB:</b> (1) Lungs (mainly), (2) Intestine, (3) Skin, (4) Oropharyngeal, (5) Lymphoidal.

Ghon's complex

The lesion of primary TB, consisting of:

Ghon's focus: the primary site of infection in the lung (sub-pleural region — lower part of upper lobe or upper part of lower lobe)
Lymphangitis
Hilar lymphadenitis (inflammation of hilar lymph nodes)

<i>Yes, primary TB can occur more than once — in elderly and profoundly immunosuppressed (who lose their immunity; source is exogenous). Most people go on to have latent disease.</i>

Fates of primary pulmonary TB:

Complete resolution
Healing with fibrosis & calcification
Progressive pulmonary TB (or disseminated miliary TB)
Progresses to secondary TB (re-infection)

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 369–371; Arif pp. 117–118

<b>🔴 What is secondary TB? Mention the sites and differences between primary and secondary TB.</b>

<b>Secondary tuberculosis:</b> Develops in a <b>previously sensitised host</b>. May follow shortly after primary TB but more commonly arises from <b>reactivation of dormant primary lesion</b> months to years later (when host resistance is weakened). May also result from exogenous reinfection.

<b>Sites of primary TB:</b> sub-pleural region (lower part of upper lobe or upper part of lower lobe).

<b>Sites of secondary TB:</b> apex of the upper lobe of one or both lungs — high O₂ tension promotes bacterial growth.

<b>Define miliary tuberculosis. Describe the pathology/morphology of pulmonary TB.</b>

<b>Miliary TB:</b> Bacilli within macrophages spread to regional hilar lymph nodes → blood stream → distributes organisms to almost all parts of the body. Most prominent in liver, bone marrow, spleen, adrenals, meninges, kidneys, fallopian tubes & epididymis — but can involve any organ. Occurs when body immunity fails to control primary infection.

Pathology / morphology of pulmonary TB:

<b>Primary TB:</b>

Focal lesion in lung = Ghon's focus
Lymphangitis and hilar lymph node enlargement
Ghon's focus + nodal involvement = Ghon's complex
Gradually heals → calcified (Ghon's body)
If uncontained → bloodstream → miliary TB

<b>Secondary TB — Gross:</b>

Lesion at apex of one or both lungs
Starts as small focus of consolidation, usually <3 cm
Regional nodes develop similar tubercular foci

<b>Secondary TB — Microscopic:</b>

Accumulation of activated macrophages → tubercle formation
Organised aggregation of activated macrophages = epithelioid cells (pale, pink, granular cytoplasm)
Epithelioid cells fuse → giant cells (40–50 μm): Langhans-type (peripheral nuclei) or foreign-body type (haphazard nuclei)
Peripheral collar of fibroblasts, lymphocytes, macrophages surrounds the granuloma
Central caseous necrosis → soft tubercle = characteristic hallmark of TB

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 371, 373; Arif pp. 119–120

<b>Describe the formation of TB granuloma (soft tubercle) — immunopathogenesis.</b>

Formation of tubercle / immunopathogenesis of TB granuloma:

Causative agent (infectious or immunological) at the site
Polymorphonuclear cells (neutrophils) arrive first
After 24 hours, macrophages arrive
When the inciting agent is poorly degradable/particulate, macrophages engulf → process → present to T lymphocytes → T-cell activation
Responding T-cells produce IL-2 (activates other T-cells, perpetuating the response) and IFN-γ (activates macrophages → epithelioid cells & multinucleated giant cells)
Accumulation of lymphocytes, fibroblasts, plasma cells
Caseation necrosis occurs
→ Caseating granuloma / soft tubercle

<b>Morphology of tubercle (microscopic):</b> Organised aggregation of activated macrophages = epithelioid cells (pale, pink, granular cytoplasm) → fuse to form giant cells (40–50 μm; Langhans-type or foreign-body type) → peripheral collar of fibroblasts, lymphocytes, macrophages → central caseous necrosis = soft tubercle.

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 100, 371; Arif pp. 120–121

<b>Mention the clinical features of pulmonary TB and how to diagnose it.</b>

Clinical features of pulmonary TB:

Systemic: malaise, anorexia, weight loss, fever
Fever: low grade and remittent (appears late afternoon, subsides)
Night sweats
Persistent cough for ≥3 weeks with sputum (initially mucoid, later purulent)
Haemoptysis in about 50% of cases
Pleuritic chest pain
Extrapulmonary manifestations depend on organ involved

Diagnosis of pulmonary TB:

History and physical examination
Radiologic findings: consolidation/cavitation in lung apices
Sputum for AFB (acid-fast bacilli) staining and culture — gold standard (culture on solid agar: 3–6 weeks; liquid media: ~2 weeks)
ESR — high
PCR / GeneXpert — can detect as few as 10 organisms (vs >10,000 for AFB smear) — confirmatory test
FNAC and biopsy of lymph node (if present)
Antibiotic susceptibility test (C/S)
MT test / tuberculin skin test (not diagnostic by itself)

<b>Staining:</b> Ziehl-Neelsen (Z-N) stain. <b>Culture:</b> Lowenstein-Jensen (LJ) medium. <b>Why early morning sample:</b> secretions build up overnight → more bacilli.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.371; Arif pp. 121–122

<b>Short note: Mantoux / tuberculin test.</b>

<b>Mantoux (tuberculin) test:</b> Detects <b>delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH)</b> to M. tuberculosis antigens. No diagnostic value alone — indicates past or recent infection when positive. Becomes positive 2–4 weeks after infection.

Reagent: purified protein derivative (PPD). WHO recommended: PPD-RT-23 with Tween 80
Dose: 5 tuberculin units (TU) — 0.1 ml
Technique: intradermal injection on flexor aspect of forearm
Reading: after 72 hours (3rd day); induration measured in mm

Interpretation:

Positive: induration >10 mm (person infected but doesn't differentiate infection vs active disease)
Negative: <6 mm
Doubtful: 6–9 mm (may be M. tuberculosis or atypical mycobacteria)

False positive:

Prior BCG (Bacillus Calmette-Guerin) vaccination
Atypical mycobacterium infection

False negative:

Viral infections (HIV, measles, chickenpox)
Sarcoidosis, malnutrition, malignancy, Hodgkin lymphoma
Immunosuppression, overwhelming active TB, incorrect injection

Ref: Robbins 10e p.371; Park 25e p.197; Arif pp. 122

<b>Mention the chest X-ray findings of pulmonary TB.</b>

Chest X-ray findings of pulmonary TB:

Patchy opacity

Cavitation

<b>🔴 What are the risk factors / aetiopathogenesis of bronchogenic carcinoma?</b>

Tobacco smoking (most important): 85–90% of lung cancers arise in smokers; risk proportional to duration and amount (pack years). Small cell and squamous cell are most common in smokers. Heavy smokers (2 packs/day × 20 years) = 60-fold greater risk. Cessation for 10 years reduces risk but never returns to non-smoker level.
Air pollution: Indoor air pollution, especially radon (2nd most common cause)
Radiation exposure: high-dose ionising radiation (radium, uranium workers)
Asbestos: increased lung cancer risk; synergistic with smoking
Industrial hazards: nickel, chromium, beryllium, arsenic, vinyl chloride, mustard gas
Molecular genetics: oncogenes — c-MYC, K-RAS, EGFR, HER-2/neu
Precursor (pre-invasive) lesions: (a) squamous dysplasia/CIS, (b) atypical adenomatous hyperplasia, (c) adenocarcinoma in situ, (d) diffuse idiopathic pulmonary neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 716; BRS Pathology 5e p.216; Arif pp. 126–127

<b>🔴 Write down the morphology of bronchogenic squamous cell carcinoma. Describe its pathogenesis.</b>

<b>Pathogenesis:</b> Chronic smoking → chronic irritation of columnar ciliated epithelium → <b>squamous metaplasia</b> (columnar cells replaced by stratified squamous epithelium) → dysplasia → carcinoma in situ → invasive squamous cell carcinoma.

Morphology — Macroscopic:

Location: central (most often around the hilum)
Types of growth: fungating, infiltrative, cauliflower-like
Colour: grey-white
Consistency: firm to hard
Large lesions may undergo central necrosis → cavitation

Morphology — Microscopic:

Keratinisation (keratin pearls) and/or intercellular bridges
Varying grades: well-differentiated, moderately-differentiated, poorly-differentiated
Squamous metaplasia, dysplasia, and foci of CIS may be seen in adjacent bronchial epithelium

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 539, 718; Arif pp. 127–128

<b>🔴 Write down the morphology of small cell carcinoma of lung. Why is it most dangerous?</b>

<b>Small cell carcinoma</b> (formerly "oat cell" carcinoma): ~15% of all bronchogenic carcinoma.

Characteristics:

99% smokers — strongest smoking association
Arise from neuroendocrine cells (Kulchitsky cells)
Highly malignant, rapidly growing (no known pre-invasive phase)
Early metastasis; inoperable at presentation
Usually responds to chemo/radiotherapy but rarely cured by surgery
Poor prognosis: survival 3 months to 1 year

Morphology — Macroscopic:

Central/hilar (most will have metastasised to hilar/mediastinal lymph nodes by diagnosis)
Colour: pale-grey

Morphology — Microscopic:

Relatively small (oat-like) tumour cells — round, oval, or spindle-shaped with:
Scant cytoplasm
Ill-defined borders
Finely granular nuclear chromatin ("salt and pepper" appearance)
Absent or inconspicuous nucleoli
Frequent nuclear moulding
High mitotic count
Cells organised into cords, sheets, aggregates, ribbons, or around blood vessels → pseudorosettes

<b>Why most dangerous:</b> (1) Most aggressive; (2) early wide-spread metastasis (mostly at diagnosis); (3) poor prognosis; (4) virtually incurable by surgery; (5) almost invariably fatal.

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 539–540, 718; Arif pp. 128–129

<b>🔴 Name 5 paraneoplastic syndromes related to bronchogenic carcinoma.</b>

Non-metastatic extrapulmonary manifestations (paraneoplastic syndromes):

<b>1. Endocrine:</b>

SIADH → hyponatraemia (inappropriate ADH secretion)
Cushing syndrome (ectopic ACTH secretion)
Hypercalcaemia (PTH, PTHrP, prostaglandin E, cytokines)
Hypocalcaemia (calcitonin)
Gynaecomastia (gonadotropins)
Carcinoid syndrome (serotonin, bradykinin)

<b>2. Neurological:</b>

Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome
Peripheral neuropathy
Myelopathy
Cerebellar degeneration

<b>3. Others:</b>

Digital clubbing
Hypertrophic pulmonary osteoarthropathy (HPOA)
Polymyositis & dermatomyositis
Acanthosis nigricans
Leukemoid reactions
Trousseau syndrome (DVT, thromboembolism)
Nephrotic syndrome

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 543, 722; Davidson's 23e p.601; Arif pp. 129

<b>🔴 What are the clinical features and investigations for diagnosis of bronchogenic carcinoma?</b>

Clinical features:

<b>Local symptoms:</b> cough (most common early symptom), chest pain, dyspnoea, haemoptysis.

<b>Bronchial obstruction →</b> bronchopneumonia, lung abscess, bronchiectasis, pleural effusion.

<b>Local spread →</b> hoarseness, dysphagia, superior vena cava obstruction (SVCO) syndrome, Horner syndrome.

<b>Metastasis (liver, brain, bone, adrenal, skin, contralateral lung, lymph nodes) →</b> back pain (bone), headache/hemiparesis/seizures (brain).

<b>Paraneoplastic syndromes.</b>

<b>General features:</b> anorexia, weight loss, malaise, fatigue.

Laboratory investigations:

<b>To confirm diagnosis:</b>

Chest X-ray P/A view (lobar collapse, mass, effusion, bony destruction)
CT scan of chest
Bronchoscopy + biopsy (central lesions)
CT/USG-guided percutaneous needle biopsy (peripheral lesions)
Sputum cytology for malignant cells (for unfit patients)
FNAC/biopsy of affected lymph nodes
Pleural aspiration + biopsy (if effusion present)

<b>For staging (metastasis):</b> USG abdomen, CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, whole-body bone scan, MRI brain, PET-CT scan.

<b>Baseline:</b> CBC, LFT, RFT, PFT (especially FEV₁).

<b>Complication of percutaneous needle biopsy:</b> iatrogenic pneumothorax.

<b>Prognosis:</b> poor overall; 5-year survival 15%. Small cell = worst prognosis (surgical treatment ineffective though sensitive to chemo/radiotherapy).

Ref: Davidson's 23e p.601; Robbins 10e pp. 721–722; Arif pp. 129–132

<b>Short note: Pancoast tumour.</b>

<b>Pancoast tumour</b> (pulmonary sulcus tumour / superior sulcus tumour): A tumour of the <b>pulmonary apex</b>. Most are non-small cell cancers. Typically spreads to nearby ribs and vertebrae.

Symptoms:

Horner syndrome — ipsilateral partial ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis, enophthalmos (sympathetic ganglion compression)
Thoracic outlet syndrome — pain/weakness in arm and hand (brachial plexus compression)
Hoarseness and bovine cough (recurrent laryngeal nerve compression)

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 540, 721; Arif p. 133

## 17 · Pleural Diseases
<b>Define pleural effusion. Mention its types and causes. What is the most common cause?</b>

<b>Pleural effusion:</b> Abnormal accumulation of fluid within the pleural space. Normal pleural fluid: 5–15 mL (serous, relatively acellular, clear fluid). Common manifestation of both primary and secondary pleural diseases — inflammatory or non-inflammatory.

Types and causes:

<table>
<tr><th>Type</th><th>Fluid nature</th><th>Cause / Example</th></tr>
<tr><td rowspan="2"><strong>Inflammatory</strong></td><td>Serofibrinous (exudate)</td><td>Pulmonary TB, pneumonia, bronchial carcinoma, pulmonary infarction, lymphoma, collagen disease (SLE, rheumatoid arthritis)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Suppurative (pus)</td><td>Lung abscess, bronchiectasis</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Malignant</strong></td><td>—</td><td>Pleural mesothelioma, metastatic involvement</td></tr>
<tr><td rowspan="2"><strong>Non-inflammatory</strong></td><td>Hydrothorax (transudate)</td><td>Congestive cardiac failure (CCF), nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, malnutrition, hypothyroidism</td></tr>
<tr><td>Haemothorax (blood) / Chylothorax (chyle)</td><td>Filariasis, malignancies obstructing major lymphatic ducts</td></tr>
</table>

Most common 4 causes of pleural effusion:

Pulmonary TB
Pneumonia
Bronchial carcinoma
Pulmonary infarction

<b>Hydrothorax:</b> non-inflammatory serous fluid collection. <b>Haemothorax:</b> blood in pleural cavity. <b>Chylothorax:</b> milky lymphatic fluid in pleural cavity. <b>Empyema:</b> pus in pleural space.

Ref: Robbins 10e p.725; Arif pp. 133–135

<b>Short note: Pneumothorax. What are pleural tumours?</b>

Pneumothorax

Pneumothorax = air or gas in the pleural cavities. May be spontaneous, traumatic, or therapeutic.

<b>Types:</b>

Spontaneous: any pulmonary disease causing alveolar rupture
Open pneumothorax
Closed pneumothorax
Tension pneumothorax — defect acts as a flap valve: air enters during inspiration but cannot escape during expiration → progressively increasing pressures → can compress vital mediastinal structures and contralateral lung
Traumatic: penetrating chest injury, thoracic surgery
Therapeutic / Iatrogenic: during pleural fluid aspiration
Idiopathic in many cases

<b>Most common causes of pneumothorax:</b> (1) Emphysema, (2) Asthma, (3) Tuberculosis.

Pleural tumours:

<b>(a) Primary (arising from pleura):</b> benign mesothelioma (fibroma), <b>malignant mesothelioma</b> (with heavy asbestos exposure).

<b>(b) Secondary / metastatic:</b> most frequently from lung and breast.

Ref: Robbins 10e pp. 725–726; Arif pp. 135
