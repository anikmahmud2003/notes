# Ch 02 — The Heart     <i>Full-detail flashcards (Q→A) · All abbreviations expanded · Robbins 4-pillar answers</i>
### 🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of the Heart (boss-level overview)
The heart can fail by <b>six principal mechanisms</b>: <b>(1)</b> Pump failure (systolic/diastolic dysfunction → heart failure), <b>(2)</b> Obstruction to flow (e.g., aortic stenosis, coarctation), <b>(3)</b> Regurgitant flow (valvular insufficiency), <b>(4)</b> Shunted flow (congenital septal defects), <b>(5)</b> Conduction disorders (arrhythmias → sudden cardiac death), <b>(6)</b> Rupture (cardiac free wall, septum, papillary muscle).
The full classification (VIVA favourite): <b>1.</b> Heart failure (left-sided / right-sided) · <b>2.</b> Congenital heart disease (CHD) — acyanotic (ASD, VSD, PDA), cyanotic (TOF, TGA, tricuspid atresia), obstructive (coarctation, stenosis) · <b>3.</b> Ischaemic heart disease (IHD) — angina, MI, chronic IHD, sudden cardiac death · <b>4.</b> Arrhythmias & sudden cardiac death (SCD) · <b>5.</b> Hypertensive heart disease · <b>6.</b> Valvular heart disease — rheumatic fever & RHD, infective endocarditis (IE), non-infected vegetations (NBTE, Libman-Sacks) · <b>7.</b> Cardiomyopathies — dilated (DCM), hypertrophic, restrictive, arrhythmogenic · <b>8.</b> Myocarditis · <b>9.</b> Pericardial disease — effusion, acute pericarditis, constrictive pericarditis · <b>10.</b> Cardiac tumours — myxoma (most common benign adult), sarcoma (malignant), metastases.
Robbins 10e, Ch 12 (The Heart), pp. 527–580 · Arif 15e (2025) Vol-2, pp.45–75

## 2 · Congenital Heart Disease (CHD)
<b>🔴 Short note: Congenital heart disease. What are the three major categories?</b>

<b>Congenital heart disease (CHD)</b> = abnormalities of the heart or great vessels present at birth. Most arise from <b>faulty embryogenesis during gestational weeks 3–8</b>, when major cardiovascular structures form. <b>Incidence: ~5% of live births.</b>

A <b>shunt</b> is an abnormal communication between chambers or vessels. Blood flows down pressure gradients from left (systemic) to right (pulmonary) side or vice versa.

Three major categories

<table>
<tr><th>Category</th><th>Features</th><th>Examples</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>1. Right-to-left shunt (cyanotic CHD)</strong></td><td>Hypoxaemia, cyanosis (dusky blue skin/mucous membranes), paradoxical embolism, clubbing, polycythaemia</td><td>Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF), transposition of the great arteries (TGA), persistent truncus arteriosus, tricuspid atresia, total anomalous pulmonary venous connection</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>2. Left-to-right shunt (acyanotic CHD)</strong></td><td>Not initially cyanotic; may become cyanotic late (Eisenmenger syndrome); right ventricular hypertrophy (RVH)</td><td>Atrial septal defect (ASD), ventricular septal defect (VSD), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>3. Obstructive CHD</strong></td><td>Abnormal narrowing of chambers, valves, or vessels (complete obstruction = atresia)</td><td>Coarctation of the aorta, pulmonary stenosis &amp; atresia, aortic stenosis &amp; atresia</td></tr>
</table>

Robbins 10e pp.534–536; Arif pp.48–49

## 3 · Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)
<b>🔴 Short note: Tetralogy of Fallot. Give the four cardinal features, clinical features, and morphology.</b>

<b>Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)</b> — the four cardinal features (mnemonic: <b>V.V.O.V</b>):

Ventricular septal defect (VSD)
Obstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract (subpulmonary stenosis)
An aOrta that overrides the VSD
Ventricular (right) hypertrophy

<b>Embryology:</b> the first three features result from anterosuperior displacement of the infundibular septum; RVH is a secondary consequence of pressure overload.

Clinical features

Even untreated, TOF can be tolerated into adulthood
Most infants are cyanotic from birth or soon thereafter (classic TOF); some may not show cyanosis ("pink tetralogy")
As the child grows, the heart increases in size

Morphology

Heart is typically enlarged and classically <b>"boot-shaped"</b> due to marked right ventricular hypertrophy.

<b>Treatment:</b> complete surgical repair is possible for classic TOF but more complicated for persons with pulmonary atresia and dilated bronchial arteries.

Robbins 10e p.538; Arif p.49

<b>🎯 True/False practice — Tetralogy of Fallot</b>
<b>Components of tetralogy of Fallot: Left ventricular hypertrophy · Right ventricular hypertrophy · Ventricular septal defect · Atrial septal defect · Aortic stenosis</b>
Left ventricular hypertrophy <b>F</b> · Right ventricular hypertrophy <b>T</b> · Ventricular septal defect <b>T</b> · Atrial septal defect <b>F</b> · Aortic stenosis <b>F</b> (the obstruction is subpulmonary, not aortic) <i>(RMU-22M)</i>
## 4 · Heart Failure
<b>🔴 Define heart failure. What is pressure-overload vs volume-overload hypertrophy?</b>

<b>Heart failure</b> = the condition in which the heart cannot pump blood to adequately meet the metabolic demands of peripheral tissues, or can do so only at elevated filling pressures.

Patterns of hypertrophy (Robbins)

<table>
<tr><th>Type</th><th>Mechanism</th><th>Sarcomere pattern</th><th>Result</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Pressure-overload hypertrophy</strong></td><td>Hypertension, aortic stenosis</td><td>New sarcomeres assembled <strong>in parallel</strong></td><td>Concentric increase in wall thickness; ↑ cross-sectional area of myocytes</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Volume-overload hypertrophy</strong></td><td>Valvular regurgitation</td><td>Sarcomeres assembled <strong>in series</strong></td><td>Primary ventricular dilation; wall thickness may be normal or increased</td></tr>
</table>

In dilated hearts, <b>heart weight</b> (not wall thickness) is the best measure of hypertrophy.

Robbins 10e pp.530–532

<b>🔴 What are the causes and morphologic changes in left-sided heart failure?</b>

<b>Causes of left-sided heart failure:</b>

Ischaemic heart disease (IHD)
Hypertension
Aortic and mitral valvular disease
Primary myocardial diseases

<b>Morphologic changes (organ-by-organ):</b>

Heart: hypertrophied & dilated left ventricle, secondary left atrial enlargement, thrombus formation, MI & valvular deformity
Lung: pulmonary congestion & oedema (heavy, wet lungs), hemosiderin-laden macrophages in alveoli (siderophages / "heart failure cells"), pleural effusion (serous)
Kidney: reduction in renal perfusion, prerenal azotaemia
Brain: hypoxic encephalopathy

Cardinal features of left ventricular failure

Pulsus alternans
Gallop rhythm
Bilateral basal crepitations

Robbins 10e p.532; Arif pp.50–52

<b>🔴 What are the causes and morphologic changes in right-sided heart failure?</b>

<b>Causes:</b>

Secondary to left-sided heart failure
Isolated right-sided heart failure (often referred to as cor pulmonale)

<b>Morphologic changes (organ-by-organ):</b>

Heart: hypertrophy & dilation of right atrium and ventricle; rarely structural defects such as valvular abnormalities or endocardial fibrosis
Liver & portal system:
Congestive hepatomegaly — prominent passive congestion, most prominent around central veins → "nutmeg liver" appearance (congested red-brown pericentral zones with tan periportal regions)
Long-standing → central fibrosis → cardiac cirrhosis
Portal venous HTN → congestive splenomegaly (platelet sequestration), chronic congestion & oedema of bowel wall
Pleural, pericardial, peritoneal spaces: effusions (peritoneal effusion = ascites)
Subcutaneous tissues: peripheral/dependent oedema — foot/ankle (pedal) and pretibial oedema; in bedridden patients, presacral oedema; generalized massive oedema (anasarca)

Cardinal features of right ventricular failure

Raised JVP (jugular venous pressure)
Tender hepatomegaly
Dependent oedema

<b>Cardinal features of congestive cardiac failure (bilateral):</b> bilateral basal crepitations + raised JVP + tender hepatomegaly + dependent oedema.

Robbins 10e p.533; Arif pp.51–52

## 5 · Ischaemic Heart Disease (IHD)
<b>🔴 Define and classify ischaemic heart disease (IHD). Why is it called coronary artery disease?</b>

<b>Ischaemic heart disease (IHD)</b> = a group of related entities resulting from <b>myocardial ischaemia</b> — an imbalance between myocardial supply (perfusion) and cardiac demand for oxygenated blood.

In >90% of cases, myocardial ischaemia results from reduced blood flow due to obstructive <b>atherosclerotic lesions in the epicardial coronary arteries</b> → hence IHD is frequently called <b>coronary artery disease (CAD)</b>.

<b>NB:</b> IHD is often the late manifestation of coronary atherosclerosis that began during childhood or adolescence — a long period (up to decades) of silent, slow progression before sudden onset of symptoms.

Types / syndromes of IHD

Angina pectoris (chest pain — ischaemia insufficient to cause necrosis)
Stable (typical) angina
Unstable or crescendo angina
Prinzmetal angina (vasospastic)
Myocardial infarction (MI) — frank cardiac necrosis
Chronic IHD with heart failure
Sudden cardiac death (SCD)

<b>Acute coronary syndrome (ACS):</b> the triad of unstable angina, acute MI, and SCD.

🎯 Atherosclerotic lesions in coronary arteries: highest incidence in <b>LAD</b> (left anterior descending) > RCA (right coronary) > CXA (circumflex). One-third single-vessel, one-third two-vessel, one-third three-vessel disease.

Robbins 10e pp.542–543; BRS Pathology 5e p.137; Harsh Mohan 7e p.407

<b>🔴 Discuss the pathogenesis of IHD. What are the non-atherosclerotic causes?</b>

The dominant cause of IHD = partial or complete interruption of arterial blood flow to the myocardium → insufficient coronary perfusion relative to myocardial demand.

Three broad headings of etiology

Coronary atherosclerosis: chronic, progressive atherosclerotic narrowing of epicardial coronary arteries
Acute plaque change: rupture, superficial erosion, ulceration, fissuring, or deep haemorrhage
Non-atherosclerotic causes:
Vasospasm
Stenosis of coronary ostia
Thrombotic diseases (e.g., polycythaemia vera, sickle cell anaemia)
Embolism
Trauma to coronary arteries
Aneurysms
Compression of a coronary from outside

<b>Consequences of myocardial ischaemia</b> (depending on suddenness, duration, degree, location, extent): stable angina, unstable angina, MI, SCD.

Robbins 10e p.542; BRS Pathology 5e p.137; Harsh Mohan 7e p.408

## 6 · Angina Pectoris
<b>🔴 Define angina pectoris. Describe the three types.</b>

<b>Angina pectoris</b> (literally "chest pain") = paroxysmal, usually recurrent attacks of substernal or precordial chest discomfort caused by <b>transient (15 seconds to 15 minutes) myocardial ischaemia</b> insufficient to induce myocyte necrosis.

Three types

<table>
<tr><th>Type</th><th>Description</th><th>Mechanism</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>1. Stable (typical) angina</strong></td><td>Most common. Crushing/squeezing substernal sensation radiating down left arm or to jaw. Precipitated by exertion (physical activity, emotional excitement, stress). Relieved by rest or vasodilators (nitroglycerin, calcium channel blockers).</td><td>Imbalance in coronary perfusion (chronic stenosing coronary atherosclerosis) relative to myocardial demand</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>2. Unstable / crescendo angina</strong></td><td>Increasingly frequent, prolonged (&gt;20 min), or severe angina at progressively lower levels of activity or even at rest. "Pre-infarction angina" / "acute coronary insufficiency" — warning for MI.</td><td>Disruption of atherosclerotic plaque with superimposed thrombosis; distal embolization; vasospasm</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>3. Prinzmetal variant angina</strong></td><td>Uncommon; episodic myocardial ischaemia at rest.</td><td>Coronary artery spasm</td></tr>
</table>

🎯 In angina, <b>no elevation of enzymes</b> in blood — there is no irreversible myocardial injury.

Robbins 10e p.543; BRS Pathology 5e p.137; Harsh Mohan 7e p.409

## 7 · Myocardial Infarction (MI)
<b>🔴 Define myocardial infarction (MI).</b>

<b>Myocardial infarction (MI)</b> ("heart attack") = death of cardiac muscle due to <b>prolonged ischaemia</b>. The most important and feared consequence of coronary artery disease.

Robbins 10e pp.543, 546–547

<b>What are the two patterns of MI?</b>

<table>
<tr><th>Pattern</th><th>Description</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Transmural (full-thickness) infarction</strong></td><td>Ischaemic necrosis involves full or nearly full thickness of ventricular wall (endocardium → epicardium) in the distribution of the affected coronary artery</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Subendocardial (non-transmural) infarction</strong></td><td>Ischaemic necrosis involves inner third of ventricular wall; occurs when plaque disruption → coronary thrombus → lysed before necrosis extends full thickness</td></tr>
</table>

<b>Multifocal microinfarction:</b> pathology involving only smaller intramural vessels.

Robbins 10e pp.546–547

<b>What type of necrosis occurs in MI?</b>

<b>Coagulative necrosis</b>.

Robbins 10e p.543; BRS Pathology 5e p.137; Harsh Mohan 7e p.410

<b>🔴 Discuss the pathogenesis of MI. What is the "area at risk"?</b>

A. Coronary arterial occlusion

<b>a) Thrombotic occlusion (most common):</b>

Atheromatous plaque eroded or disrupted (endothelial injury, intraplaque haemorrhage, mechanical forces) → exposes subendothelial collagen & necrotic plaque contents
Platelets adhere, aggregate, activate → release thromboxane A₂, ADP, serotonin → further aggregation & vasospasm
Coagulation activation by tissue factor → growing thrombus
Within minutes → complete coronary artery lumen occlusion

<b>b) Non-thrombotic occlusion:</b>

Vasospasm (with or without atherosclerosis; cocaine, ephedrine)
Embolism (left atrial fibrillation, mural thrombus, IE vegetations, prosthetic material, paradoxical emboli via patent foramen ovale)
Uncommon: small vessel disease (vasculitis), haematologic (sickle cell), amyloid, dissection, marked hypertrophy (aortic stenosis), hypotension (shock), inadequate protection during cardiac surgery

B. Myocardial response

Obstruction → diminished blood flow → ischaemia → rapid dysfunction → with prolonged compromise → myocyte death. The anatomic region supplied by that artery = <b>"area at risk"</b>. Outcome depends on severity and duration of flow deprivation.

🎯 Myocardial ischaemia leads to loss of function within <b>1–2 minutes</b> but causes necrosis only after <b>20–30 minutes</b>.

Robbins 10e pp.544, 553; Arif pp.56–57

<b>🔴 Mention the clinical features of MI.</b>

Prolonged (>30 minutes) central chest pain — crushing, stabbing, or squeezing
Anxiety and fear of impending death
Nausea and vomiting
Hypotension
Rapid, weak pulse
Profuse sweating (diaphoresis)
Breathlessness
Collapse / syncope

🎯 <b>Painless / "silent" MI:</b> in as many as <b>25%</b> of patients MI is entirely asymptomatic (e.g., diabetic neuropathy) — discovered only by ECG or lab tests.

Robbins 10e p.550; Davidson's 23e p.495; Arif p.58

<b>🔴 Describe the biochemical changes and cardiac markers in MI with their timing.</b>

Biochemical changes

Cessation of aerobic glycolysis within seconds (ischaemia)
Inadequate high-energy phosphate production (creatinine phosphate, ATP) → anaerobic glycolysis
Accumulation of noxious metabolites (lactic acid)
Enzyme release from injured cardiac myocytes (membrane leak = hallmark of irreversible cell injury)
Release of myoglobin, troponin-I, and troponin-T

Cardiac biomarkers — timing table

<tr><th>Marker</th><th>Rises</th><th>Peaks</th><th>Returns to normal</th></tr>
<tr><td><strong>CK-MB</strong> (creatine kinase-MB)</td><td>4–6 hours</td><td>12–24 hours</td><td>48–72 hours</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Troponin-I &amp; Troponin-T</strong></td><td>4–6 hours</td><td>24–48 hours</td><td>5–14 days</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>AST</strong> (aspartate aminotransferase)</td><td>12 hours</td><td>48 hours</td><td>3–4 days</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>LDH</strong> (lactate dehydrogenase)</td><td>12 hours</td><td>2–3 days</td><td>1–2 weeks</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Myoglobin</strong></td><td>6 hours</td><td>48 hours</td><td>—</td></tr>
</table>

Key facts

Troponin-I & CK-MB elevations are highly characteristic of MI
Troponin-I = most sensitive & specific cardiac marker (nearly complete tissue specificity)
Myoglobin = earliest to appear but NOT cardiospecific
CK-MB = useful for detecting reinfarction (returns to normal by 72 hours)
Total LDH & total CK = nonspecific, passing into disuse

Other investigations

ECG: ST segment elevation (or new bundle branch block), pathological Q-wave formation, T-wave inversion
Blood: neutrophilic leukocytosis, raised ESR & CRP
Chest X-ray: demonstrate pulmonary oedema
Echocardiography: assess ventricular function, detect complications
Coronary angiography: for moderate-to-high risk patients (PCI or urgent CABG)

🎯 <b>CRP</b> (C-reactive protein): normal <5 mg/L; >3 mg/L = highest cardiovascular risk; 1–3 mg/L = moderate risk. Strongly predicts MI, stroke, peripheral arterial disease, SCD.

Robbins 10e p.550; Davidson's 23e pp.497–498; BRS Pathology 5e p.138; Arif pp.59–60

<b>📌 Mention the treatment of MI (therapeutic interventions).</b>

Urgent hospitalization (CCU — coronary care unit)
Oxygen supplementation
Morphine (pain relief, improve dyspnoea)
Nitrates (vasodilation, reverse vasospasm)
Antiplatelet agents (aspirin, ADP receptor inhibitors, GPIIb/IIIa inhibitors)
Anticoagulant therapy (unfractionated heparin, LMWH, direct thrombin inhibitors, factor Xa inhibitors) — prevent thrombus propagation
Beta blockers (↓ myocardial O₂ demand, ↓ arrhythmia risk — unless contraindicated in HF)
Prompt reperfusion (fibrinolytics or transcatheter interventions — PCI)
Manage O₂ supply vs demand: anxiety, ischaemic pain, haemodynamics, anaemia, respiratory disorders
Early arrhythmia monitoring & management

Robbins 10e p.550; Davidson's 23e p.498

<b>🔴 What are the complications of acute MI? (enumerate all 10)</b>

Nearly three-fourths of patients experience one or more complications:

Contractile dysfunction: left ventricular failure with hypotension, pulmonary oedema, respiratory impairment, severe "pump failure" (cardiogenic shock)
Papillary muscle dysfunction → postinfarct mitral regurgitation
Right ventricular infarction → right-sided heart failure
Myocardial rupture (1–5% of MIs, frequently fatal; most common 3–7 days post-MI):
Left ventricular free wall rupture (most common) → fatal haemopericardium & cardiac tamponade
Ventricular septal rupture → VSD with left-to-right shunting
Papillary muscle rupture → severe mitral regurgitation
Arrhythmias — most common cause of death in the first hour: heart block, bradycardia, supraventricular tachyarrhythmias, ventricular premature contractions, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation
Pericarditis — typically 2–3 days post-MI; rarely intense pericarditis weeks later (Dressler syndrome)
Chamber dilation — disproportionate stretching, thinning, dilation of infarcted region (especially anteroseptal infarcts)
Mural thrombosis → left-sided thromboembolism
Ventricular aneurysm
Progressive heart failure (chronic IHD)

🎯 One-third of patients with STEMI (ST-elevation MI) at home will die — usually of arrhythmia within 1 hour of symptom onset, before medical attention.

Robbins 10e p.551; BRS Pathology 5e pp.138–139; Arif p.61

<b>Probable diagnosis:</b> acute myocardial infarction (MI).

<b>Expected biochemical findings:</b>

CK-MB — rises within 4–6 hours
Troponin-I & Troponin-T — rise within 4–6 hours
AST — rises within 12 hours
LDH — rises within 12 hours

## 8 · Sudden Cardiac Death (SCD)
<b>🔴 Short note: Sudden cardiac death (SCD). Give the causes.</b>

<b>Sudden cardiac death (SCD)</b> = unexpected death from cardiac causes, either without symptoms, or within <b>1–24 hours of symptom onset</b> (Pathoma: <1 hour).

<b>Mechanism:</b> most often a lethal arrhythmia (asystole or ventricular fibrillation).

Causes of SCD

Coronary artery disease / severe atherosclerosis (80–90% of cases)
Non-atherosclerotic causes (younger victims):
Cardiac conduction abnormalities (hereditary or acquired)
Dilated or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
Congenital coronary arterial abnormalities
Myocarditis
Mitral valve prolapse
Pulmonary hypertension
Miscellaneous: pericardial tamponade, pulmonary embolism, metabolic & haemodynamic alterations, catecholamines, drugs of abuse (cocaine, methamphetamine)

Robbins 10e pp.551, 555; Arif pp.62–63

## 9 · Vegetative Heart Disease

<b>Vegetation</b> = complex of thrombotic debris and organisms, often resulting from colonisation/invasion of heart valves or mural endocardium by a microbe, with destruction of underlying cardiac tissue. (Thrombi on heart valves = vegetations; may be infected or sterile.)

Three categories of vegetative heart disease

Rheumatic fever & rheumatic heart disease (RHD)

## 10 · Rheumatic Fever & Rheumatic Heart Disease
<b>🔴 Define rheumatic fever (RF) and rheumatic heart disease (RHD). Which valve is most commonly involved?</b>

<b>Rheumatic fever (RF)</b> = an acute, immunologically mediated, multisystem inflammatory disease occurring a few weeks after <b>group A streptococcal pharyngitis</b>; occasionally follows streptococcal skin infections. Affects children <b>5–15 years</b>, 1–4 weeks after pharyngitis.

<b>Rheumatic heart disease (RHD)</b> = the chronic stage of RF involving all layers of the heart (<b>pancarditis</b>), characterised principally by <b>deforming fibrotic valvular disease</b>, particularly the mitral valve.

Valves involved in RHD

Mitral valve: 65–70% of cases
Aortic valve: 25%

🎯 RHD is virtually the <b>only cause of mitral stenosis</b>.

Robbins 10e p.560; Harsh Mohan 7e p.418; BRS Pathology 5e p.139

<b>🔴 Enumerate the Jones criteria for diagnosis of rheumatic fever. (major + minor + supporting evidence)</b>

Diagnosis requires <b>2 major OR 1 major + 2 minor manifestations</b> PLUS evidence of preceding streptococcal infection.

Major manifestations

Migratory polyarthritis of large joints (most common major manifestation)
Pancarditis (myocarditis, pericarditis, or endocarditis)
Subcutaneous nodules (on extensor surfaces of extremities)
Erythema marginatum (irregular circinate skin rash)
Sydenham chorea (involuntary rapid movements, at least 3 months after acute RF)

Minor manifestations

Fever
Arthralgia (joint pain without clinical inflammation)
Raised ESR or CRP
Previous rheumatic fever
Leukocytosis
First-degree AV block (prolonged P-R interval on ECG)

Supporting evidence of preceding streptococcal infection

Recent scarlet fever
Raised anti-streptolysin O (ASO) or other streptococcal antibody titer
Positive throat culture

Robbins 10e p.562; Davidson's 23e p.515; BRS Pathology 5e p.140; Arif pp.65–66

<b>🔴 Write the pathogenesis of rheumatic fever.</b>

RF is a <b>post-infectious immunologic disease</b> — cross-reaction of antibodies (produced against streptococcal antigens) with host proteins. An autoimmune reaction incited by streptococcal infection.

Molecular mimicry — step by step

Pharyngitis / sore throat by group A β-haemolytic streptococcus (S. pyogenes)
Immune response & antibody formation against streptococcal M proteins (2–3 weeks later)
Cross-reaction of antibodies with host tissues: heart valve glycoprotein, myocardial sarcolemma, cardiac myosin, joints, other tissues
Antigen-antibody complex activates complement → Type II hypersensitivity reaction
Damage to myocardium, heart valves, and other connective tissues

<b>Autoimmunity:</b> in some cases, streptococcal infection can evoke an autoimmune response against cardiac self-antigens.

Robbins 10e p.561; Arif pp.66–67

<b>🔴 Write the morphology of acute and chronic rheumatic heart disease. What is an Aschoff body?</b>

A) Morphology of acute rheumatic heart disease

Focal inflammatory lesions in various tissues, most distinctive within the heart = <b>Aschoff nodules / Aschoff bodies</b>.

<b>Aschoff body consists of:</b>

Fragmented collagen & fibrinoid material
Foci of lymphocytes (primarily T-cells)
Occasional plasma cells
Plump activated macrophages = Anitschkow cells (pathognomonic for RF)
Occasional multinucleated giant cells = Aschoff cells / Aschoff giant cells

Diffuse inflammation & Aschoff bodies may be found in any of the three heart layers → <b>pancarditis</b> (pericarditis, myocarditis, endocarditis).

<b>Endocardial/valvular lesions:</b> fibrinoid necrosis within cusps or along tendinous cords, with overlying small (1–2 mm) sterile vegetations = <b>verrucae</b> along lines of closure.

B) Morphology of chronic rheumatic heart disease

<b>Gross:</b>

Leaflet thickening
Commissural fusion & shortening
Thickening & fusion of tendinous cords
Fibrous bridging across commissures + calcification → "fish mouth" or "buttonhole" stenosis
Dilatation of left atrium
Mural thrombus in appendage or along LA wall
Right ventricular hypertrophy (from long-standing lung changes)

<b>Microscopic:</b> diffuse fibrosis & neovascularisation in valvular leaflets; Aschoff bodies replaced by fibrous scar.

Aschoff body — detailed

The <b>classic lesion of rheumatic fever</b>. Focal inflammatory lesions found in various tissues but most distinctive in the heart.

<b>Anitschkow cells</b> = enlarged macrophages within Aschoff bodies with nuclei showing: longitudinal section → <b>caterpillar-like serrated nuclear chromatin</b>; cross-section → <b>owl-eye appearance</b> of central chromatin mass with perinuclear halo.

Robbins 10e p.561; BRS Pathology 5e p.139; Harsh Mohan 7e pp.420–422; Arif pp.67–68

<b>🔴 Write down the cardiac lesions of rheumatic fever (endocarditis, myocarditis, pericarditis).</b>

Cardiac lesions of RF = <b>pancarditis</b> — focal inflammatory involvement of interstitial tissue of all three heart layers.

1. Rheumatic endocarditis

Involves valvular & mural endocardium → rheumatic valvulitis (chiefly responsible for major cardiac manifestations in chronic RHD) & mural endocarditis.

2. Rheumatic myocarditis

Most characteristic feature = <b>Aschoff bodies</b> scattered throughout interstitial tissue, most frequent in the <b>interventricular septum, left ventricle, and left atrium</b>.

3. Rheumatic pericarditis

Inflammatory involvement of the pericardium → breathlessness or chest pain.

Harsh Mohan 7e pp.420–422; Davidson's 23e p.515; Arif p.69

<b>🔴 Enumerate the complications of rheumatic fever. What is the most common cause of death in acute RF?</b>

Complications

Mitral stenosis
Cardiac hypertrophy (usually right ventricle)
Cardiac dilation
Carditis
Heart failure
Arrhythmias (particularly atrial fibrillation)
Thromboembolic complications
Infective endocarditis

<b>Most common cause of death in acute rheumatic fever:</b> <b>acute rheumatic myocarditis</b>.

Robbins 10e p.562; Pathoma Lecture Notes; Arif pp.69–70

## 11 · Infective Endocarditis (IE)
<b>🔴 Classify endocarditis. Define infective endocarditis. Compare acute vs subacute IE.</b>

<b>Endocarditis</b> = inflammation of the inner layer (endocardium) of the heart, usually involving the heart valves, characterised by the prototypic lesion — the <b>vegetation</b>.

Classification by etiology

Infective endocarditis (IE): microorganisms are the source
Non-infective endocarditis:
Non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis (NBTE) — on previously damaged valves
Libman-Sacks endocarditis (LSE) — more often in SLE patients

<b>Infective endocarditis (IE)</b> = microbial infection of heart valves or mural endocardium leading to vegetations composed of thrombotic debris & organisms, often with destruction of underlying cardiac tissue.

Acute IE (ABE) vs Subacute IE (SABE)

<b>🔴 Discuss the etiopathogenesis of infective endocarditis.</b>

A. Etiology

<b>Causative agents:</b>

Streptococcus viridans (50–60%)
Staphylococcus aureus (20–30%)
HACEK group (Haemophilus, Actinobacillus, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella, Kingella)
Streptococcus bovis
Streptococcus pneumoniae
Staphylococcus epidermidis
Gram-negative enteric bacilli
Fungi

<b>Predisposing factors:</b>

Bacteraemia / septicaemia / pyaemia sources: dental procedures (tooth brushing, extraction), GI/GU tract infections/surgery, skin infections (boils, carbuncles), respiratory infections, intravenous drug abuse
Underlying heart disease: chronic rheumatic valvular disease (~50%), congenital heart disease (VSD, subaortic stenosis, pulmonary stenosis, bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation, PDA), syphilitic aortic disease, floppy mitral valve, prosthetic valves
Impaired host defences: neutropenia, immunodeficiency, malignancy, immunosuppression, diabetes mellitus, alcohol/IV drug abuse

B. Pathogenesis

Organisms entering bloodstream → implanted on cardiac valves or mural endocardium via <b>surface adhesion molecules</b> mediating adherence to injured endocardium.

Robbins 10e p.563; Harsh Mohan 7e p.425; Arif pp.71–72

<b>🔴 What are the complications of infective endocarditis? (cardiac + extracardiac)</b>

A. Cardiac complications

Valvular stenosis or insufficiency
Perforation, rupture, and aneurysm of valve leaflets
Abscesses in the valve ring (ring abscess)
Myocardial abscesses
Suppurative pericarditis
Cardiac failure

B. Extracardiac complications

Systemic emboli → infarcts & abscesses in brain, kidneys, spleen, lung
Petechiae (skin & conjunctiva)
Osler's nodes (SABE) — painful, tender nodules on finger tips of hands & feet
Janeway's spots (ABE) — painless, non-tender subcutaneous maculopapular lesions on pulp of fingers
Glomerulonephritis (antigen-antibody complex deposition in glomerulus)

🎯 Causes of death in IE: cardiac failure, persistent infection, embolism to vital organs, renal failure, rupture of mycotic aneurysm of cerebral arteries.

Robbins 10e p.564; Harsh Mohan 7e p.428; Arif pp.72–73

## 12 · Cardiomyopathies
<b>🔴 Define cardiomyopathy. Classify it (primary vs secondary; anatomical patterns).</b>

<b>Cardiomyopathy</b> (literally "heart muscle disease") = any cardiac dysfunction resulting from a myocardial abnormality.

By aetiology

Primary cardiomyopathies: involve predominantly the heart; may be genetic or acquired (viral myocarditis, anthracycline cardiotoxicity)
Secondary cardiomyopathies: myocardial involvement as component of systemic/multiorgan disorder (haemochromatosis, amyloidosis)

By anatomic abnormalities (three patterns)

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) (including arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy) — most common (90% of cases)
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
Restrictive cardiomyopathy

Robbins 10e p.567; Arif p.73

## 14 · Pericarditis
<b>🔴 Define and classify pericarditis. Enumerate its causes.</b>

<b>Pericarditis</b> = inflammation of the pericardium. May occur secondary to cardiac, thoracic, or systemic disorders, metastases, or cardiac surgery.

Classification

<b>🔴 Give an account of constrictive pericarditis.</b>

<b>Constrictive pericarditis:</b> the heart is encased in a dense, fibrous or fibrocalcific scar that limits diastolic expansion and seriously restricts cardiac output — resembling restrictive cardiomyopathy.

History of previous pericarditis may or may not be present
Pericardial space obliterated; heart surrounded by dense, adherent layer of scar ± calcification
Scar often 0.5–1.0 cm thick
Extreme cases: resembles a plaster mold → concretio cordis

LVH = <b>True</b> · RVH = <b>True</b> (late, secondary to pulmonary HTN) · Cor pulmonale = <b>False</b> (that is pulmonary HTN from lung disease, not systemic HTN) · Acute MI = <b>False</b> · VT = <b>True</b>.
