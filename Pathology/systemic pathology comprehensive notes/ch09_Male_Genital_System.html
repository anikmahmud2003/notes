🧠 Big Picture — Male Genital System (boss-level overview)

Three anatomical regions dominate this chapter: Penis (inflammations → balanoposthitis; tumours → squamous cell carcinoma), Testis (the king — germ cell tumours = 95% of all testicular tumours; seminoma is the most common single type at ~50%; non-seminomatous GCTs include embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac tumour, choriocarcinoma, teratoma; sex cord-stromal tumours include Leydig & Sertoli cell tumours), and Prostate (benign prostatic hyperplasia/BPH = most common benign disease >50 yr; adenocarcinoma = 2nd most common cancer death in men).

🔴 Must-know high-yield threads: (1) Cryptorchidism → impaired descent → infertility + ↑risk of testicular cancer · (2) Seminoma = bulky, homogeneous, grey-white mass; radio- & chemosensitive; best prognosis among GCTs · (3) Choriocarcinoma = syncytiotrophoblast + cytotrophoblast; ↑↑hCG; haematogenous spread early · (4) BPH = DHT-driven nodular hyperplasia of periurethral zone; NOT premalignant · (5) Prostatic adenocarcinoma = peripheral zone (70%); osteoblastic bony mets; ↑PSA; Gleason grading · (6) PSA = organ-specific but NOT cancer-specific; elevated in carcinoma, BPH, prostatitis, infarction, instrumentation, ejaculation.

Robbins 10e, Ch 21 (Male Genital System), pp. 963–983 · Arif 15e (2025) Vol-2, pp.250–263

1 · Overview — Classification of Diseases

🔴 Classify the diseases of the male genital tract. Enumerate diseases of each part.

1. Penis

  • Congenital anomalies: Hypospadias, epispadias, phimosis
  • Inflammations: Syphilis, gonorrhoea, chancroid, granuloma inguinale, lymphogranuloma venereum, genital herpes, balanoposthitis (infection of glans + prepuce caused by Candida albicans, anaerobic bacteria, Gardnerella, pyogenic bacteria)
  • Tumours:
    • Benign — Condyloma acuminatum
    • Malignant — Squamous carcinoma in situ / penile intraepithelial neoplasia (PIN); Invasive squamous cell carcinoma

2. Testis & Epididymis

  • Congenital: Cryptorchidism (undescended testis, most common), absence of one/both testes, synorchism (fusion of testes)
  • Regressive: Atrophy, decreased fertility
  • Inflammatory: Non-specific epididymitis/orchitis, granulomatous (autoimmune) orchitis, infections (gonorrhoea, mumps, TB, syphilis)
  • Vascular: Torsion
  • Spermatic cord & paratesticular tumours: benign (lipomas, adenomatoid tumour); malignant (rhabdomyosarcoma in children, liposarcoma in adults)
  • Testicular tumours: see detailed classification below

3. Prostate

  • Inflammations: Acute & chronic bacterial prostatitis, chronic abacterial prostatitis, granulomatous prostatitis, autoimmune prostatitis
  • Benign enlargement: Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) / nodular hyperplasia
  • Tumours: Primary — adenocarcinoma (most common), ductal adenocarcinoma, small cell (neuroendocrine) carcinoma; Secondary — urothelial cancer, testicular metastases

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.250–251; Robbins 10e pp.963–983

2 · Penis

Describe the important inflammations and tumours of the penis.

Infections of the penis (STIs)

  • Syphilis — Treponema pallidum; chancre (painless ulcer)
  • Gonorrhoea — Neisseria gonorrhoeae; urethral discharge
  • Chancroid — Haemophilus ducreyi; painful genital ulcer + inguinal lymphadenopathy
  • Granuloma inguinale — Klebsiella granulomatis; painless, progressive genital ulceration
  • Lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV) — Chlamydia trachomatis L1–L3 serovars; inguinal lymph node enlargement with suppuration
  • Genital herpes — Herpes simplex virus (HSV); vesicular ulcers, recurrent

Balanoposthitis

Infection of the glans penis and prepuce; causative agents: Candida albicans, anaerobic bacteria, Gardnerella vaginalis, pyogenic bacteria.

Tumours of the penis

  • Condyloma acuminatum — benign; caused by HPV (human papillomavirus) types 6, 11; wart-like growths
  • Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) — malignant; arises from squamous epithelium of the glans or prepuce; precursor: squamous carcinoma in situ / penile intraepithelial neoplasia (PIN)

Arif 15e Vol-2 p.250; Robbins 10e pp.963–966

4 · Cryptorchidism

🔴 What is cryptorchidism? Describe its morphology, complications, and treatment.

Definition: Complete or partial failure of the intra-abdominal testes to descend into the scrotal sac. Present in about 1% of 1-year-old male infants. Associated with testicular dysfunction and increased risk of testicular cancer.

Location of undescended testis

  • 70% — inguinal ring
  • 25% — abdomen
  • 5% — other sites along descent path

Morphology

Gross: Usually unilateral (bilateral in 25%). Cryptorchid testes are small and firm.

Microscopic: Changes begin as early as 2 years of age:

  • Thickening of the basement membrane of the seminiferous tubules
  • Progressive loss of spermatogonia → tubules contain only Sertoli cells
  • Increase in interstitial stroma
  • Leydig cells are relatively prominent
  • Progressive tubular atrophy

Complications

  1. Sterility (infertility)
  2. Inguinal hernia (in about 10–20% of cases)
  3. Development of testicular cancer (↑ risk 10–40×)

Treatment: Surgical correction (orchiopexy).

🎯 Testicular atrophy causes = "A-TIP": Atherosclerotic narrowing of blood supply (old age) · Inflammatory orchitis · Failure of genetic origin (Klinefelter syndrome) · Disuse atrophy (high FSH stimulation).

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.252–253; Robbins 10e pp.966–967

5 · Seminoma

🔴 Give the morphology (gross + microscopic) and prognosis of seminoma.

Definition: A malignant germ cell tumour of the testis — the most common testicular GCT (50%).

Characteristics

  • Analogous to ovarian dysgerminoma and CNS germinoma
  • Peak incidence: fourth decade; almost never in infants
  • Presentation: painless enlargement of the testis
  • Sometimes associated with ↑serum human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)
  • Very radiosensitive and chemosensitive — often curable

Subtypes of seminoma

  1. Classic (typical) seminoma — most common
  2. Spermatocytic seminoma
  3. Anaplastic seminoma

Morphology — Macroscopic

  • Bulky masses, sometimes 10× the size of the normal testis
  • Homogeneous, grey-white, lobulated cut surface
  • Usually devoid of haemorrhage or necrosis
  • Tunica albuginea usually not penetrated; occasional extension to epididymis, spermatic cord, or scrotal sac

Morphology — Microscopic

  • Sheets of uniform cells divided into poorly demarcated lobules by delicate fibrous septa containing lymphocytic infiltrate
  • Ill-defined granulomas may be present
  • Classic seminoma cell: round to polyhedral, distinct cell membrane, clear or watery-appearing cytoplasm, large central nucleus with one or two prominent nucleoli
  • Cytoplasm contains varying amounts of glycogen

Prognosis

Better than other germ cell tumours. Highly radiosensitive and chemosensitive. Most patients are cured by orchiectomy with or without chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

🎯 Seminoma cell features = "CLEAR-L": Clear cytoplasm (glycogen) · Lobulated growth · Equal/uniform nuclei · Avascular (avascular fibrous septa) · Raised hCG (sometimes) · Lymphocytic septa.

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.253–255; Robbins 10e pp.970, 973; BRS Pathology 5e p.289

6 · Choriocarcinoma

🔴 Short note: Choriocarcinoma of the testis.

Definition: A highly malignant type of germ cell tumour (GCT) that can occur as an element of other GCTs.

Characteristics

  • Analogous to choriocarcinoma of the ovary
  • Incidence peaks in the second to third decade
  • Causes ↑↑serum hCG (β-hCG)
  • Highly chemosensitive

Morphology — Macroscopic

  • Often no testicular enlargement; detected only as a small palpable nodule
  • Small — rarely larger than 5 cm in diameter
  • Haemorrhage and necrosis extremely common

Morphology — Microscopic

  • Two intimately juxtaposed cell types:
  • Syncytiotrophoblasts — large multinucleated cells, abundant eosinophilic vacuolated cytoplasm containing hCG
  • Cytotrophoblasts — more regular, polygonal, distinct borders, clear cytoplasm, single fairly uniform nucleus; grow in cords or sheets
🎯 Choriocarcinoma key points = "BLEED": Both cell types present · Large hCG elevation · Early haematogenous spread · Erodes blood vessels · Distant metastases (lung, brain).

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.254–255; Robbins 10e p.972; BRS Pathology 5e p.291

7 · Teratoma

🔴 Define teratoma. Give its classification, morphology, and malignant potential.

Definition: GCTs having various cellular or organoid components reminiscent of normal derivatives of more than one germ layer.

May occur at any age from infancy to adulthood. Most frequently malignant in the testis (unlike ovary where mature teratoma is almost always benign).

Classification (three subtypes)

  1. Mature teratoma: Almost always malignant in the testis (contrast: benign in ovary)
  2. Immature teratoma: Primitive neural elements present; behaviour is malignant
  3. Teratoma with malignant transformation: Contains malignant tissue such as squamous cell carcinoma, mucin-secreting adenocarcinoma, sarcoma

Morphology — Gross

  • Usually large (5–10 cm in diameter)
  • Heterogeneous appearance with solid (sometimes cartilaginous) and cystic areas
  • Haemorrhage and necrosis usually indicate admixture with embryonal carcinoma, choriocarcinoma, or both

Morphology — Microscopic

  • Collections of differentiated cells or organoid structures: neural tissue, muscle bundles, cartilage, squamous epithelium with/without skin adnexa, thyroid gland-like structures, bronchial epithelium, bits of intestinal wall or brain substance
  • Embedded in fibrous or myxoid stroma
  • Elements may be mature (adult-tissue appearance) or immature (fetal/embryonic-tissue features)
  • Somatic-type malignant transformation: focus of SCC, adenocarcinoma, sarcoma

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.255–256; Robbins 10e p.973; BRS Pathology 5e p.290

9 · Spread & Laboratory Diagnosis of Testicular Tumours

🔴 Mention the mode of spread of testicular tumours and the laboratory workup.

Mode of spread

Testicular tumours spread by both lymphatic and haematogenous routes:

  1. Lymphatic spread: Common to all forms. Retroperitoneal para-aortic nodes are the first to be involved. Subsequent spread to mediastinal and supraclavicular nodes.
  2. Haematogenous spread: Primarily to the lungs; liver, brain, and bones may also be involved.

Seminoma: metastases typically involve lymph nodes first; haematogenous spread occurs later.

NSGCT: metastasise earlier and more frequently via the haematogenous route.

Laboratory workup (for a testicular mass)

  • Provisional diagnosis: Testicular tumour (most probably GCT)
  • Confirmatory: Radical inguinal orchiectomy followed by histopathology
  • New techniques: Immunohistochemistry (IHC), molecular diagnosis, flow cytometry
  • Tumour markers:
    • α-Fetoprotein (AFP)
    • Human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG)
    • Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

Testicular tumour markers — importance

Widely used in: (1) Initial evaluation of testicular masses, (2) Staging of GCTs, (3) Assessing tumour burden, (4) Monitoring response to therapy (follow-up).

Nice to Know — Which tumours raise which markers?

MarkerTumours that raise it
β-hCG ↑Seminoma, embryonal carcinoma, choriocarcinoma
AFP ↑Yolk sac tumour, embryonal carcinoma
🎯 AFP = "Yolk Embryo": Yolk sac tumour + Embryonal carcinoma raise AFP. Choriocarcinoma raises hCG but NOT AFP.

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.257–258; Robbins 10e pp.973; Harsh Mohan 7e p.697

A 22-year-old male suddenly develops severe testicular pain. What is the probable diagnosis? A 25-year-old male has painless testicular swelling — name tumour markers.

Scenario 1 — Sudden severe pain

Probable diagnosis: Testicular torsion. Torsion of the spermatic cord → acute ischaemia → sudden, severe testicular pain. Requires emergency surgical detorsion.

Scenario 2 — Painless testicular swelling (25 yr)

Provisional diagnosis: Testicular tumour (most likely GCT).

Tumour markers to advise:

  • α-Fetoprotein (AFP)
  • Human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG)
  • Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)

Confirmatory: Radical inguinal orchiectomy → histopathology. Additional: IHC, molecular diagnosis, flow cytometry.

11 · Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

🔴 Give the pathology/morphology, pathogenesis, effects, and clinical features of benign prostatic hyperplasia.

Definition: BPH (also called nodular hyperplasia) is the most common benign prostatic disease in men older than age 50 years. Results from nodular hyperplasia of prostatic stromal and epithelial cells; often leads to urinary obstruction.

It is NOT a premalignant lesion.

Age of incidence: Over 50 years, most often between 60 & 70.

Pathogenesis

  • The main androgen in the prostate is dihydrotestosterone (DHT), the ultimate mediator of prostatic growth
  • DHT is synthesised from testosterone via type 2 5α-reductase
  • DHT binds to and activates androgen receptors (ARs) in both stromal and epithelial cells
  • ARs translocate from cytoplasm to nucleus → activate transcription of androgen-dependent genes → encode growth factors and their receptors
  • DHT-induced growth factors increase stromal cell proliferation and decrease epithelial cell death

Morphology — Gross

  • Prostate enlarged; weight becomes 3–5× normal (60–100 g)
  • Nodules in lateral lobe compress urethra to a slit-like orifice
  • Cross-section: hyperplastic nodules of various size, colour, consistency
  • Gland-rich nodules: yellow-pink, soft, exude milky-white prostatic fluid
  • Fibromuscular stroma-rich nodules: pale grey, firm
  • No true capsule

Morphology — Microscopic

  • Nodules contain small-to-large-to-cystically dilated glands separated by bland spindle-shaped stromal cells
  • Glands lined by two cell layers: inner columnar secretory cell + outer cuboidal/flattened basal epithelium
  • Gland infolding may produce papillary architecture
  • Markedly enlarged glands may have compromised vascular supply → prostatic infarcts; adjacent squamous metaplasia may occur

Effects of BPH

  • Prostatic enlargement + stromal smooth muscle contraction → urinary obstruction
  • ↑Resistance to urinary outflow → bladder hypertrophy & distension + urine retention
  • Incomplete bladder emptying → reservoir of residual urine → common source of infection

Clinical Features

  • Increased urinary frequency & nocturia
  • Difficulty starting and stopping the urinary stream
  • Overflow dribbling
  • Dysuria (painful micturition)
  • ↑Risk of bacterial infections of the bladder and kidney
  • Sudden acute urinary retention (requires emergency catheterisation)

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.258–260; Robbins 10e pp.976–977

12 · Prostatic Carcinoma

🔴 Describe the morphology, spread, clinical features, and treatment of prostatic carcinoma.

Prostate cancer is the second most common cause of cancer-related death in men (surpassed only by lung cancer).

Incidence: Men over age 50.

Etiology

  • Age, race, family history
  • Hormone levels (androgen dependence)
  • Environmental influences
  • Precursor lesion: prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia (PIN)

Morphology — Gross

  • In ~70% of cases, arises in the peripheral zone
  • Cross-section: neoplastic tissue is gritty and firm

Morphology — Microscopic

  • Glands arranged in well-defined, easily recognised patterns
  • Glands typically smaller than benign glands; lined by a single uniform layer of cuboidal or low columnar epithelium
  • Malignant glands: tightly packed cells, lacking branching and papillary infoldings
  • Outer basal cell layer is absent (key diagnostic feature)
  • Cytoplasm ranges from pale-clear to distinctive amphophilic appearance
  • Nuclei enlarged, one or more large nucleoli; some nuclear size/shape variation but pleomorphism not marked
  • Mitotic figures uncommon

Local Extension

Most commonly involves periprostatic tissue, seminal vesicles, and base of urinary bladder → advanced disease may cause ureteral obstruction.

Metastases

  • Lymphatic: Obturator nodes → para-aortic nodes
  • Haematogenous: Chiefly to bones (axial skeleton); bony metastases are typically osteoblastic. Bones involved (descending order): lumbar spine, proximal femur, pelvis, thoracic spine, ribs

Clinical Features

  • Localised cancer: asymptomatic; discovered by suspicious nodule on DRE or ↑serum PSA
  • Advanced: urinary obstruction symptoms (difficulty starting/stopping stream, dysuria, frequency, haematuria)
  • Back pain from vertebral metastases (diagnosed by radionuclide bone scan)

Diagnosis

  • Digital rectal examination (DRE)
  • Transrectal ultrasound (TRUS)
  • Transrectal ultrasound-guided needle biopsy
  • Measurement of serum PSA levels

Treatment

  1. Surgery (radical prostatectomy)
  2. Radiation therapy
  3. Androgen deprivation therapy: orchiectomy, LHRH agonists

Grade (Gleason system) and stage are the most important prognostic factors.

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.261–262; Robbins 10e pp.978–982

13 · Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA)

🔴 What is PSA? Give its normal value, causes of raised PSA, and clinical importance.

Definition: Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is a product of prostatic epithelium, normally secreted in semen. An androgen-regulated serine protease whose function is to cleave and liquefy the seminal coagulum formed after ejaculation.

In normal men, only minute amounts circulate in the serum.

Normal value: <4 ng/mL.

Causes of raised PSA

PSA is organ-specific but NOT cancer-specific. Serum levels elevated in:

  • Carcinoma of prostate
  • Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)
  • Prostatitis
  • Prostate gland infarction
  • Instrumentation of the prostate
  • Ejaculation

Clinical importance of PSA

  1. Most important test for diagnosis and management of prostate cancer
  2. 20–40% of patients with serum PSA >4.0 ng/mL will have prostate cancer on biopsy
  3. High levels (>100 ng/mL) almost always indicate distant bone metastases
  4. Most useful for monitoring treatment response and disease progression
  5. Also a tumour marker of some breast cancers
🎯 PSA = "Prostate Specific but NOT cancer Specific": Elevated in Ca prostate, BPH, prostatitis, infarction, instrumentation, ejaculation. Useful for staging and follow-up.

Arif 15e Vol-2 pp.263; Robbins 10e p.981; Davidson's 23e pp.439, 1322, 1324

A 70-year-old male presents with difficulty in micturition, urgency, frequency. PR exam reveals palpable prostate. Likely diagnosis? Give the diagnostic workup.

Likely diagnosis: Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) / nodular hyperplasia of prostate.

Diagnostic workup

  1. Urine examination:
    • Routine — RBC, glucose, protein, pus cells
    • Culture & sensitivity (C/S) — infection
    • Cytology — if suspicious, to exclude malignancy
  2. Blood examination:
    • Complete blood count (CBC)
    • Urea, creatinine, electrolytes
    • Blood sugar
    • PSA — to exclude carcinoma prostate
  3. Ultrasound (USG) of whole abdomen
  4. Uroflowmetry
  5. Urodynamic study
  6. Intravenous urography (IVU) — usually not required unless haematuria
  7. Cystourethroscopy
  8. Transrectal ultrasound scanning (TRUS)
  9. FNAC — in suspected cases of carcinoma prostate

Arif 15e Vol-2 p.918 (typo in original = p.263 area); Robbins 10e pp.976–977

Nodular hyperplasia of prostate (DU-14Ju): related to dihydrotestosterone (DHT) stimulation; occurs in elderly male; involves peripheral region; shows proliferation of both glands and stroma; risk factor of prostate carcinoma?
DHT stimulation = True · elderly male = True · peripheral region = False (transition zone) · glands + stroma = True · risk factor for Ca = False (NOT premalignant; though Arif answer key says True for some editions).
Nodular hyperplasia of prostate (DU-10Ju): related to DHT stimulus; occurs in ~90% males over 70; involves peripheral region; histologically composed of gland + stroma; is a risk factor for adenocarcinoma?
DHT = True · 90% over 70 = False · peripheral = False · gland + stroma = True · risk factor for adenocarcinoma = True (note: Arif marks this T, though clinically BPH is not truly premalignant — BPH coexists with cancer in same older population).
Carcinoma prostate is associated with (DU-18Ja): production of hormone; raised acid phosphatase; hypercalcaemia; frequent bony metastasis; raised PSA?
Production of hormone = False · raised acid phosphatase = True · hypercalcaemia = False · bony metastasis = True (osteoblastic) · raised PSA = True.
Carcinoma of prostate (DU-16Ja): hormone-dependent tumour; soft feeling on PR; related to raised acid phosphatase; associated with hypercalcaemia; metastasis causes osteosclerosis?
Hormone-dependent = True · soft on PR = False (hard, stony-hard on DRE) · raised acid phosphatase = True · hypercalcaemia = False · osteosclerosis (osteoblastic mets) = True.
Serum PSA level is raised in (DU-15Ja): acute prostatitis; nodular hyperplasia; testicular neoplasm; carcinoma of seminal vesicle; adenocarcinoma of prostate?
Acute prostatitis = True · nodular hyperplasia = False (note: Arif marks this F, but clinically BPH does raise PSA — the MCQ answer key says False) · testicular neoplasm = False · carcinoma seminal vesicle = False · adenocarcinoma prostate = True.
Prostatic carcinoma (DU-12/09Ja): is usually SCC; metastasises to lumbosacral spine; produces osteolytic lesions; regresses with oestrogen therapy; secretes alkaline phosphatase?
Usually SCC = False (adenocarcinoma) · lumbosacral spine mets = True · osteolytic = False (osteoblastic) · regresses with oestrogen = True (androgen deprivation) · secretes alkaline phosphatase = False (acid phosphatase).
🎯 One-liners: Most common testicular tumour? Most common testicular tumour in elderly? Most common tumour of the prostate? GCT analogous to ovarian dysgerminoma?
Most common testicular tumour = Seminoma · most common in elderly (>60) = Testicular lymphoma · most common prostate tumour = Adenocarcinoma · analogous to dysgerminoma = Seminoma (identical tumour arises in ovary as dysgerminoma, in CNS as germinoma).
🎯 What are testicular tumour markers? Which tumour raises AFP? Which raises hCG?
Markers: AFP (α-fetoprotein), hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin), LDH (lactate dehydrogenase). AFP ↑ in: yolk sac tumour, embryonal carcinoma. hCG ↑ in: seminoma (low), embryonal carcinoma, choriocarcinoma (high).
🎯 Define PSA. Normal value? In which conditions is it raised?
PSA = Prostate-Specific Antigen, an androgen-regulated serine protease from prostatic epithelium. Normal: <4 ng/mL. Raised in: Ca prostate, BPH, prostatitis, prostatic infarction, instrumentation, ejaculation. Organ-specific but NOT cancer-specific.
🎯 What is cryptorchidism? What are its complications? Treatment?
Cryptorchidism = failure of testicular descent into scrotum. Complications: sterility, inguinal hernia (10–20%), testicular cancer. Treatment: surgical correction (orchiopexy).
🎯 What are the types of seminoma? Which is most common? What is anaplastic seminoma?
Types: (1) Classic/typical (most common), (2) Spermatocytic, (3) Anaplastic. Classic is the most common (~50% of all testicular GCTs).
