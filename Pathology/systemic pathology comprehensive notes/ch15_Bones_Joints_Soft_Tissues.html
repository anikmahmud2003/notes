🧠 Big Picture — Bones, Joints and Soft Tissues (boss-level overview)

This chapter covers three interlinked domains:

1. Bone infections — Osteomyelitis: 🔴 Pyogenic (Staphylococcus aureus 80–90%) and tubercular forms; key morphological landmarks = sequestrum (dead bone), involucrum (reactive new bone), Brodie's abscess (intraosseous abscess); Pott's disease = TB spine → kyphosis. Chronicity from delayed diagnosis, extensive necrosis, inadequate antibiotics.

2. Bone tumours — Benign vs Malignant: 🔴 Primary tumours classified by matrix: osteogenic (osteosarcoma = most common primary malignant; osteoid osteoma, osteoblastoma = benign), chondrogenic (chondrosarcoma = 2nd most common malignant; osteochondroma = most frequent benign), unknown origin (Ewing sarcoma = childhood round-cell tumour with onion-skin periosteal reaction; giant cell tumour/osteoclastoma = locally aggressive epiphyseal lesion), notochordal (chordoma). Secondary metastases: prostate, breast, kidney, lung (adults); neuroblastoma, Wilms (children).

3. Joint diseases: 📌 Osteoarthritis (OA) = mechanical/degenerative (weight-bearing joints, cartilage degeneration, osteophytes); Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) = autoimmune (CD4⁺ T cells, pannus, symmetric small joints, ACPA/rheumatoid factor); gout = crystal arthropathy; infectious arthritis; reactive arthritis (Reiter syndrome = arthritis + urethritis + conjunctivitis).

4. Soft tissue tumours: 🎯 Lipoma (most common soft tissue tumour), liposarcoma, rhabdomyoma/myosarcoma, leiomyoma/myosarcoma, haemangioma, angiosarcoma, schwannoma, neurofibroma, synovial sarcoma, fibrosarcoma, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma.

5. Pathological fractures: fracture through diseased bone — causes: osteomyelitis, benign tumours (osteoclastoma), malignant tumours (osteosarcoma, Ewing, metastatic thyroid/kidney/breast), metabolic (osteoporosis, osteomalacia), cystic (aneurysmal bone cyst).

1 · Osteomyelitis — Definition, Classification & Routes

🔴 Define osteomyelitis and classify it according to clinical course and aetiology.

Osteomyelitis: inflammation of bone and marrow, virtually always secondary to infection. It frequently manifests as a primary solitary focus of disease but can also be a complication of any systemic infection. All types of organisms — including viruses, parasites, fungi, and bacteria — can produce osteomyelitis, but certain pyogenic bacteria and mycobacteria are the most common culprits.

A) Classification by clinical course

  1. Acute suppurative osteomyelitis
  2. Chronic osteomyelitis:
    • Chronic suppurative osteomyelitis
    • Tubercular osteomyelitis
    • Syphilitic lesions of bone

B) Classification by aetiology

GroupTypes
1. Bacterial (most common)Pyogenic osteomyelitis · Tubercular osteomyelitis · Syphilitic osteomyelitis
2. ViralViral osteomyelitis
3. FungalFungal osteomyelitis
4. ParasiticParasitic osteomyelitis

Robbins 10e p.1185; Arif 2025 Vol-2 p.348

Enlist the routes by which bacteria enter the bone.

Bacteria may reach the bone by:

  1. Haematogenous spread (most common route, especially in children)
  2. Extension from a contiguous site (e.g. nearby soft tissue infection, open fracture)
  3. Direct implantation (e.g. in traumatic injury, surgical procedures)

In adults, osteomyelitis more often occurs as a complication of open fractures, surgical procedures, and infections of the feet in the setting of diabetes.

Robbins 10e p.1185

2 · Chronic Osteomyelitis — Pathogenesis, Predisposing Factors & Etiologic Agents

🔴 Describe the aetiopathogenesis and predisposing factors of chronic osteomyelitis. What are the etiologic agents?

Definition: "A severe, persistent and incapacitating infection of bone and bone marrow."

A) Predisposing factors

  1. History of trauma
  2. Debilitation
  3. Immunodeficiency state
  4. Sickle cell anaemia (especially Salmonella osteomyelitis)

B) Etiologic agents

GroupOrganisms
1. Pyogenic bacteriaStaphylococcus aureus (80–90%) · Group B streptococci · E. coli · Pseudomonas · Klebsiella · Salmonella · Haemophilus influenzae
2. MycobacteriaTubercular osteomyelitis (Mycobacterium tuberculosis)

C) Pathogenesis & morphology — sequential steps

  1. Localised infection at metaphyseal endosteum
  2. Bacteria proliferate → induce acute inflammatory reaction → cell death
  3. Entrapped bone undergoes necrosis within the first 48 hours
  4. Bacteria and inflammation spread within the shaft → percolate through the Haversian system to reach the periosteum
  5. Subperiosteal abscess formation → lifts the periosteum
  6. Impairs blood supply → ischaemia and suppurative necrosis of bone
  7. Segmental bone necrosis → formation of a piece of dead bone = sequestrum
  8. Formation of soft tissue abscess → rupture through periosteum → draining sinus
  9. After ~1 week, chronic inflammatory cells cause fibrous growth and formation of reactive bone around the devitalised bone = involucrum
  10. The interosseous stenotic abscess = Brodie's abscess

Robbins 10e pp.1185–1186; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.349–350

When does acute osteomyelitis turn into the chronic form?

In 5–25% of cases, acute osteomyelitis fails to resolve and persists as chronic infection. Chronicity may develop in the following situations:

  • Delay in diagnosis
  • Extensive bone necrosis
  • Inadequate antibiotic therapy
  • Inadequate surgical debridement
  • Weakened host defence

Robbins Basic Pathology 10e p.807; Arif 2025 Vol-2 p.351

3 · Sequestrum, Brodie's Abscess & Involucrum

🔴 What is a sequestrum? Describe its cause, mechanism, and fate.

Sequestrum: a dead piece of bone within a living bone, produced as a result of avascular necrosis from underlying infection, ultimately separated by osteoclasts.

Cause: Chronic pyogenic osteomyelitis.

Mechanism of formation: bacteria at the metaphyseal endosteum → acute inflammation → bone necrosis within 48 h → spread through Haversian canals → subperiosteal abscess → periosteal lifting → ischaemia → segmental necrosis of bone → dead bone fragment = sequestrum.

Fate of sequestrum (depends on wound size):

  • If small → can be absorbed by osteoclastic activity and replaced by granulation tissue (resolution)
  • If large → persists for long with chronic infection inside and discharging sinus

Robbins 10e p.1186; Arif 2025 Vol-2 p.351

🔴 What is Brodie's abscess?

Brodie's abscess: a small intraosseous abscess that frequently involves the cortex, walled off by reactive bone. It is a sequelae of chronic osteomyelitis.

Alternatively: a circumscribed, round/oval cavity containing pus and pieces of dead bone (sequestra) surrounded by sclerosis.

Robbins Basic Pathology 10e p.807; Arif 2025 Vol-2 p.351

What is an involucrum? (VIVA favourite)

Involucrum: the reactive lamellar/woven bone that forms around devitalised (necrotic) bone in chronic osteomyelitis. It is produced by chronic inflammatory cells about one week after the initial infection, and represents the body's attempt to wall off and stabilise the necrotic segment.

Together with sequestrum (dead bone inside) and a cloaca (opening in the involucrum through which sequestra and pus may extrude), the involucrum is a hallmark of chronic osteomyelitis morphology.

Robbins 10e p.1186; SMU-23M exam question

4 · Clinical Features — Pyogenic vs Tuberculous Osteomyelitis

🔴 Compare the clinical features of pyogenic and tuberculous osteomyelitis.

Pyogenic osteomyelitis

  • Malaise
  • Fever
  • Chills
  • Leukocytosis
  • Intense throbbing pain over the infected bone
  • Characteristic X-ray findings: lytic focus of bone destruction surrounded by a zone of sclerosis
  • Blood cultures can be positive (in untreated cases)

Tuberculous osteomyelitis

  • Localised pain
  • Low-grade fever
  • Chills
  • Weight loss

Robbins 10e pp.1186–1187; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.351–352

🔴 What is Pott's disease? Describe its clinical features and complications.

Pott's disease: tuberculous osteomyelitis of the spine (especially thoracic and lumbar vertebrae), which is the most common site of skeletal TB.

In the spine, infection breaks through intervertebral discs to involve multiple vertebrae and extends into the surrounding soft tissues, forming abscesses.

Clinical features

  • Localised pain
  • Low-grade fever
  • Chills
  • Weight loss

Complications

  • Permanent compression fracture of discs & vertebrae → severe scoliosis, kyphosis, and neurologic deficits
  • Tuberculous arthritis
  • Sinus tract formation
  • Psoas abscess
  • Amyloidosis (secondary AA amyloid from chronic inflammation)

Robbins 10e pp.1186–1187; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.352–353

5 · Diseases Associated with Abnormal Mineral Homeostasis

What are the diseases associated with abnormal mineral homeostasis of bone?

Three main disease groups:

  1. Rickets (children) and osteomalacia (adults) — defective mineralisation of osteoid
  2. Hyperparathyroidism — excess parathyroid hormone (PTH) → increased osteoclastic bone resorption
  3. Renal osteodystrophy — bone disease secondary to chronic kidney disease (includes osteitis fibrosa cystica, osteomalacia, osteosclerosis, osteoporosis)

Arif 2025 Vol-2 p.353

8 · Bone-Forming Tumours (Osteogenic)

What are the bone-forming tumours? Describe their key features.

Bone-forming tumours are characterised by production of bone by neoplastic cells. The tumour bone is usually deposited as woven trabeculae (except in osteomas) and is variably mineralised.

The bone-forming tumours are:

  • Osteoid osteoma (benign)
  • Osteoblastoma (benign)
  • Osteosarcoma (malignant — most common primary malignant bone tumour)

Robbins 10e p.1187; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.354

10 · Osteosarcoma

🔴 Describe osteosarcoma — definition, age, sites, morphology (gross + microscopic), clinical features, and radiographic findings.

Osteosarcoma: the most common primary malignant tumour of bone, accounting for approximately 20% of bone cancers.

Age & demographics

  • Bimodal age distribution: 75% occur before 20 years of age; smaller peak in older adults (>60 yr) — frequently associated with predisposing conditions: Paget disease of bone, infarcts, and prior radiation (sometimes called secondary osteosarcomas)
  • Males > females (ratio 1.6 : 1)

Sites of origin

Tumours usually arise in the metaphyseal region of long bones; almost 50% are near the knee — distal femur or proximal tibia. Although any bone can be involved.

Morphology — Gross

  • Bulky, gritty, grey-white tumours often containing haemorrhage and cystic degeneration
  • Frequently destroy surrounding cortices and produce soft tissue masses
  • Spread extensively in the medullary canal, replacing haematopoietic marrow
  • Infrequently, they penetrate the epiphyseal plate, or enter the joint

Morphology — Microscopic

  • Tumour cells vary in size and shape (pleomorphism) with large hyperchromatic nuclei
  • Bizarre tumour giant cells and abundant mitoses including abnormal (e.g. tripolar) forms are common
  • Extensive necrosis and intravascular invasion are common
  • If neoplastic cells produce abundant cartilage → classified as chondroblastic osteosarcoma

Clinical features

  • Painful progressively enlarged mass
  • Pathologic fractures

Metastases: lungs, bones, brain, and elsewhere.

Radiographic findings

  • Destructive, mixed lytic and blastic mass with infiltrative margins
  • Frequently breaks through the cortex and lifts the periosteum, inducing reactive periosteal bone formation
  • Codman triangle — triangular shadow between the cortex and raised periosteal ends (indicates aggressive tumour)

Robbins 10e pp.1188; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.354–356

A 17-year-old male presents with pain and progressive swelling around the right knee. X-ray shows mixed osteoblastic and lytic mass at the lower part of the femur. What is your diagnosis?

Diagnosis: Osteosarcoma

The age group (adolescent/young adult), metaphyseal location around the knee (distal femur), and the mixed lytic/blastic X-ray appearance are all classic features of osteosarcoma.

Arif 2025 Vol-2 p.356

11 · Chondrosarcoma

🔴 Describe chondrosarcoma — definition, subtypes, age, sites, and morphology.

Chondrosarcoma: malignant cartilage-producing tumours. After osteosarcoma, it is the second most common malignant matrix-producing tumour of bone.

Subtypes

Conventional · Clear cell · Dedifferentiated · Mesenchymal.

Age & demographics

Usually in their 40s or older; men affected twice as frequently as women.

Sites of origin

Commonly arise in the axial skeleton — pelvis, shoulder, and ribs. Unlike benign enchondroma, distal extremities are rarely involved.

Morphology — Gross

  • Large bulky tumours made up of nodules of glistening grey-white, translucent cartilage
  • Matrix is often gelatinous or myxoid and can ooze from the cut surface
  • Spotty calcifications typically present; central necrosis may create cystic spaces
  • Tumour spreads through the cortex into surrounding muscle and fat

Morphology — Microscopic

  • Malignant cartilage infiltrates the marrow space & surrounds pre-existing bony trabeculae
  • Tumours vary in degree of cellularity, cytologic atypia & mitotic activity

Robbins 10e p.1191; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.356–357

12 · Ewing Sarcoma

🔴 Describe Ewing sarcoma — definition, age, sites, morphology (gross + histology), clinical features, and radiographic findings.

Ewing sarcoma: a malignant bone tumour characterised by primitive round cells without obvious differentiation. Accounts for approximately 6–10% of primary malignant bone tumours and is the second most common group of bone sarcomas in children.

Age & demographics

Most affected individuals are 10 to 15 years old; approximately 80% are younger than 20 years. Boys affected slightly more frequently than girls.

Sites of origin

Usually arises in the diaphysis of long tubular bones, especially the femur and the flat bones of the pelvis. Presents as a painful, enlarging mass.

Morphology — Gross

  • Arises in the medullary cavity and invades the cortex, periosteum, and soft tissue
  • Soft, tan-white tumour with areas of haemorrhage and necrosis

Morphology — Histology

  • Sheets of uniform small, round cells with scant cytoplasm that may be clear due to abundant glycogen
  • Homer-Wright rosettes (rounded cell clusters with a central fibrillary core) → indicate neuroectodermal differentiation
  • Fibrous septae may be present; little stroma
  • Geographic necrosis may be prominent

Clinical features

  • Painful enlarging masses; affected site is tender, warm and swollen
  • Some may have fever, high ESR, anaemia, leukocytosis

Radiographic findings

  • Destructive lytic tumour with permeative or moth-eaten margins extending into surrounding soft tissues
  • Characteristic periosteal reaction producing layers of reactive bone in an "onion-skin" fashion

Robbins 10e p.1192; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.357–358

13 · Giant Cell Tumour (Osteoclastoma)

🔴 Describe the giant cell tumour of bone — definition, age, sites, behaviour, and morphology (gross + microscopic).

Giant cell tumour: so named because it is characterised by the presence of numerous multinucleated osteoclast-type giant cells. Also called osteoclastoma. Although classified as benign, it can be locally aggressive.

Age & demographics

Uncommon tumour; usually arises in the third to fifth decades of life (20–40 years).

Sites of origin

Develop within the epiphysis and may extend into the metaphysis. The majority are near the knee (distal femur or proximal tibia), but virtually any bone can be involved.

Morphology — Gross (macroscopic)

  • Large, red-brown mass
  • Frequently undergo cystic degeneration

Morphology — Microscopic

  • Consist of uniform oval mononuclear tumour cells and abundant osteoclast-type giant cells with 100 or more nuclei
  • Necrosis and mitotic activity may be prominent
  • Although reactive bone may be present (especially at periphery), tumour cells do NOT synthesise bone or cartilage

Robbins 10e p.1193; Arif 2025 Vol-2 pp.358–359

What is osteoarthritis (OA)?

Osteoarthritis (OA), also called degenerative joint disease, is characterised by cartilage degeneration that results in structural and functional failure of synovial joints. It is the most common type of joint disease.

Primary pathogenic abnormality = mechanical injury to articular cartilage. Primarily involves weight-bearing joints (knees, hips). Pathology includes cartilage degeneration and fragmentation, bone spurs (osteophytes), subchondral cysts, with minimal inflammation. No serum antibodies. No involvement of other organs.

Robbins 10e pp.1197–1198; Arif 2025 Vol-2 p.361

16 · Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) — Pathogenesis & Morphology

🔴 Describe rheumatoid arthritis — definition, occurrence, pathogenesis, cytokines, and morphology (joints + extra-articular).

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA): a chronic autoimmune disorder that principally attacks the joints, producing a non-suppurative proliferative and inflammatory synovitis. RA often progresses to articular cartilage destruction and, in some cases, joint fusion (ankylosis). Extra-articular lesions may involve skin, heart, blood vessels, and lungs.

Occurrence

Three times more common in women than men. Most common in those aged 40 to 70.

Pathogenesis

RA is an autoimmune disease mediated by antibodies against self-antigens and inflammation caused by cytokines, predominantly secreted by CD4⁺ T cells. CD4⁺ T helper (TH) cells initiate the autoimmune response by reacting with an arthritogen — perhaps microbial or a chemically modified self-antigen.

Continuing autoimmune reaction → Activation of CD4⁺ T helper cells and other lymphocytes → Local release of inflammatory mediators and cytokines → Destruction of bone and cartilage in joints.

Mediators (cytokines) and their role in joint injury

Cytokine / MoleculeSourceRole
IFN-γTH cellsActivates macrophages and synovial cells
IL-17TH17 cellsRecruits neutrophils and monocytes
RANKLActivated T cellsStimulates osteoclasts → bone resorption
TNF and IL-1βMacrophagesStimulate synovial cells to secrete proteases → destroy hyaline cartilage

Morphology — Joints

  • RA typically affects small joints of the hands and feet
  • Grossly: synovium is oedematous, thickened, and hyperplastic
  • Histologic features:
    • Synovial cell hyperplasia and proliferation
    • Dense inflammatory infiltrates (often forming lymphoid follicles) of CD4⁺ helper T cells, B cells, plasma cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages
    • Increased vascularity due to angiogenesis
    • Fibrinopurulent exudate on the synovial and joint surfaces
    • Osteoclastic activity in subchondral bone → inflamed synovium penetrates bone → periarticular erosions and subchondral cysts
    • Pannus formation (exuberant synovium invading and destroying cartilage)

17 · Reiter Syndrome

What is Reiter syndrome? (VIVA)

Reiter syndrome: a form of reactive arthritis, defined by the triad:

  1. Arthritis
  2. Non-gonococcal urethritis or cervicitis
  3. Conjunctivitis

🎯 "Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree" = conjunctivitis + urethritis + arthritis.

🔁 Rapid-Fire MCQ & Viva Sweep (incl. folded Systemic Pathology MCQs)

Bones, Joints & Soft Tissues — True/False & one-liners (test yourself, then reveal)
Characteristics of pyogenic osteomyelitis: (a) Always occurs by blood-borne infection? (b) May be induced by viruses? (c) Commonest organism is E. coli? (d) Sequestrum formation? (e) Chronicity is a common consequence?
(a) False — can also occur by direct implantation or contiguous spread. (b) True — viruses can rarely cause it. (c) False — S. aureus is the commonest (80–90%). (d) False — sequestrum is a feature of chronic, not acute, osteomyelitis (MCQ answer is F). (e) True — in 5–25% of cases chronicity develops.
Ewing sarcoma is characterized by: (a) Involvement of long bone only? (b) Metaphysis commonly affected? (c) Onion skin appearance radiologically? (d) Painful presentation common? (e) Prognosis always favourable?
(a) False — also flat bones (pelvis, ribs). (b) False — diaphysis is commonly affected. (c) True — classic onion-skin periosteal reaction. (d) True — painful enlarging mass. (e) False — aggressive tumour, prognosis variable.
Malignant tumours of bone are: (a) Paget's disease? (b) Osteoblastoma? (c) Multiple myeloma? (d) Ewing's tumour? (e) Chordoma?
(a) False — Paget's is a metabolic bone disease. (b) False — osteoblastoma is benign. (c) True. (d) True. (e) True — chordoma is malignant (notochordal).
Osteosarcoma: (a) Is a secondary malignant tumour of bone? (b) Is a malignant tumour that produces cartilage? (c) Majority occurs in younger men? (d) Presents as painful gradually enlarging mass? (e) Majority occurs around the wrist joint?
(a) False — it is the most common primary malignant tumour. (b) False — it produces bone (osteoid). (c) True — M:F = 1.6:1, 75% before age 20. (d) True. (e) False — majority around the knee (distal femur/proximal tibia).
Osteomyelitis is related to: (a) Pathological fracture? (b) Sequestrum formation? (c) No new bone formation? (d) Salmonella typhi may cause it? (e) Only bone is inflamed?
(a) True. (b) True. (c) False — new bone (involucrum) does form. (d) True — especially in sickle cell patients. (e) False — marrow and soft tissues are also affected.
Pyogenic osteomyelitis: (a) Occurs by blood-borne infection? (b) Is caused by bacteria? (c) Is caused frequently by Streptococcus? (d) Frequently shows granuloma? (e) Frequently reveals new bone formation?
(a) True — most common route. (b) True. (c) False — S. aureus is the most frequent. (d) False — pyogenic = neutrophilic, not granulomatous. (e) True — involucrum formation.
Bone-forming tumours include: (a) Giant cell tumour? (b) Osteoid osteoma? (c) Enchondroma? (d) Chondroblastoma? (e) Osteosarcoma?
(a) False — giant cell tumour is of unknown origin. (b) True. (c) False — enchondroma is cartilage-forming. (d) False — chondroblastoma is cartilage-forming. (e) True.
Ewing's sarcoma: (a) Is a childhood tumour? (b) Occurs in epiphysis of long bone? (c) Associated with p53 gene mutation? (d) Has prominent necrosis? (e) Has characteristic lytic lesion in X-ray?
(a) True — 80% <20 yr. (b) False — occurs in the diaphysis. (c) False — associated with EWS-FLI1 translocation (t(11;22)). (d) True — geographic necrosis. (e) True — destructive lytic tumour with permeative margins.
Osteomalacia: (a) May occur in coeliac disease? (b) Associated with normal serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP)? (c) Characterised by excessive osteoid formation which is poorly mineralised? (d) May be complicated by treatment with phenobarbitone? (e) Associated with atrophy of parathyroid gland?
(a) True — vitamin D malabsorption. (b) False — serum ALP is elevated. (c) True — defective mineralisation. (d) True — phenobarbitone induces vitamin D metabolism. (e) False — parathyroid hormone (PTH) is usually elevated secondary (secondary hyperparathyroidism).
🎯 One-liner: Most common organism in osteomyelitis? Most common primary malignant bone tumour? Most frequent benign bone tumour? Classic X-ray of Ewing? Codman triangle seen in?
S. aureus (80–90%) · Osteosarcoma · Osteochondroma · Onion-skin periosteal reaction · Osteosarcoma.
🎯 Mnemonic: "Pott's disease" = ? · "Onion skin" periosteal reaction = ? · "Codman triangle" = ? · "Pannus" = ? · "Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree" = ?
Pott's disease = TB of spine · Onion skin = Ewing sarcoma · Codman triangle = Osteosarcoma · Pannus = Rheumatoid arthritis · Can't see/pee/climb = Reiter syndrome.
🎯 Most common site of skeletal TB? Most common primary bone tumour in children (after excluding haematopoietic)? Giant cell tumour location? Most common soft tissue tumour? Most common site of osteosarcoma?
Thoracic/lumbar spine (Pott's) · Ewing sarcoma · Epiphysis of long bones (near knee) · Lipoma · Metaphysis of long bones (distal femur/proximal tibia).
🎯 Rheumatoid arthritis is associated with which serum antibodies? OA has which serum antibodies? RA mainly affects which joints first? OA mainly affects which joints?
RA: ACPA (anti-citrullinated peptide antibody) + rheumatoid factor · OA: none · RA: small joints of hands and feet · OA: weight-bearing joints (knees, hips).
🎯 Name the cytokines in RA joint destruction: which cytokine activates macrophages? which recruits neutrophils? which stimulates osteoclasts? which stimulates protease secretion?
IFN-γ (from TH cells) activates macrophages · IL-17 (from TH17) recruits neutrophils · RANKL (from T cells) stimulates osteoclasts · TNF and IL-1β (from macrophages) stimulate protease secretion.
🎯 Homer-Wright rosettes are seen in which tumour? Onion-skin periosteal reaction? Codman triangle? Multinucleated giant cells with 100+ nuclei?
Homer-Wright rosettes = Ewing sarcoma · Onion-skin = Ewing sarcoma · Codman triangle = Osteosarcoma · Giant cells with 100+ nuclei = Giant cell tumour (osteoclastoma).
🎯 Secondary bone tumours in adults: most common primary sites? In children? Which tumour causes pathological fracture in sickle cell patients?
Adults: prostate, breast, kidney, lung · Children: neuroblastoma, Wilms tumour, osteosarcoma, Ewing, rhabdomyosarcoma · Salmonella osteomyelitis in sickle cell patients → pathological fracture.
🎯 What is Reiter syndrome? What are its three components? Name the mnemonic.
Reiter syndrome = reactive arthritis triad: arthritis + non-gonococcal urethritis/cervicitis + conjunctivitis. Mnemonic: "Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree."
🎯 In osteomyelitis pathogenesis: what is a sequestrum? What is an involucrum? What is a Brodie's abscess? What is a cloaca?
Sequestrum = dead piece of bone separated by osteoclasts · Involucrum = reactive new bone formed around sequestrum · Brodie's abscess = small intraosseous abscess walled off by reactive bone · Cloaca = opening in involucrum through which pus and sequestra extrude.
🎯 Carcinoma of prostate is associated with: raised acid phosphatase? frequent bony metastasis? raised PSA? osteosclerotic or osteolytic bone metastases?
Raised acid phosphatase = True · Frequent bony metastasis = True · Raised PSA = True · Osteosclerotic (blastic) bone metastases.
