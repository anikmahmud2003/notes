🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of Blood Vessels (boss-level overview)

Two principal mechanisms underlie ALL vascular disease: (1) narrowing/obstruction of the lumen (progressive → atherosclerosis; rapid → thrombosis/embolism) and (2) weakening of vessel walls → dilation or rupture (aneurysm/dissection).

The full classification (VIVA favourite): 1. Congenital anomalies · 2. Hypertensive vascular disease (HTN) · 3. Arteriosclerosis (→ ATHEROSCLEROSIS = the big one) · 4. Aneurysms & dissection · 5. Vasculitis (infectious + non-infectious) · 6. Raynaud phenomenon · 7. Diseases of veins · 8. Diseases of lymphatics · 9. Tumours (benign → intermediate → malignant).

Robbins 10e, Ch 11 (Blood Vessels), pp. 485–526 · Arif 15e (2025) Vol-2, pp.21–44

1 · Vascular Structure & Function

What are the 3 layers of a vessel wall (outside → in)?

In all vessels except capillaries: a supportive adventitia (external layer), a smooth-muscle media, and an endothelium-lined intima separated from the media by the internal elastic lamina. The external elastic lamina separates adventitia from media in many arteries.

Robbins 10e p.485

How do capillaries differ in structure?

About the diameter of a red cell (7–8 μm), endothelial lining but no media; variable numbers of pericytes (smooth-muscle-like cells) lie just deep to the endothelium.

Robbins 10e p.485

Name the 3 types of arteries by size/structure.
TypeExamples
1. Large / elasticAorta + major branches (innominate, subclavian, common carotid, iliac) and pulmonary arteries
2. Medium / muscularSmaller aortic branches (coronary and renal arteries)
3. Small arteries (<2 mm) & arterioles (20–100 μm)Within tissues and organs

Robbins 10e p.485

What do lymphatics do?

Thin-walled channels lined by specialized endothelium; return interstitial tissue fluid and inflammatory cells to the bloodstream.

Robbins 10e p.485

What are the 2 principal mechanisms of vascular disease?

(1) Narrowing (stenosis) or complete obstruction of the lumen — progressive (atherosclerosis) or precipitous (thrombosis/embolism). (2) Weakening of vessel walls → dilation or rupture.

Robbins 10e p.518

2 · Congenital Anomalies of Blood Vessels

Name the 4 congenital anomalies of blood vessels.
  1. Developmental / berry aneurysms — cerebral vessels; rupture → fatal intracerebral haemorrhage.
  2. Arteriovenous (AV) fistulas — direct connections (usually small) between arteries & veins, bypass capillaries.
  3. Fibromuscular dysplasia — focal irregular thickening in medium & large muscular arteries (renal, carotid, splanchnic, vertebral) → "string-of-beads" on angiography.
  4. Anomalous coronary artery origin — both coronary arteries arise over the same coronary cusp of the aortic valve.

Robbins 10e p.487

Give the causes (aetiology) of an AV fistula.
  • Developmental defects
  • Rupture of an arterial aneurysm into the adjacent vein
  • Penetrating injuries that pierce arteries and veins
  • Inflammatory necrosis of adjacent vessels
  • Surgically generated AV fistulas (for haemodialysis)

Robbins 10e p.487

Clinical importance of congenital vascular anomalies?
  • Rupture → intracerebral haemorrhage.
  • Large/multiple AV fistulas shunt blood arterial→venous → heart pumps extra volume → high-output cardiac failure.

Robbins 10e p.487

3 · Hypertension

Define hypertension.

US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute: a sustained diastolic pressure >89 mmHg, or a sustained systolic pressure >139 mmHg = clinically significant hypertension.

Essential (primary) HTN: 90–95% of all cases.

Robbins 10e pp.489–490

Define malignant (accelerated) hypertension.

A clinical syndrome with severe pressure elevation (SBP >200, DBP >120 mmHg), renal failure, and retinal haemorrhages & exudates, with or without papilloedema. May arise de novo, but more often superimposed on pre-existing "benign" HTN.

Robbins 10e p.490

List the complications of hypertension.
  • ↑Risk of atherosclerosis
  • Cardiac hypertrophy & heart failure (hypertensive heart disease)
  • Myocardial infarction
  • Multi-infarct dementia
  • Aortic dissection
  • Renal failure
  • Cerebral haemorrhage (stroke)
  • Hypertensive retinopathy
  • Hypertensive encephalopathy

4 · Arteriosclerosis (Hardening of the Arteries)

Define arteriosclerosis.

A generic term for arterial wall thickening and loss of elasticity (literally "hardening of the arteries").

Robbins 10e p.493

Classify arteriosclerosis — the 4 patterns.
TypeAffectsKey features
1. ArteriolosclerosisSmall arteries & arteriolesMay cause downstream ischaemic injury. Two variants: Hyaline (hyaline thickenīng) & Hyperplastic (concentric, laminated "onion-skin")
2. Mönckeberg medial calcific sclerosisMuscular arteries (adults >50)Calcification of medial walls, starting along internal elastic membrane. Does NOT narrow the lumen.
3. Fibromuscular intimal hyperplasiaMuscular arteries > arteriolesHealing response to inflammation (healed arteritis, transplant arteriopathy) or mechanical injury (stents, angioplasty)
4. AtherosclerosisIntima of large/medium arteriesMost frequent & clinically important — intimal fibrous plaques (atheromas)

Robbins 10e p.493; Harsh Mohan 7e p.371

Distinguish arteriosclerosis vs atherosclerosis.

Arteriosclerosis = generic wall thickening + loss of elasticity. Atherosclerosis = the most frequent/important variety, with intimal lesions (atheromas) that protrude into & obstruct the lumen and weaken the underlying tunica media.

🎯 Arteriosclerosis = "AAA-M": Arteriolosclerosis · Atherosclerosis · Mönckeberg · (Fibromuscular hyperplasia). Hyaline = Harmless(ish) HTN; Onion-skin = Ominous (malignant HTN).

Robbins 10e p.493

5 · ATHEROSCLEROSIS — THE KEY CHAPTER

Define atherosclerosis and atheroma.

Atherosclerosis: a vascular-disease pattern with intimal lesions called atheromas (atheromatous/atherosclerotic plaques) that protrude into & obstruct the vascular lumen and weaken the underlying tunica media.

Atheroma / atherosclerotic plaque: a fully developed atherosclerotic lesion — also called fibrous plaque, fibrofatty plaque, or atheroma.

Robbins 10e pp.493, 501

Give the common sites of atherosclerosis (in order).

(1) Lower abdominal aorta & iliac arteries → (2) Coronary arteries → (3) Popliteal arteries → (4) Internal carotid arteries → (5) Vessels of the circle of Willis.

Robbins 10e p.501

Gross morphology of an atheromatous plaque.
  • Colour: yellow-tan, raised; thrombus over ulcerated plaques → red-brown.
  • Size: vary; may coalesce.
  • Shape: patchy, rarely circumferential → involve only a portion → eccentric on cross-section.
  • Cut section: central (necrotic) core covered by a subendothelial fibrous cap:
    • Fibrous cap: SMC + dense collagen.
    • Necrotic core: lipid (cholesterol & esters), debris, foam cells, fibrin, thrombus, plasma proteins.
  • Periphery: neovascularisation.

Robbins 10e pp.499–502

Microscopy of an atheromatous plaque — 4 principal components.
  1. Cells: variable SMC, macrophages, T lymphocytes
  2. Extracellular matrix (ECM): collagen, elastic fibres, proteoglycans
  3. Intracellular & extracellular lipids: mostly cholesterol & cholesterol ester
  4. Calcifications (later-stage plaques)

Some ("fibrous plaques") are almost exclusively SMC + fibrous tissue & may be heavily calcified.

Robbins 10e p.502

Complications of an atheromatous plaque.

(1) Mechanical obstruction of blood flow; (2) Rupture → catastrophic obstructive vascular thrombosis; (3) Plaques increase diffusion distance lumen→media → ischaemic injury & wall weakening → aneurysm formation.

Robbins 10e p.494

Robbins 10e pp.494, 503

What is a foam cell?

Lipid-laden macrophages and lipid-laden smooth-muscle cells (foamy cytoplasm full of ingested lipid). Monocyte/macrophage and SMC derived.

Robbins 10e p.499

What is a fatty streak and its significance?

Lesions under the endothelium of closely-packed foam cells + lipid-containing elongated SMC + a few lymphoid cells + small extracellular lipid, collagen, proteoglycans. Begin as small flat yellow macules → coalesce into elongated streaks (≥1 cm). Prominent in aorta & major arteries, more on the posterior than anterior wall.

Significance: not raised, no significant flow disturbance — but are precursor lesions of atheromatous plaques.

Robbins 10e pp.499, 501

Non-modifiable risk factors for atherosclerosis.
  1. Genetic abnormalities
  2. Family history (most important independent risk factor)
  3. Increasing age
  4. Male gender

Robbins 10e p.494

Modifiable (controllable) risk factors for atherosclerosis.
  1. Hyperlipidaemia (esp. hypercholesterolaemia)
  2. Hypertension
  3. Cigarette smoking
  4. Diabetes

Robbins 10e p.494

Additional / other risk factors for atherosclerosis.

Additional

  • Inflammation & elevated CRP
  • Hyperhomocystinaemia
  • Metabolic syndrome
  • Lipoprotein(a) — Lp(a)
  • Prothrombotic factors, elevated fibrinogen

Other

  • Obesity
  • Lack of exercise
  • High carbohydrate intake
  • Competitive, stressful lifestyle (type A personality)

Robbins 10e pp.494–497

Detail on key risk factors: genetics, age, gender.
  • Genetics: family history = most important independent risk factor.
  • Age: plaques manifest in middle age/later (40–60 yr).
  • Gender: more common in men, but ↑ postmenopausal women.

Robbins 10e p.494

Detail on hyperlipidaemia as a risk factor.

Hypercholesterolaemia is sufficient alone to initiate lesions; atherosclerosis ∝ ↑LDL ("bad cholesterol") and ↓HDL ("good cholesterol").

Robbins 10e p.494

Detail on HTN, diabetes, metabolic syndrome as risk factors.
  • Hypertension: prematurely accelerates atherosclerosis; ↑risk of IHD (ischaemic heart disease) by ~60%.
  • Diabetes: induces hypercholesterolaemia, ↑atherosclerosis markedly; peripheral vascular occlusive disease → gangrene; MI twice as high in diabetics.
  • Metabolic syndrome: insulin resistance + HTN + dyslipidaemia (↑LDL, ↓HDL) + hypercoagulability + proinflammatory state.

Robbins 10e pp.494–497

State the etio-pathogenesis of atherosclerosis (sequence).
  1. Endothelial injury & dysfunction → ↑vascular permeability, leukocyte adhesion, thrombosis.
  2. Accumulation of lipoproteins (mainly LDL + oxidised forms) in vessel wall.
  3. Monocyte adhesion → migration into intima → macrophages & foam cells.
  4. Platelet adhesion.
  5. Factor release from activated platelets, macrophages, wall cells → SMC recruitment.
  6. SMC proliferation + ECM production + T-cell recruitment.
  7. Lipid accumulation — extracellular & intracellular.
  8. Calcification (late).

Robbins 10e p.497

Role of lipids in atherogenesis — general.

Hyperlipidaemia may initiate endothelial injury, promote foam-cell formation, act as a monocyte chemotactic factor, inhibit macrophage motility, or injure SMCs.

Robbins 10e p.498

Mechanism A of lipids — impaired endothelial-cell function.

Chronic hypercholesterolaemia ↑local reactive oxygen species → membrane/mitochondrial damage; free radicals accelerate nitric oxide (NO) decay, dampening vasodilation.

Robbins 10e p.498

Mechanism B of lipids — modified (oxidised) LDL.
  • Lipoproteins accumulate in intima; aggregate or become oxidised by free radicals from inflammatory cells.
  • Oxidised LDL is ingested by macrophages/SMCs but cannot be fully degraded → chronic ingestion → foam cells; lipid-filled macrophages = fatty streaks.
  • Oxidised LDL is toxic to endothelial cells, SMCs, macrophages → endothelial dysfunction.
  • Oxidised LDL stimulates growth factors, cytokines, chemokines → vicious inflammatory cycle.

Robbins 10e p.498

Robbins 10e pp.502, 494

Prevention of atherosclerosis.

1. Primary prevention

  • Cessation of cigarette smoking
  • Control of hypertension
  • Weight reduction
  • Increase exercise
  • Moderation of alcohol
  • Lower total & LDL cholesterol while raising HDL

2. Secondary prevention

Prevent recurrence of events (e.g., MI).

Robbins 10e pp.519–522

Short note — glomus tumour.

Definition: benign but exquisitely painful tumour of modified smooth-muscle cells of the glomus body (a specialised AV thermoregulatory structure).

Location: distal digits, especially under the fingernails. Treatment: excision is curative.

Morphology: usually <1 cm, round, slightly elevated, red-blue firm nodule. Microscopy: nests of specialised glomus cells with branching vascular channels; cells small, uniform, round/cuboidal, scant cytoplasm, ultrastructure like SMC.

Robbins 10e p.521

Short note — haemangioma (definition + types).

Haemangioma: benign neoplasm of blood vessels, blood-filled vessels. Age: infancy & childhood (7% of all benign tumours of this age). Most common site: skin of face & mucosal surfaces.

Types: capillary, cavernous, pyogenic granuloma (granulation-tissue type), juvenile/"strawberry".

Robbins 10e p.519

Capillary haemangioma morphology.

Gross: bright red-to-blue, few mm to several cm, level or slightly elevated, intact overlying epithelium.

Microscopy: unencapsulated lobules of capillary-sized, thin-walled, blood-filled vessels lined by flattened endothelium surrounded by a layer of pericytes; vessels separated by scant connective tissue; lumens may be thrombosed/organised; haemosiderin pigment; focal scarring.

Robbins 10e p.519

Cavernous haemangioma & pyogenic granuloma histology.
  • Cavernous haemangioma: thin-walled cavernous vascular spaces (fully/partly blood-filled) lined by flattened endothelium, separated by scanty stroma.
  • Pyogenic granuloma: proliferating capillaries like capillary haemangioma, but separated by abundant oedema + inflammatory infiltrate → resembles granulation tissue.

Robbins 10e p.519

Short notes — lymphangioma, angiosarcoma, spider telangiectasia.
  • Lymphangioma: benign lymphatic counterpart of haemangioma. Two types: simple (capillary) & cavernous (cystic hygroma).
  • Angiosarcoma: the malignant endothelial neoplasm of the blood vessel.
  • Spider telangiectasia: non-neoplastic malformation — radial dilated subcutaneous arteries/arterioles (legs) about a central core (body) that blanches with pressure. Location: face, neck, upper chest; associated with hyperoestrogenic states (pregnancy, cirrhosis).

Robbins 10e pp.520–522

7 · Aneurysms & Dissections

Define aneurysm.

A localised abnormal dilation of a blood vessel or the heart, congenital or acquired. In the aorta → aortic aneurysm.

Robbins 10e p.504

True vs false aneurysm.
TypeDefinition
True aneurysmInvolves all layers (intima, media, adventitia) of an intact-but-attenuated wall. Atherosclerotic, congenital vascular, and ventricular aneurysms after transmural MI.
False aneurysm (pseudoaneurysm)A wall defect → extravascular haematoma freely communicating with the intravascular space ("pulsating haematoma"). e.g., contained ventricular rupture, leak at a vascular-graft anastomosis.
Dissecting aneurysm (dissection)Blood enters through an intimal tear and splits medial lamellae, creating a blood-filled channel within the wall.

Robbins 10e pp.504–506

Classify aneurysm by shape (saccular vs fusiform).
  • Saccular: spherical outpouching involving only a portion of the wall (e.g., berry aneurysm).
  • Fusiform: diffuse, circumferential dilation of a long segment (typical AAA — abdominal aortic aneurysm).

Robbins 10e p.505

Give the causes of aneurysm.
  1. Atherosclerosis
  2. Hypertension
  3. Advanced age
  4. Smoking
  5. Trauma
  6. Vasculitis
  7. Congenital defects (fibromuscular dysplasia, berry aneurysms of circle of Willis)
  8. Infections (mycotic aneurysms)

Robbins 10e p.505

What is arterial dissection / aortic dissection?

Blood enters a defect in the arterial wall and tunnels through medial or medial-adventitial planes → blood-filled channel within the aortic wall. Often (not always) aneurysmal; disastrous if it ruptures through the adventitia.

Robbins 10e p.507

Risk factors of aortic dissection.

Hypertension is the major risk factor. Two groups:

  1. Men 40–60 with antecedent hypertension (>90% of cases).
  2. Younger patients with syndromic aortic disease (e.g., Marfan syndrome).

Robbins 10e p.507

Clinical features of aortic dissection; cause of death.

Sudden onset of excruciating pain starting in anterior chest, radiating to the back between scapulae, moving downward as dissection progresses — can be confused with MI.

Most common cause of death: rupture → bleeding into pericardial, pleural, or peritoneal cavities.

Robbins 10e p.507

8 · Vasculitis (Vessel-Wall Inflammation)

Define vasculitis.

A general term for vessel-wall inflammation, with protean manifestations depending on the vascular bed affected (CNS vs heart vs small bowel).

Clinical manifestations: fever, myalgias, arthralgias, malaise. Most vasculitides affect small vessels.

Robbins 10e p.508

Pathogenesis of vasculitis — 2 mechanisms.
  1. Infectious vasculitis: direct invasion by pathogens.
  2. Non-infectious (immune-mediated):
    • Immune-complex deposition
    • Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA)
    • Anti-endothelial-cell (anti-EC) antibodies
    • Autoreactive T cells

Robbins 10e p.508

Robbins 10e pp.508–516

Short note — giant cell (temporal) arteritis.

Chronic, classically granulomatous inflammation of large-to-small arteries. Most common vasculitis among the elderly; rare before 50. Affects arteries of the head; vertebral & ophthalmic arteries and aorta (giant cell aortitis). Ophthalmic artery involvement → sudden, permanent blindness.

Robbins 10e p.511

Short note — Takayasu arteritis.

Granulomatous vasculitis of medium & large arteries, with ocular disturbances and weak upper-extremity pulses → "pulseless disease". Classically involves the aortic arch; in ⅓ affects rest of aorta & branches, pulmonary artery in half; coronary & renal arteries may be affected. Distinguished from giant-cell by age: >50 = giant cell, <50 = Takayasu.

Robbins 10e p.511

Short note — polyarteritis nodosa (PAN).

A systemic vasculitis of small- or medium-sized muscular arteries, typically renal & visceral vessels but sparing the pulmonary circulation. Involves kidneys, heart, liver, GI tract (descending order). Mostly young adults.

Clinical: rapidly accelerating HTN, abdominal pain & bloody stools, diffuse myalgias, peripheral neuritis (esp. motor). Renal involvement = major cause of mortality. Untreated → fatal; immunosuppression → remission/cure in 90%.

Robbins 10e p.513

Short note — Kawasaki disease.

Acute, febrile, usually self-limited illness of infancy/childhood with large-to-medium arteritis (80% <4 yr). Triggered in susceptible persons by infections (mostly viral). Clinical significance = coronary artery involvement → coronary arteritis → aneurysms that rupture or thrombose → MI.

Robbins 10e p.514

Short note — granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA / Wegener).

A necrotising vasculitis with the triad:

  1. Acute necrotising granulomas of upper (ear, nose, sinuses, throat) or lower (lung) respiratory tract, or both
  2. Necrotising/granulomatous vasculitis of small-to-medium vessels (most prominent in lungs & upper airways)
  3. Focal necrotising, often crescentic, glomerulonephritis

Robbins 10e p.514

Short note — Churg-Strauss syndrome.

A small-vessel necrotising vasculitis classically associated with asthma, allergic rhinitis, lung infiltrates, peripheral eosinophilia, extravascular necrotising granulomas, and striking eosinophilic infiltration of vessels/tissues. Associations: palpable purpura, GI bleeding, renal disease. Cardiac involvement in 60% (eosinophilic cardiomyopathy) — major cause of morbidity & death.

Robbins 10e p.515

Short note — Buerger disease (thromboangiitis obliterans).

Segmental, thrombosing, acute & chronic inflammation of medium & small arteries, esp. tibial and radial → vascular insufficiency of extremities. Almost exclusively in heavy cigarette smokers, usually before age 35. Clinical: Raynaud phenomenon, instep foot pain on exercise (instep claudication), superficial nodular phlebitis, severe rest pain, chronic extremity ulcerations → frank gangrene.

Robbins 10e p.515

9 · Raynaud Phenomenon

What is Raynaud phenomenon?

Exaggerated vasoconstriction of arteries & arterioles in response to cold or emotion. Most commonly affects extremities (fingers, toes), occasionally nose, earlobes, lips. Digits show "red, white, and blue" colour changes (proximal vasodilation, central vasoconstriction, distal cyanosis).

Robbins 10e p.516

Types of Raynaud phenomenon.
  • Primary Raynaud (formerly Raynaud disease): caused by cold or emotional stress.
  • Secondary Raynaud: caused by other diseases — SLE, scleroderma, Buerger disease, or atherosclerosis.

Robbins 10e p.516

10 · Varicose Veins

What are varicose veins?

Abnormally dilated, tortuous veins produced by prolonged, increased intraluminal pressure with vessel dilation and incompetence of the venous valves.

Robbins 10e p.517

Why are the legs, oesophagus and anal canal special sites?

Superficial veins of upper & lower leg are commonly involved because venous pressures can be markedly elevated (up to 10× normal) by prolonged dependent posture. Two other special sites: oesophageal varices & haemorrhoids.

Robbins 10e p.517
