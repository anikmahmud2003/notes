🧠 Big Picture — Diseases of Blood Vessels (boss-level overview)

Two principal mechanisms underlie ALL vascular disease: (1) narrowing/obstruction of the lumen (progressive → atherosclerosis; rapid → thrombosis/embolism) and (2) weakening of vessel walls → dilation or rupture (aneurysm/dissection).

The full classification (VIVA favourite): 1. Congenital anomalies · 2. Hypertensive vascular disease (HTN) · 3. Arteriosclerosis (→ ATHEROSCLEROSIS = the big one) · 4. Aneurysms & dissection · 5. Vasculitis (infectious + non-infectious) · 6. Raynaud phenomenon · 7. Diseases of veins · 8. Diseases of lymphatics · 9. Tumours (benign → intermediate → malignant).

Robbins 10e, Ch 11 (Blood Vessels), pp. 485–526 · Arif 15e (2025) Vol-2, pp.21–44

1 · Vascular Structure & Function

Describe the structure and function of blood vessels. What are the two mechanisms of vascular disease?

Vessel wall (all vessels except capillaries) — three layers, outside → in: a supportive adventitia (external layer), a smooth-muscle media, and an endothelium-lined intima separated from the media by the internal elastic lamina. The adventitia is separated from the media by the external elastic lamina in many arteries.

Capillaries: about the diameter of a red cell (7–8 μm), endothelial lining but no media; variable numbers of pericytes (cells that resemble smooth muscle) lie just deep to the endothelium.

Three types of arteries (by size/structure):

TypeExamples
1. Large / elasticAorta + its major branches (innominate, subclavian, common carotid, iliac) and pulmonary arteries
2. Medium / muscularSmaller aortic branches (e.g., coronary and renal arteries)
3. Small arteries (<2 mm) & arterioles (20–100 μm)Within tissues and organs

Lymphatics: thin-walled channels lined by specialized endothelium; return interstitial tissue fluid and inflammatory cells to the bloodstream.

Two principal mechanisms of vascular disease: (1) Narrowing (stenosis) or complete obstruction of the lumen — progressive (atherosclerosis) or precipitous (thrombosis/embolism); (2) Weakening of vessel walls → dilation or rupture.

Robbins 10e pp.485, 518

2 · Congenital Anomalies of Blood Vessels

🔴 What are the congenital anomalies of blood vessels? Describe any one of them.

Four congenital anomalies (VIVA favourite):

  1. Developmental / berry aneurysms — occur in cerebral vessels; when ruptured → fatal intracerebral haemorrhage.
  2. Arteriovenous (AV) fistulas — direct connections (usually small) between arteries and veins that bypass capillaries.
  3. Fibromuscular dysplasia — focal irregular thickening in medium and large muscular arteries (renal, carotid, splanchnic, vertebral) → "string-of-beads" on angiography.
  4. Anomalous coronary artery origin — both coronary arteries arise over the same coronary cusp of the aortic valve.

Arteriovenous fistula — causes (etiology)

  • Developmental defects
  • Rupture of an arterial aneurysm into the adjacent vein
  • Penetrating injuries that pierce arteries and veins
  • Inflammatory necrosis of adjacent vessels
  • Surgically generated AV fistulas (for haemodialysis)

Clinical importance

  • Rupture → intracerebral haemorrhage.
  • Large/multiple AV fistulas shunt blood from arterial to venous circulation → heart pumps extra volume → high-output cardiac failure.

Robbins 10e p.487

🔴 Define malignant hypertension. What are the complications of hypertension?

Malignant (accelerated) hypertension: a clinical syndrome with severe pressure elevation (systolic >200 mmHg, diastolic >120 mmHg), renal failure, and retinal haemorrhages and exudates, with or without papilloedema (optic-nerve swelling reflecting ↑intracranial pressure). May arise in previously normotensive persons, but more often is superimposed on pre-existing "benign" hypertension.

Complications of hypertension:

  • ↑Risk of atherosclerosis
  • Cardiac hypertrophy & heart failure (hypertensive heart disease)
  • Myocardial infarction (heart attack)
  • Multi-infarct dementia
  • Aortic dissection
  • Renal failure
  • Cerebral haemorrhage (stroke)
  • Hypertensive retinopathy
  • Hypertensive encephalopathy

Robbins 10e p.490

4 · Arteriosclerosis (Hardening of the Arteries)

🔴 Define and classify arteriosclerosis. Distinguish it from atherosclerosis.

Arteriosclerosis (literally "hardening of the arteries") is a generic term for arterial wall thickening and loss of elasticity.

Four patterns (classification)

TypeAffectsKey features
1. ArteriolosclerosisSmall arteries & arteriolesMay cause downstream ischaemic injury. Two variants: Hyaline (hyaline thickening of arteriolar walls) and Hyperplastic (concentric, laminated, "onion-skin" thickening)
2. Mönckeberg medial calcific sclerosisMuscular arteries (adults >50)Calcification of medial walls, starting along the internal elastic membrane. Does NOT narrow the lumen.
3. Fibromuscular intimal hyperplasiaMuscular arteries > arteriolesA healing response to inflammation (healed arteritis, transplant arteriopathy) or mechanical injury (stents, balloon angioplasty)
4. AtherosclerosisIntima of large/medium arteriesMost frequent & clinically important — intimal fibrous plaques (atheromas), esp. coronary, cerebral, lower-extremity, renal & mesenteric arteries

Difference (arteriosclerosis vs atherosclerosis): arteriosclerosis = generic wall thickening + loss of elasticity; atherosclerosis = the most frequent/important variety, with intimal lesions (atheromas) that protrude into and obstruct the lumen and weaken the underlying tunica media.

🎯 Arteriosclerosis = "AAA-M": Arteriolosclerosis · Atherosclerosis · Mönckeberg · (Fibromuscular hyperplasia). Hyaline = Harmless(ish) HTN; Onion-skin = Ominous (malignant HTN).

Robbins 10e p.493; Harsh Mohan 7e p.371; BRS Pathology

5 · ATHEROSCLEROSIS — THE KEY CHAPTER

🔴 Define atherosclerosis and atheroma. Give the common sites.

Atherosclerosis: a vascular-disease pattern with intimal lesions called atheromas (atheromatous/atherosclerotic plaques) that protrude into and obstruct the vascular lumen and weaken the underlying tunica media. (Also: thickening & hardening of large/medium muscular arteries, primarily involving the intima, characterised by fibrofatty plaques.)

Atheroma / atherosclerotic plaque: a fully developed atherosclerotic lesion — also called fibrous plaque, fibrofatty plaque, or atheroma.

Common sites (descending order of involvement): (1) Lower abdominal aorta & iliac arteries → (2) Coronary arteries → (3) Popliteal arteries → (4) Internal carotid arteries → (5) Vessels of the circle of Willis.

Robbins 10e pp.493, 501; Harsh Mohan 7e pp.373, 378; BRS Pathology

🔴 Give the morphology (gross + microscopic) of an atheromatous plaque.

Key processes: intimal thickening + lipid accumulation, which together form plaques.

A) Macroscopic

  • Colour: yellow-tan, raised above the vessel wall; superimposed thrombus over ulcerated plaques → red-brown.
  • Size: vary; can coalesce into larger masses.
  • Shape: patchy, rarely circumferential → typically involve only a portion of the wall → eccentric on cross-section.
  • Cut section: central (necrotic) core covered by a subendothelial fibrous cap:
    • Superficial fibrous cap: smooth muscle cells + relatively dense collagen.
    • Necrotic core: lipid (cholesterol & cholesterol esters), debris of dead cells, foam cells, fibrin, thrombus, other plasma proteins.
  • Periphery: neovascularisation (proliferating small vessels).

B) Microscopic — four principal components

  1. Cells: variable SMC (smooth muscle cells), macrophages, T lymphocytes
  2. Extracellular matrix (ECM): collagen, elastic fibres, proteoglycans
  3. Intracellular & extracellular lipids: mostly cholesterol & cholesterol ester
  4. Calcifications (in later-stage plaques)

Most atheromas have abundant lipid; some ("fibrous plaques") are almost exclusively SMC + fibrous tissue and may be heavily calcified.

Complications of an atheromatous plaque: (1) Mechanical obstruction of blood flow; (2) Rupture → catastrophic obstructive vascular thrombosis; (3) Plaques increase the diffusion distance from lumen to media → ischaemic injury and wall weakening → aneurysm formation.

Robbins 10e pp.499–502, 494; BRS Pathology; Harsh Mohan 7e p.378

🔴 What is a foam cell? What is a fatty streak and its significance?

Foam cell: lipid-laden macrophages and lipid-laden smooth muscle cells (the cell with a foamy cytoplasm full of ingested lipid). Monocyte/macrophage and SMC derived.

Fatty streak: lesions lying under the endothelium composed of closely-packed foam cells + lipid-containing elongated smooth muscle cells + a few lymphoid cells, with small amounts of extracellular lipid, collagen, proteoglycans. Begin as small flat yellow macules → coalesce into elongated streaks (≥1 cm). Especially prominent in the aorta & major arteries, more on the posterior than anterior wall.

Significance: not raised, cause no significant flow disturbance, harmless by themselves — but are precursor lesions of atheromatous plaques.

Robbins 10e pp.499, 501; BRS Pathology; Harsh Mohan 7e pp.377–378

Detail on important risk factors

  • Genetics: family history = most important independent risk factor. Mendelian disorders (familial hypercholesterolaemia) account for a small %.
  • Age: age-related; plaques manifest in middle age/later (40–60 yr).
  • Gender: more common in men all ages, but ↑ in postmenopausal women.
  • Hyperlipidaemia: hypercholesterolaemia is sufficient alone to initiate lesions; atherosclerosis ∝ ↑LDL ("bad cholesterol") and ↓HDL ("good cholesterol").
  • Hypertension: prematurely accelerates atherosclerosis; ↑risk of IHD (ischaemic heart disease) by ~60%.
  • Diabetes: induces hypercholesterolaemia, ↑atherosclerosis markedly; peripheral vascular occlusive disease → gangrene common; MI twice as high in diabetics.
  • Metabolic syndrome: insulin resistance + HTN + dyslipidaemia (↑LDL, ↓HDL) + hypercoagulability + proinflammatory state.

Robbins 10e pp.494, 497; BRS Pathology; Harsh Mohan 7e p.373

🔴 State the etio-pathogenesis of atherosclerosis (how an atheroma forms).

Sequence of atherogenesis (Robbins)

  1. Endothelial injury & dysfunction → ↑vascular permeability, leukocyte adhesion, thrombosis.
  2. Accumulation of lipoproteins (mainly LDL and its oxidised forms) in the vessel wall.
  3. Monocyte adhesion → migration into intima → transformation into macrophages and foam cells.
  4. Platelet adhesion.
  5. Factor release from activated platelets, macrophages, and vascular-wall cells → SMC recruitment (from media or circulating precursors).
  6. SMC proliferation + ECM production + T-cell recruitment.
  7. Lipid accumulation — extracellular and within cells.
  8. Calcification of ECM & necrotic debris (late).

Robbins 10e p.497

Describe the role of lipids in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis.

General: hyperlipidaemia may initiate endothelial injury, promote foam-cell formation, act as a monocyte chemotactic factor, inhibit macrophage motility, or injure SMCs.

Mechanisms

  • A. Impaired endothelial-cell function: chronic hypercholesterolaemia ↑local reactive oxygen species → membrane/mitochondrial damage; free radicals accelerate nitric oxide (NO) decay, dampening vasodilation.
  • B. Modified (oxidised) LDL:
    • Lipoproteins accumulate in the intima; aggregate or become oxidised by free radicals from inflammatory cells.
    • Oxidised LDL is ingested by macrophages/SMCs but cannot be fully degraded → chronic ingestion → foam cells; early lesions with lipid-filled macrophages = fatty streaks.
    • Oxidised LDL is toxic to endothelial cells, SMCs, macrophages → endothelial dysfunction.
    • Oxidised LDL stimulates release of growth factors, cytokines, chemokines → vicious inflammatory cycle of monocyte recruitment/activation.

Robbins 10e p.498; BRS Pathology

🔴 Mention the complications / effects of atherosclerosis in different organs.

Clinically important changes (plaque complications)

  1. Rupture/ulceration/erosion → exposes thrombogenic substances → thrombosis (partial or complete occlusion).
  2. Ischaemia: occlusion → acute infarction (myocardial or cerebral).
  3. Haemorrhage into a plaque.
  4. Atheroembolism: plaque rupture discharges debris → microemboli.
  5. Aneurysm formation: pressure/ischaemic atrophy of media → weakness/rupture.
  6. Calcification of atheromas.
  7. Infarction in organs: Heart → MI; Brain → stroke; Aorta → aortic aneurysm; Small intestine → ischaemic bowel disease/infarction; Peripheral vessels → peripheral vascular disease (gangrene).
  8. Consequences of atherosclerotic stenosis (gradual occlusion): mesenteric occlusion & bowel ischaemia; sudden cardiac death; chronic IHD; ischaemic encephalopathy; intermittent claudication.

Lower-extremity large vessels (e.g., popliteal)Peripheral vascular occlusive disease → claudication → gangrene

Plaque rupture/fissuring · erosion/ulceration · haemorrhage · thrombus formation · embolisation.

Robbins 10e pp.502, 494(bottom)

Write about the prevention of atherosclerosis.

1. Primary prevention

  • Cessation of cigarette smoking
  • Control of hypertension
  • Weight reduction
  • Increase exercise
  • Moderation of alcohol consumption
  • Lower total & LDL cholesterol while raising HDL

Prevent recurrence of events (e.g., MI) in patients with symptomatic disease.

6 · Vascular Tumours

🔴 Classify vascular tumours. Write short notes on glomus tumour, haemangioma, lymphangioma, angiosarcoma, spider telangiectasia.

Classification (all endothelial in origin; markers CD31, CD34, von-Willebrand factor/vWF):

CategoryExamples
BenignHaemangioma (capillary, cavernous, pyogenic granuloma, juvenile/"strawberry"), lymphangioma (simple/cavernous=cystic hygroma), glomus tumour, vascular ectasias (naevus flammeus, spider telangiectasia, Osler-Weber-Rendu), reactive proliferations (bacillary angiomatosis)
Intermediate-gradeKaposi sarcoma, haemangioendothelioma
MalignantAngiosarcoma, haemangiopericytoma

Glomus tumour (glomangioma) — short note

Definition: benign but exquisitely painful tumours arising from modified smooth muscle cells of the glomus body (a specialised AV structure involved in thermoregulation).

Location: distal digits, especially under the fingernails. Treatment: excision is curative.

Morphology: Gross: usually <1 cm, round, slightly elevated, red-blue, firm nodules (initially resemble a minute focus of haemorrhage). Microscopy: aggregates/nests/masses of specialised glomus cells with branching vascular channels in connective-tissue stroma; cells small, uniform, round/cuboidal, scant cytoplasm, ultrastructure like SMC.

Haemangioma — definition + capillary haemangioma morphology

Haemangioma: benign neoplasm of blood vessels — very common, composed of blood-filled vessels. Age: infancy & childhood (7% of all benign tumours of this age). Most common site: skin of face & mucosal surfaces.

Types: capillary haemangioma, cavernous haemangioma, pyogenic granuloma (haemangioma of granulation-tissue type), juvenile/"strawberry" haemangioma.

Capillary haemangioma morphology: Gross — bright red-to-blue, few mm to several cm, at level with or slightly elevated above skin, intact overlying epithelium. Microscopy — unencapsulated lobules of capillary-sized, thin-walled, blood-filled vessels lined by flattened endothelium surrounded by a layer of pericytes; vessels separated by scant connective-tissue stroma; lumens may be thrombosed/organised; haemosiderin pigment from vessel rupture; focal scarring.

Cavernous haemangioma histology: thin-walled cavernous vascular spaces (filled partly/fully with blood) lined by flattened endothelium, separated by scanty stroma.

Pyogenic granuloma histology: proliferating capillaries like capillary haemangioma, but separated by abundant oedema + inflammatory infiltrate → resembles inflammatory granulation tissue.

Lymphangioma

The benign lymphatic counterpart of haemangioma. Two types: (1) simple (capillary) lymphangioma, (2) cavernous lymphangioma (cystic hygroma).

Angiosarcoma

The malignant endothelial neoplasm of the blood vessel.

Spider telangiectasia

A non-neoplastic vascular malformation resembling a spider: radial, often pulsatile lesions of dilated subcutaneous arteries/arterioles (spider legs) about a central core (spider body) that blanches with pressure. Location: face, neck, upper chest; associated with hyperoestrogenic states (pregnancy, cirrhosis).

Robbins 10e pp.519–522; Harsh Mohan 7e pp.392–393

7 · Aneurysms & Dissections

🔴 Define and classify aneurysm. Distinguish true vs false aneurysm.

Aneurysm: a localised abnormal dilation of a blood vessel or the heart, congenital or acquired. In the aorta → aortic aneurysm.

TypeDefinition
True aneurysmInvolves all layers (intima, media, adventitia) of an intact-but-attenuated wall. Atherosclerotic, congenital vascular, and ventricular aneurysms after transmural MI are this type.
False aneurysm (pseudoaneurysm)A defect in the wall → an extravascular haematoma that freely communicates with the intravascular space ("pulsating haematoma"). e.g., contained ventricular rupture, leak at a vascular-graft anastomosis.
Dissecting aneurysm (dissection)Blood enters through an intimal tear and splits (dissects) the medial lamellae, creating a blood-filled channel within the wall.

Classification by shape/size

  • Saccular: spherical outpouching involving only a portion of the wall (e.g., berry aneurysm).
  • Fusiform: diffuse, circumferential dilation of a long vascular segment (typical AAA — abdominal aortic aneurysm).

Causes of aneurysm

  1. Atherosclerosis
  2. Hypertension
  3. Advanced age
  4. Smoking
  5. Trauma
  6. Vasculitis
  7. Congenital defects (fibromuscular dysplasia, berry aneurysms of the circle of Willis)
  8. Infections (mycotic aneurysms)

Arterial dissection

Blood enters a defect in the arterial wall and tunnels through medial or medial-adventitial planes. Dissections are often (not always) aneurysmal. All of the above can rupture, often catastrophically.

Robbins 10e pp.504–506

🔴 What is aortic dissection? Give its risk factors and clinical features.

Aortic dissection: blood separates the laminar planes of the media → a blood-filled channel within the aortic wall. Usually associated with aortic dilation; disastrous if it ruptures through the adventitia into adjacent spaces.

Two groups of patients

  1. Men aged 40–60 with antecedent hypertension (>90% of cases).
  2. Younger patients with syndromic aortic disease (e.g., Marfan syndrome).

Hypertension is the major risk factor for aortic dissection.

Clinical features

Sudden onset of excruciating pain, usually starting in the anterior chest, radiating to the back between the scapulae, moving downward as the dissection progresses — can be confused with MI. Most common cause of death = rupture → bleeding into pericardial, pleural, or peritoneal cavities.

Robbins 10e p.507

8 · Vasculitis (Vessel-Wall Inflammation)

🔴 Define vasculitis. Give its pathogenesis and classify non-infectious vasculitis.

Vasculitis: a general term for vessel-wall inflammation, with protean manifestations depending on the vascular bed affected (CNS vs heart vs small bowel).

Clinical manifestations: constitutional signs of systemic inflammation — fever, myalgias, arthralgias, malaise. Affected vessels: any type (large, medium, small); most vasculitides affect small vessels.

Pathogenesis — two mechanisms

  1. Infectious vasculitis: direct invasion of vascular walls by infectious pathogens.
  2. Non-infectious (immune-mediated):
    • Immune-complex deposition
    • Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA)
    • Anti-endothelial-cell (anti-EC) antibodies
    • Autoreactive T cells

🔴 Short notes on each major vasculitis: Giant cell arteritis, Takayasu, PAN, Kawasaki, GPA (Wegener), Churg-Strauss, Buerger.

Giant cell (temporal) arteritis

Chronic, classically granulomatous inflammation of large-to-small arteries. Most common vasculitis among the elderly; rare before 50. Affects arteries of the head; vertebral & ophthalmic arteries and aorta (giant cell aortitis) can be involved. Ophthalmic artery involvement → sudden, permanent blindness.

Takayasu arteritis

A granulomatous vasculitis of medium & large arteries, with ocular disturbances and marked weakening of upper-extremity pulses → "pulseless disease". Classically involves the aortic arch; in ⅓ affects the rest of the aorta & branches, pulmonary artery in half; coronary & renal arteries may be affected. Nearly identical to giant cell aortitis — distinguished by age: >50 = giant cell, <50 = Takayasu.

Polyarteritis nodosa (PAN)

A systemic vasculitis of small- or medium-sized muscular arteries, typically affecting renal & visceral vessels but sparing the pulmonary circulation. Involves kidneys, heart, liver, GI tract (descending order). Unknown cause in most. Mostly young adults. Clinical: rapidly accelerating HTN (renal artery involvement), abdominal pain & bloody stools (GI vascular lesions), diffuse myalgias, peripheral neuritis (esp. motor). Renal involvement = major cause of mortality. Untreated → fatal; immunosuppression → remission/cure in 90%.

Kawasaki disease

Acute, febrile, usually self-limited illness of infancy/childhood with large-to-medium arteritis (80% <4 yr). Triggered in susceptible persons by infections (mostly viral). Clinical significance = coronary artery involvement → coronary arteritis → aneurysms that rupture or thrombose → myocardial infarction.

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA, formerly Wegener granulomatosis)

A necrotising vasculitis with the triad:

  1. Acute necrotising granulomas of upper (ear, nose, sinuses, throat) or lower (lung) respiratory tract, or both
  2. Necrotising/granulomatous vasculitis of small-to-medium vessels (most prominent in lungs & upper airways)
  3. Focal necrotising, often crescentic, glomerulonephritis

Churg-Strauss syndrome (allergic granulomatosis and angiitis)

A small-vessel necrotising vasculitis classically associated with asthma, allergic rhinitis, lung infiltrates, peripheral eosinophilia, extravascular necrotising granulomas, and striking eosinophilic infiltration of vessels/tissues. Major associations: cutaneous involvement (palpable purpura), GI bleeding, renal disease (focal/segmental glomerulosclerosis). Cardiac involvement in 60% (eosinophilic cardiomyopathy) — a major cause of morbidity & death.

Thromboangiitis obliterans (Buerger disease)

Segmental, thrombosing, acute & chronic inflammation of medium & small arteries, esp. tibial and radial → vascular insufficiency of extremities. Almost exclusively in heavy cigarette smokers, usually before age 35. Clinical features: Raynaud phenomenon, instep foot pain on exercise (instep claudication), superficial nodular phlebitis, severe pain at rest (neural), chronic extremity ulcerations → frank gangrene.

Robbins 10e pp.511–515

9 · Raynaud Phenomenon

What is Raynaud phenomenon? Describe its types.

Raynaud phenomenon: exaggerated vasoconstriction of arteries & arterioles in response to cold or emotion. Most commonly affects the extremities (fingers, toes) and occasionally nose, earlobes, lips.

The involved digits classically show "red, white, and blue" colour changes from most proximal to most distal, correlating with proximal vasodilation, central vasoconstriction, and distal cyanosis.

Types

  • Primary Raynaud phenomenon (formerly Raynaud disease): caused by cold or emotional stress.
  • Secondary Raynaud phenomenon: caused by other diseases — SLE, scleroderma, Buerger disease, or atherosclerosis.

Robbins 10e p.516

10 · Varicose Veins

What are varicose veins? Why are the legs, oesophagus and anal canal special sites?

Varicose veins: abnormally dilated, tortuous veins produced by prolonged, increased intraluminal pressure with vessel dilation and incompetence of the venous valves.

The superficial veins of the upper & lower leg are commonly involved because venous pressures can be markedly elevated (up to 10× normal) by prolonged dependent posture. Two other special sites: oesophageal varices & haemorrhoids.

Robbins 10e p.517
